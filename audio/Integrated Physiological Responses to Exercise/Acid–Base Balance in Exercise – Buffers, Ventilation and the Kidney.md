## Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney

Hard exercise floods the blood with acid.
Yet the body works properly only when its acidity stays within a narrow band.
In this note, we'll see how the body holds that band, and what happens to it in an all-out effort.

## 1. What pH is and why exercise threatens it

Acid-base balance is the control of how acidic the body's fluids are.
Acidity comes from hydrogen ions, the tiny charged particles that acids release.
It is measured on the p H scale, where a lower number means more hydrogen ions.
Each unit on the scale is a tenfold change.
Body fluids cover a range of p H values.
At one end, the stomach is at about p H one point zero.
Blood sits at the other end, between seven point three five and seven point four five.
That makes blood slightly alkaline, meaning a little less acidic than pure water, which sits at p H seven point zero.
A rise in hydrogen ions, which lowers the p H, is called acidosis.
A fall in hydrogen ions, which raises the p H, is called alkalosis.
Any chemical or physiological mechanism that minimises changes in hydrogen ion concentration is called buffering.
Three mechanisms do this.
They are chemical buffers, breathing and the kidneys.
Breathing here is also called pulmonary ventilation.
Hard exercise strains this system, because working muscle relies increasingly on breaking down glucose without oxygen, a process called anaerobic glycolysis.
This releases lactate together with hydrogen ions, so p H gets harder to control as intensity rises.

## 2. How a chemical buffer works

Let's look at the first of these mechanisms, the chemical buffer.
A chemical buffer is a pair of molecules that keeps acidity steady.
One partner is a weak acid, an acid that lets go of only a few of its hydrogen ions and keeps most of them.
The other partner is called the conjugate base, which is what the weak acid becomes after it has let go of a hydrogen ion.
The two keep turning into each other.
The acid releases hydrogen ions, and the base takes them back.
In the equation for this two-way balance, the weak acid sits on the left, and a hydrogen ion plus the conjugate base sit on the right.
The reaction runs in both directions.
When there are too many hydrogen ions, the reaction runs to the left, back towards the weak acid.
The base takes up the extra hydrogen ions and becomes the weak acid again, which removes them from the solution.
When there are too few hydrogen ions, the reaction runs to the right, and the weak acid releases them.
A buffer therefore works like a sponge, soaking up hydrogen ions or squeezing them out as needed.
Buffers are already present in the body fluids, which lets them act at once.
This makes them the rapid first line of defence.
But a sponge only hides the acid, and does not remove it from the body.
The blood uses three chemical buffers.
These are bicarbonate, phosphate and proteins.
One such protein is haemoglobin, the oxygen carrier in red blood cells.

## 3. The bicarbonate buffer and how breathing removes acid

The bicarbonate buffer is special, since one of its products is a gas, which the lungs can get rid of.
Its weak acid is carbonic acid, and its base is bicarbonate, supplied by sodium bicarbonate.
In acidosis, when the blood holds too much acid, the extra hydrogen ions join bicarbonate to make carbonic acid.
Carbonic acid then splits into carbon dioxide and water.
Put together, the whole chain runs from a hydrogen ion plus bicarbonate, through carbonic acid, to carbon dioxide plus water.
The hydrogen ion is now locked inside a water molecule.
The lungs breathe out the carbon dioxide, and in this way the acid leaves the body.
Breathing harder pulls this reaction along, an effect called the ventilatory buffer.
A rise in hydrogen ions or carbon dioxide in the blood excites chemoreceptors, the chemical sensors that drive breathing.
The respiratory centre in the brainstem responds by raising alveolar ventilation.
Alveolar ventilation is the fresh air that reaches the air sacs, or alveoli, each minute.
This lowers the carbon dioxide first in the air sacs and next in the blood.
With less carbon dioxide at the end of the reaction, more hydrogen ions recombine with bicarbonate, and the hydrogen ion concentration falls.
The ventilatory buffer is the second line of defence.
It is slower than a chemical buffer, because breathing must change first, but it removes acid instead of hiding it.
This buffer relies on the same reaction that red blood cells use to carry carbon dioxide.
The same chemistry explains the ventilatory threshold, the point where breathing starts to rise faster than oxygen uptake.
Buffering lactate's hydrogen ions with bicarbonate releases extra carbon dioxide, which breathing must clear.
In alkalosis, the fall in hydrogen ions weakens the drive to breathe, and as a result carbon dioxide is retained.
In that case, the bicarbonate reaction runs the other way.
Carbon dioxide combines with water to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions and lowers the p H again.

## 4. Phosphate and protein buffers, and why haemoglobin leads

Phosphate and proteins are the other two chemical buffers.
The phosphate buffer, made of phosphoric acid with sodium phosphate, works like the bicarbonate buffer, except that it has no gas end.
Proteins are chains of building blocks called amino acids, and many of these carry charged groups.
Negatively charged carboxyl groups attract positive hydrogen ions.
Positively charged amino groups attract negative hydroxyl ions, the particles that make a solution alkaline.
Together, proteins provide two-thirds of the buffering power of the blood, and most of the buffering inside cells.
Haemoglobin is a major acceptor of hydrogen ions.
It is about six times more potent as a buffer than the proteins of the plasma, the liquid part of the blood.
Two things make it so effective.
First, there is far more haemoglobin in blood than any other protein.
Second, it binds hydrogen ions more readily once it has released its oxygen in working muscle, exactly where acid is made.

## 5. The kidney, the slow final defence

The kidneys are the body's last and slowest defence against acid.
They are needed since the buffers run down.
Every hydrogen ion that bicarbonate catches uses up one bicarbonate.
The work is done in the renal tubules, tiny tubes that turn fluid filtered from the blood into urine.
The tubules secrete hydrogen ions into the urine.
They also release ammonia, which picks up more hydrogen ions and carries them out as well.
At the same time, they reabsorb chloride and alkaline substances such as bicarbonate, returning them to the blood.
This restores the blood's buffering power.
In this way, unlike a chemical buffer, the renal buffer removes acid from the body and restores the bicarbonate that was used up.
Together, chemical buffers, breathing and the kidneys defend p H over different timescales.
Chemical buffers act over seconds, breathing over minutes, and the kidneys over hours to days.

## 6. Blood pH and lactate in all-out exercise

The harder the exercise, the more hydrogen ions pour in from both carbon dioxide and lactate.
The turning point is the lactate threshold, the intensity at which lactate starts to build up in the blood.
Above it, hydrogen ions are produced faster than they can be cleared, so blood p H starts to fall.
Repeated, brief all-out efforts can raise blood lactate to thirty millimoles per litre or more.
That is about thirty times the resting level of about one millimole per litre, and acid-base regulation becomes difficult.
The body can tolerate such a pronounced disturbance for a short time during maximal exercise.
Even so, a plasma p H of seven point zero or below can cause nausea, headache and dizziness.
In blood samples taken at rest and during short bouts of exercise up to maximum, p H falls evenly with every rise in lactate.
Blood p H drops from about seven point four at rest to about six point eight at a lactate level of just over thirty millimoles per litre.
As exercise intensity climbs, lactate rises slowly up to seventy-five per cent of maximum, and then steeply.
Meanwhile, p H falls steadily, to about six point eight.
The two move like the ends of a seesaw.
The reason is that the hydrogen ions released with lactate pull the p H down.
Buffering is harder still at altitude, where the blood carries less bicarbonate.

## Summary

To recap, blood p H is held between seven point three five and seven point four five.
A rise in hydrogen ions is acidosis, while a fall is alkalosis.
A chemical buffer is a weak acid paired with its conjugate base.
It soaks up or releases hydrogen ions within seconds, though it only hides the acid.
Bicarbonate, phosphate and proteins are the blood's chemical buffers.
Proteins, led by haemoglobin, provide two-thirds of the blood's buffering power.
In the ventilatory buffer, hydrogen ions join bicarbonate to make water and carbon dioxide, which the lungs breathe out within minutes.
Over hours to days, the kidneys secrete hydrogen ions and reabsorb bicarbonate, removing acid and restoring the bicarbonate used up.
Repeated all-out bouts can push blood lactate to thirty millimoles per litre or more.
A plasma p H of seven point zero or below can cause nausea, headache and dizziness.
And that's how buffers, breathing and the kidneys defend the blood's acidity, from seconds to days, when hard exercise floods it with acid.
