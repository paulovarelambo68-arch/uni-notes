## Adaptive Immunity – Principles and Clonal Selection

Adaptive immunity relies on two kinds of lymphocyte, the B cells and the T cells.
B cells mature in the bone marrow and make antibodies, which is known as humoral immunity.
T cells, on the other hand, mature in the thymus and provide cell-mediated immunity.
This branch follows three Rs, namely Recognise, Respond and Remember.
Recognition rests on clonal selection, in which the antigen selects the rare pre-existing lymphocytes whose receptor fits.
Those lymphocytes divide into a clone of effector and memory cells, and the process is confined to T and B cells.
We'll meet the two cell types first, then work through the three Rs, and finish by following clonal selection step by step.

## 1. The adaptive branch and its two cell types

To begin, consider what sets this branch apart.
Adaptive immunity, or acquired immunity, is the branch we are not born with.
Instead, it develops, or adapts, throughout a lifetime.
It is slower to respond than the innate system, and it recognises antigens with high specificity.
Antigens are the molecules that its receptors bind.
It also improves with each contact, and it has a memory function.
The cells of this branch are the lymphocytes, which are white blood cells of two kinds.
The two kinds mature in different places, and they defend the body in different ways.
To mature, in this sense, means to become a fully working cell.
These two ways of defending the body are the two arms of adaptive immunity.
The first arm belongs to the B cells, which make antibodies.
Antibodies are proteins that bind one particular antigen, and they work dissolved in the body fluids.
Those fluids were once called humours, hence the name humoral, or antibody-mediated, immunity.
In cell-mediated immunity, by contrast, the T cells themselves act directly on other cells.

## 2. The three Rs

Whichever cell is involved, adaptive immunity follows the same three Rs.
The first R is Recognise.
Each lymphocyte must recognise its antigen, since every cell carries a specific receptor for a particular antigen.
Recognition is then followed by clonal selection.
The second R is Respond.
In responding, lymphocytes secrete cytokines, which are small signalling proteins, and produce antibodies, while T cells also kill cells directly.
The third R is Remember, which the system achieves through long-lived memory cells.
Together, the three Rs form the scaffold of Immunology Two, and the first of them, Recognise, is what we explain next.

## 3. Clonal selection

Now we come to clonal selection, on which recognition rests.
It was proposed by Burnet in nineteen fifty-seven.
Each lymphocyte has receptors that bind a single antigen, and this defines its specificity.
Because lymphocytes differ in specificity, the lymphocyte repertoire is extremely diverse.
The repertoire simply means the full set of different lymphocytes in the body.
As a result, there are very few cells of any one specificity, and each of them is highly specific.
What happens when one of these rare cells finds its antigen?
It binds the antigen and can become activated, in other words, switched on.
The activated cell divides rapidly to make many copies, a clone of identical cells that share its receptor.
This clonal expansion provides the large number of antigen-specific lymphocytes needed to keep pace with a multiplying microbe.
The clone contains effector cells, which do the job now, and memory cells, which stay for the future.
It's worth asking why the name stresses selection rather than instruction.
The antigen does not instruct a cell to make a matching receptor.
Rather, the receptors exist before the antigen is ever met.
Out of millions of pre-existing lymphocytes, the antigen selects the few whose receptor fits, so only those expand.
Since the process depends on these single-antigen receptors, clonal selection occurs for both T and B cells, the only cells that carry them.
Innate cells such as monocytes, neutrophils and basophils do not undergo it.

## 4. Reading the figure

Let's follow the whole sequence through for B cells.
The primary response is the first meeting with an antigen.
Imagine three B cells, each carrying receptors of a different specificity.
The antigen fits the receptors of only one of them, so only that cell binds it and is selected.
It goes on to form clones of activated B cells, which divide again.
Most of these become plasma cells, which are effector cells that secrete antibody.
That antibody fits the same antigen as the selected cell's receptor.
One of the activated cells, though, becomes a memory B cell.
Next comes the secondary response, when the antigen returns and binds this memory cell.
This memory cell descends from the cell that was selected in the first place.
In a single step, it yields more plasma cells and more memory cells, giving a faster and larger response.
We've followed B cells here, but the same logic applies to T cells.

## Summary

To sum up, adaptive, or acquired, immunity is slow but highly specific, and it improves with each contact and remembers.
B cells mature in the bone marrow and make antibodies, giving humoral immunity.
T cells mature in the thymus and act directly on other cells, which is cell-mediated immunity.
Every response follows the three Rs.
Lymphocytes recognise through specific receptors, respond with cytokines, antibodies and killing by T cells, and remember through memory cells.
In clonal selection, proposed by Burnet in nineteen fifty-seven, the antigen selects the rare pre-existing cells whose receptor fits, drawing them from an extremely diverse repertoire.
Those cells divide rapidly into a clone of effector and memory cells.
This happens in T and B cells and never in innate cells such as monocytes, neutrophils and basophils.
That completes the principles of adaptive immunity and the story of clonal selection.
