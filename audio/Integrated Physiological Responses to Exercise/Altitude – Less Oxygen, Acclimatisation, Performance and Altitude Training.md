## Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training

Climb high enough and every breath brings in less oxygen, although the air itself has not changed.
In this note, we'll follow the body through that challenge in time, from arrival, through the first days and the first weeks, to exercising and training up there.

## 1. Less oxygen in every breath

We begin with the basic problem.
At altitude, the problem is thin air, not different air.
Altitude is graded by height.
Moderate altitude is one thousand five hundred to three thousand metres.
High altitude is three thousand to five thousand five hundred metres, and extreme altitude is anything above five thousand five hundred metres.
The upper limit of permanent human habitation is five thousand eight hundred and twenty metres.
Air at any height is still twenty point nine per cent oxygen.
What falls is barometric pressure, the weight of the air above you.
Oxygen's partial pressure, its share of that total, falls with it.
The rule is that the oxygen partial pressure, in millimetres of mercury, equals zero point two zero nine three times the barometric pressure.
Here, zero point two zero nine three is oxygen's fraction of the air.
The numbers make the loss concrete.
At sea level, a pressure of seven hundred and sixty millimetres of mercury gives one hundred and fifty-nine millimetres of mercury of oxygen.
At Pikes Peak in Colorado, at four thousand three hundred metres, four hundred and fifty millimetres of mercury gives about ninety-four.
On Everest, at eight thousand eight hundred and fifty metres, two hundred and thirty-five millimetres of mercury gives about forty-nine.
Climbing in steps shows the same steady decline.
At one thousand five hundred metres, the air pressure is six hundred and thirty-four millimetres of mercury, and the oxygen pressure is one hundred and thirty-three.
At three thousand metres, the air pressure is five hundred and twenty-six, and the oxygen pressure is one hundred and ten.
By four thousand metres, the air pressure has fallen to four hundred and sixty-two, and the oxygen pressure to ninety-seven.
At five thousand metres, the air pressure is four hundred and five, and the oxygen pressure is eighty-five.
Higher still, at seven thousand metres, the air pressure is three hundred and eight, and the oxygen pressure is sixty-four.
At nine thousand metres, the air pressure is two hundred and thirty, and the oxygen pressure is forty-eight.
The effects begin low down.
Maximal oxygen uptake, or V O two max, is the most oxygen the body can use in a minute.
It starts to fall above about one thousand five hundred metres in most people.
In elite athletes, it can fall from as low as five hundred and eighty metres.
The body's defence is acclimatisation, the adaptive responses in physiology and metabolism that improve tolerance of altitude hypoxia.
Hypoxia is a shortage of oxygen.

## 2. The pressure staircase sags

Oxygen travels down a staircase of pressures.
The first step is inspired air, and the second is the air in the alveoli, the lung's air sacs.
Then come arterial blood and finally venous blood, once the tissues have taken their share.
This is the oxygen transport cascade.
At altitude, every step starts lower, since the first step is lower.
At sea level, inspired oxygen pressure is about one hundred and fifty millimetres of mercury, whereas at five thousand metres it is only about eighty.
Oxygen pressure falls at every step, at rest and in exercise, both at sea level and at five thousand metres.
The steepest fall comes during exercise at five thousand metres, between the alveolar air and the arterial blood.
That fall is the diffusion limitation of exercise at altitude, in which the blood leaves the lung before it is fully loaded.
The sag matters because of the oxygen dissociation curve.
This S-shaped relationship describes how fully haemoglobin, the oxygen carrier in red cells, is loaded at each oxygen pressure.
At its top the relationship is flat.
For this reason, at about two thousand metres haemoglobin is still about ninety per cent saturated, despite the lower arterial pressure.
At high altitude, the arterial pressure drops onto the steep part of the curve.
There, each further millimetre of mercury lost unloads a lot of oxygen, making saturation collapse.

## 3. The first days: the acute response

Within hours, the body fights the low oxygen with what it already has.
The heart beats faster at rest and in submaximal exercise, meaning exercise below full effort.
More oxygen therefore reaches the tissues each minute, although each litre of blood carries less.
Breathing becomes deeper and faster, which raises alveolar oxygen pressure but also blows off carbon dioxide.
Carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions consequently fall in the blood and in the fluid around the brain.
The usual carbon dioxide signal to breathe fades.
Breathing is now kept going by the hypoxic drive.
The peripheral chemoreceptors, oxygen sensors in the main arteries, detect the low oxygen and keep the brainstem ventilating.
Losing carbon dioxide also makes the blood alkaline, which shifts the dissociation curve to the left.
Haemoglobin then holds its oxygen more tightly and unloads less in the muscles.
Acute mountain sickness can follow.
The circulation tightens too.
Blood pressure rises because vascular resistance rises.
Vascular resistance is how hard it is to push blood through narrowed small arteries.
Catecholamines, the stress hormones adrenaline and noradrenaline, rise.
They increase the resistance and the production of lactate, the by-product of making energy without oxygen.
V O two max falls, which lowers exercise capacity.
Blood, muscle and liver change little at first.
Ventilation keeps rising for about two weeks, driven by hypoxia.
The alkalosis, the rise in blood p H, must be countered.
To counter it, the kidneys begin to excrete bicarbonate, the alkali the blood uses to soak up acid.
As arterial carbon dioxide drops, the central and peripheral chemoreceptors become more sensitive, so ventilation answers any further signal more strongly.
Intense exercise adds the diffusion limitation.
Arterial oxygen pressure falls because the blood leaves the lung capillary before it has caught up with the alveolar air.
Two things cause this.
The driving pressure is lower, which makes loading slower.
And exercise shortens the time each red cell spends in the capillary.
At sea level, blood in the lung capillary reaches its ceiling, the alveolar oxygen pressure, well before the exercise transit time of zero point two five seconds.
At four thousand three hundred metres, the ceiling is under fifty millimetres of mercury, and the blood's oxygen pressure climbs more slowly.
At zero point two five seconds it is still rising, so the blood leaves under-filled, like a bucket filled from a weaker tap in less time.
Behind the rises in pressure and resistance is a stress response that builds over days.
Sympathoadrenal activity, the sympathetic nerves and the adrenal medulla acting together, rises progressively at rest and in exercise.
The sign is a steady rise in the noradrenaline excreted in the urine.
Noradrenaline is the fight-or-flight transmitter, and it also shifts the fuel mix.
In the longer term, its receptors are downregulated.
The tissues make fewer of them, which damps the response.

## 4. The first weeks: acclimatisation

Over the following weeks, the blood, the cells and the acid-base balance all adjust, each at a price.
Acid-base balance is how acid or alkaline the blood is.
Low arterial oxygen makes the kidneys release erythropoietin, or E P O.
This hormone drives the bone marrow to make more red cells, and with them more haemoglobin.
The haematocrit, also called the packed cell volume, is the fraction of blood volume made up of red cells.
It is normally forty-five per cent in men and forty per cent in women.
Men have four point seven to six point zero million red cells per microlitre, and women four point two to five point four million.
At altitude, the haematocrit rises in two stages.
First, within days, the plasma volume, the liquid part of the blood, shrinks, concentrating the cells already there.
Then, over weeks, new red cells are added.
Too high a haematocrit thickens the blood dangerously.
That is the risk when athletes abuse injected E P O or blood transfusions.
In women staying at Pikes Peak, haematocrit and haemoglobin concentration both jump in the first days, driven by the shrinking plasma volume.
Both then climb more slowly for a month while new red cells arrive, and they fall back after the return to sea level.
The cells adapt to take up oxygen more easily.
Capillaries, the smallest blood vessels, remodel and new ones form, shortening the diffusion distance between blood and tissue.
Myoglobin, the muscle's own oxygen store, rises, and mitochondria, the cells' aerobic power stations, increase a little.
The red cells make more two three D P G, a molecule that loosens haemoglobin's grip on oxygen.
The dissociation curve accordingly shifts slightly right, and more oxygen is unloaded.
The heart remodels as well.
The wall between its two pumping chambers, the ventricles, thickens.
The left ventricle shrinks, and the right ventricle grows larger and thicker.
Low oxygen narrows the lung's small arteries, which raises the pressure in the lung's vessels, a condition called pulmonary hypertension.
The right ventricle pumps into those vessels, and a ventricle that pumps against a higher pressure thickens.
The kidneys finish the acid-base repair.
They slowly excrete bicarbonate until the p H, raised by the loss of carbon dioxide, is normal again.
A normal p H lets the brainstem's respiratory centre respond even more strongly to hypoxia, which raises ventilation further.
The price is a loss of alkaline reserve.
With less bicarbonate, the blood has less capacity to buffer, or soak up, the acid of hard exercise.
Lactate behaves oddly over the same weeks.
On arrival, a given submaximal exercise produces more lactate than at sea level.
The reason is that the oxygen-starved muscle leans more on anaerobic glycolysis, the fuel pathway that makes lactate.
After some weeks, the same exercise produces less lactate, yet the tissues are no better supplied with oxygen.
Less oxygen giving less lactate is the lactate paradox.
There are two smaller costs.
First, dry mountain air must be moistened in the airways, which raises water loss.
The loss rises especially with the heavy breathing and sweating of exercise, and dehydration follows.
Second, the lower the arterial saturation, the more sensory and mental function suffer.
Before gathering the adjustments together, recall that cardiac output is the blood the heart pumps each minute.
It equals heart rate times stroke volume, the volume of one beat.
Let's go system by system, first the immediate changes, then the longer-term ones.
In the lungs and acid-base balance, the immediate change is hyperventilation, and body fluids become more alkaline from the loss of carbon dioxide.
In the longer term, hyperventilation continues, the kidneys excrete bicarbonate, and the alkaline reserve falls.
In the cardiovascular system, submaximal heart rate and cardiac output rise at once.
Stroke volume is unchanged or slightly down, as is maximal cardiac output.
Over the longer term, submaximal heart rate stays up, but submaximal cardiac output falls to sea-level values or below.
Stroke volume falls, and maximal cardiac output falls too.
In the blood, there is no immediate change.
Later, plasma volume falls, while haematocrit, haemoglobin concentration and red-cell number rise.
In the tissues themselves, there is also no immediate change.
In the longer term, more muscle capillaries are possible.
There is also more two three D P G, with more mitochondria and more aerobic enzymes.
Body weight and lean mass are lost.

## 5. Time, and mountain sickness

Acclimatisation cannot be hurried.
A slow ascent to five thousand five hundred metres can be made with few symptoms.
Allow two weeks to adjust to altitudes up to two thousand three hundred metres.
Then allow another week for each six hundred and ten metres, up to four thousand six hundred metres.
A rapid ascent brings acute mountain sickness within hours.
Its symptoms are headache, nausea, irritability, weakness and poor appetite.
Vomiting, a fast heart rate and disturbed breathing also occur.
The disturbed breathing includes Cheyne-Stokes breathing, in which bouts of fast breathing alternate with slow or absent breathing.
Acute mountain sickness is common above three thousand metres.
People whose breathing responds weakly to hypoxia are more susceptible, since they do not raise their alveolar oxygen.
A slow ascent and acclimatisation hikes reduce the risk.
Acclimatisation hikes are day trips higher before returning to sleep lower.

## 6. Exercising up there

How much altitude hurts depends on how long the event lasts.
Events of two minutes or less are unaffected, as they run on anaerobic energy, made without oxygen.
Decrements appear from one thousand six hundred metres for events of two to five minutes.
For events of twenty minutes or more, they appear from only six hundred to seven hundred metres.
Measured against sea-level race time, races under two minutes stay just as fast at every altitude.
The longer the race, the earlier and more steeply it slows with height.
A race of two to three hours takes nearly one hundred and thirty per cent of its sea-level time by three thousand metres.
The reason is that the longer the event lasts, the more it depends on oxygen delivery.
Aerobic capacity falls steadily with height, with V O two max dropping about three per cent for every three hundred metres.
Above six thousand metres, the body deteriorates progressively towards death.
The oxygen cost of a given task is the same as at sea level, given that the muscles do the same work.
That cost is now a larger share of a smaller V O two max, and the effort therefore feels harder.
Steady cycling at one hundred watts takes about two litres of oxygen per minute, both at sea level and at four thousand three hundred metres.
V O two max is about four litres per minute at sea level, but under three litres per minute at altitude.
The same two litres per minute is thus fifty per cent of maximum at sea level and seventy per cent at four thousand three hundred metres.
The task is unchanged, but the ceiling has come down.
Acclimatisation lifts endurance a little, even though V O two max does not recover.
Three things explain it.
Ventilation is higher, arterial saturation and the cells' aerobic function are better, and the lactate response is blunted.

## 7. Altitude training

Living at altitude does not improve sea-level performance when V O two max is the yardstick.
The gain in the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity is cancelled by losses.
Muscle is lost, and maximal heart rate and stroke volume fall, which lowers maximal cardiac output.
Three strategies try to keep the gain and avoid the losses.
The first is live high, train high.
It gives the most altitude exposure but limited sea-level gains, owing to the impossibility of hard training in thin air.
The second is live low, train high, which uses high-intensity sessions in thin air, including chambers at about fifteen per cent oxygen.
There is substantial evidence that it helps endurance, sprint and strength athletes.
The third is live high, train low.
It combines the adaptations of simply living at altitude with full-intensity training near sea level, which is why it works best.
Live high, train low is done in two ways.
In the first, athletes live at two thousand two hundred to three thousand five hundred metres, high enough to stimulate red-cell production without acute mountain sickness.
Each day, they drive down to train below one thousand two hundred metres.
In the second, they sleep in a hypoxic tent at about fourteen per cent oxygen, an inspired oxygen pressure of one hundred and six millimetres of mercury.
The adaptation then happens while they sleep.
The results in elite runners were clear.
The method raised V O two max and running endurance, and improved three thousand metre times.
It worked only in the responders, those whose E P O rose at altitude and whose haematocrit rose with it.

## Summary

To recap, the air at altitude is still twenty point nine per cent oxygen, but barometric pressure falls.
Oxygen partial pressure, which equals zero point two zero nine three times barometric pressure, falls with it.
Every step of the oxygen transport cascade starts lower, and on the steep part of the dissociation curve, saturation collapses.
Within hours, the heart rate rises and the hypoxic drive raises ventilation.
This lifts alveolar oxygen but blows off carbon dioxide, which shifts the dissociation curve left.
Over weeks, E P O raises the haematocrit, and capillaries, myoglobin and two three D P G increase.
The right ventricle thickens, and the kidneys excrete bicarbonate at the cost of alkaline reserve.
Lactate falls without better oxygenation, which is the lactate paradox.
Acclimatisation needs two weeks up to two thousand three hundred metres, and a week per six hundred and ten metres beyond.
Without it, acute mountain sickness strikes within hours.
Events under two minutes are unharmed, while V O two max falls three per cent per three hundred metres.
Live high, train low works best, in responders whose E P O rises.
And that's how the body meets thin air, from the first hours on the mountain to training that puts altitude to work.
