## Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation

In sinus arrhythmia, the heart rate is faster when breathing in and slower when breathing out, and this is normal.
By contrast, atrioventricular nodal block, ectopic beats and re-entry are faults.
The ectopic beats in question are premature ventricular contractions, each known as a P V C.
Re-entry can cause fatal ventricular fibrillation, which a shock stops by depolarising every heart cell at once.
A raised S T segment marks a heart attack.
We'll look at each of these, from a normal irregularity to the mark a heart attack leaves on the trace.

## 1. A normal irregularity: sinus arrhythmia

We begin with sinus arrhythmia, a normal irregularity.
The sinoatrial, or S A, node is the heart's natural pacemaker.
The vagus nerve slows it by releasing acetylcholine.
In sinus arrhythmia, this braking follows the breath.
During expiration, or breathing out, lung stretch receptors trigger a rise in vagal activity, which slows the heart.
During inspiration, or breathing in, vagal activity falls, letting the heart rate increase.
Arrhythmia means an irregular rhythm, and this one is perfectly normal.
On the electrocardiogram, or E C G, the beats bunch together on breathing in and spread out on breathing out.

## 2. AV-nodal block, three degrees

Now to atrioventricular, or A V, nodal block, which comes in three degrees.
On the E C G, the P wave is the atria firing, or depolarising, and the Q R S complex is the ventricles firing.
The P R interval is the delay between them, and most of it is spent in the A V node.
That node is the only electrical bridge between the atria and the ventricles.
In normal sinus rhythm, every Q R S complex follows a P wave.
A normal P R interval lasts between zero point one two and zero point two seconds.
Mild injury to the A V node causes first degree A V nodal block.
The node still passes every signal, but slowly, which pushes the P R interval past the normal range, to longer than zero point two seconds.
Every P wave keeps its Q R S complex.
More injury causes second degree A V nodal block.
Signals from the atria are called supraventricular depolarisations, and the node passes on only some of them.
As a result, some P waves have a Q R S complex and others do not.
One example is a ratio of three to two, with three P waves for every two Q R S complexes.
In each group of three, the P wave left without a Q R S complex is the dropped beat.
Other ratios occur as well, such as two to one.
Complete block is third degree A V nodal block.
The A V node conducts no signals, leaving the atria and ventricles electrically disconnected.
The atria keep their own pace, which is why the P waves stay regular.
A secondary pacemaker, a slower backup below the block, drives the ventricles.
Their Q R S complexes are thus regular but slow.
With the two rhythms ignoring each other, both at their own regular rates, the P R interval keeps changing.
To grade the block on an E C G strip, work through four steps.
First, measure the P R interval.
Second, check that every P wave has a Q R S complex.
Third, check whether the P waves and Q R S complexes are related at all.
Finally, name the degree.
A long P R interval is first degree, dropped beats are second degree, and no relation at all is third degree.

## 3. Premature ventricular contractions

Let's turn to the premature ventricular contraction, or P V C.
It is an ectopic beat, meaning one that starts in the wrong place.
Beats normally start in the atria, at the S A node.
During acute myocardial ischaemia, which is poor blood flow to heart muscle, a beat can instead start in the ventricle and disturb the rhythm.
The P V C has no P wave, since it started in the ventricle.
It is also premature, coming before the next beat was due.
Its shape, amplitude and duration are abnormal.
The reason is that the signal skips the normal fast conducting pathways and creeps slowly through ordinary muscle.
That slow creep is what makes the complex wide.
After the P V C comes a long diastolic, or filling, gap, known as the compensatory pause.
This happens because the next signal from the S A node is ignored.
It finds the ventricles still refractory, unable to fire again yet.
The pause lets the ventricles fill more, giving a stronger next beat.

## 4. Re-entry

Next comes re-entry, another cause of extra beats.
Here, one signal loops back and fires the same tissue twice.
In normal tissue, a signal splits around a patch that cannot conduct.
The two halves meet and cancel each other out.
They cancel because each runs into muscle the other has just fired.
That muscle is refractory, meaning it is unable to fire again yet.
Injured tissue, by contrast, conducts only in the reverse direction, like a one-way street.
One half of the signal is stopped at the injured tissue, while the other goes round and comes back through it.
Crossing damaged tissue is slow.
By the time the signal gets through, the tissue it reaches has recovered and fires again, too early.
If the signal keeps circling, this ectopic beat repeats.

## 5. Ventricular fibrillation and defibrillation

When re-entry goes uncontrolled, it produces rapid, uncoordinated excitations of the ventricles.
This is called ventricular fibrillation, or V F.
The muscle quivers rather than pumping, so there is no cardiac output, and death follows within minutes.
V F is often the fatal result of myocardial ischaemia, anaesthetic overdose or electrocution.
To stop it, a manual external defibrillator delivers an electrical shock through pads or paddles placed on the chest.
The shock depolarises every heart muscle cell at once, like a reset button.
A looping signal can only travel into recovered tissue.
With every cell refractory at the same time, all the loops die out.
As the cells recover, the fastest pacemaker, the S A node, takes over again.

## 6. The ischaemic marker on the trace

Last of all, ischaemia, which is poor coronary blood flow, also shows on the E C G.
The E C G is made of waves, called P, Q, R, S and T, with segments between them.
The S T segment runs from the end of the S wave to the start of the T wave.
It is normally flat, since at that point all the ventricular cells are equally activated, and the E C G sees no difference.
After a myocardial infarction, or heart attack, damaged cells cannot stay activated.
The S T segment is therefore elevated.

## Summary

To sum up, sinus arrhythmia is normal.
Vagal braking rises during expiration, so the heart rate falls when breathing out and rises when breathing in.
In first degree A V nodal block, the signals from the atria are delayed, with a P R interval longer than zero point two seconds.
In second degree, they are partly blocked, for example at a ratio of three to two.
In third degree, they are fully blocked, which leaves regular P waves unrelated to slow, regular Q R S complexes.
A premature ventricular contraction starts in the ventricle, which is why it has no P wave.
It comes early, looks wide and is followed by a compensatory pause that boosts filling.
Re-entry through one-way injured tissue fires tissue again too early, and it can become V F, with no cardiac output.
A shock depolarises every cell at once.
As a result, the loops die out and the S A node takes over.
Finally, S T elevation marks a heart attack.
And that's the heart's rhythm, from a normal irregularity, through the shock that resets it, to the mark a heart attack leaves on the trace.
