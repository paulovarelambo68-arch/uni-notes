## Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex

With each beat, the aorta stretches to store blood, and it recoils between beats, keeping the flow continuous.
Systolic pressure is read when the Korotkoff sounds appear, and diastolic pressure when they disappear.
Mean arterial pressure equals the diastolic pressure plus one third of the difference between systolic and diastolic.
It is normally ninety-five to one hundred millimetres of mercury.
Systolic pressure rises with age.
Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and the aortic arch hold mean arterial pressure steady, acting through the S A node, the heart muscle, the arterioles and the veins.
In this note, we'll see how stop-start ejection becomes continuous flow, how pressure is measured and averaged, how it changes with age, and how the baroreflex keeps it level.

## 1. From intermittent ejection to continuous flow

We start with how intermittent ejection becomes continuous flow.
Blood enters the aorta at a peak speed of one and a half to two metres per second, and a peak flow of six hundred millilitres per second.
Averaged over the whole beat, however, it moves at only zero point two metres per second.
About two-thirds of the stroke volume is ejected in the first third of systole.
The vessels downstream cannot take blood that fast.
Instead, the aorta stretches to hold seventy to eighty per cent of the stroke volume, and only twenty to thirty per cent flows straight on.
In diastole, the stretched aorta recoils.
This pushes the stored blood into the next segment of artery, which expands and recoils in turn.
In this way, elastic recoil turns stop-start ejection into continuous, pulsing flow.
This is called the windkessel, or air-chamber, effect.

## 2. Measuring arterial pressure

Next, how is arterial pressure measured?
It is measured with a sphygmomanometer and a stethoscope.
The sphygmomanometer is an inflatable cuff on the upper arm, attached to a manometer, which is a pressure gauge.
The stethoscope is placed over the brachial artery, below the cuff.
The measurement has four steps.
First, the cuff is inflated above systolic pressure.
This squashes the artery shut, stopping flow and the pulse below the cuff.
Second, the cuff is let down to just below systolic pressure.
Now the peak of each beat forces the artery open briefly.
The turbulent, or swirling, spurt of blood makes a tapping sound, called a Korotkoff sound.
Systolic pressure is the cuff pressure at which sounds are first heard.
Third, once the cuff is below diastolic pressure, the artery stays open throughout the beat, and flow turns smooth and silent.
Diastolic pressure is the cuff pressure at which the sounds disappear.
The sounds become muffled a few millimetres of mercury before they disappear.
Fourth, in some healthy people the sounds persist well below true diastolic pressure.
For them, diastolic pressure cannot be measured accurately.
Readings are given in millimetres of mercury, as mercury is thirteen and a half times denser than water or blood, which keeps the column short.
They are taken at heart level, with the upper arm by the side.
Venous pressure, by contrast, is low enough to measure directly.
In intensive care, a saline-filled tube in a large vein reads the central venous pressure, the pressure where the great veins enter the heart.
It is normally six to eleven millimetres of mercury.

## 3. Systolic, diastolic, pulse pressure and the mean

The two readings are the peak and the trough of each pressure wave.
Systolic pressure is the highest, reached during ejection, and diastolic pressure is the lowest, reached at the end of diastole.
The difference between them is the pulse pressure, which is systolic minus diastolic.
For example, a reading of one hundred and twenty-five over eighty has a pulse pressure of forty-five millimetres of mercury.
This swing fades as blood moves into smaller vessels, until the arterioles and capillaries have an almost steady pressure.
Inside the ventricle, pressure falls almost to zero in diastole.
Yet arterial pressure stays near eighty, because the closed aortic valve traps the blood in the arteries, where the recoiling walls keep squeezing it.
Averaged over a beat, the pressure wave gives the mean arterial pressure, the pressure that drives blood round the body.
From a reading, mean arterial pressure equals the diastolic pressure plus one third of the pulse pressure.
This is not the simple average.
The reason is that diastole lasts about two-thirds of each beat at rest, leaving pressure longer near its low value.
For the same reading of one hundred and twenty-five over eighty, the formula gives eighty plus a third of forty-five.
This comes to ninety-five millimetres of mercury, not the simple average of one hundred and two point five.

## 4. Normal values and age

In resting adults, normal systolic pressure is one hundred and ten to one hundred and thirty-nine millimetres of mercury.
Normal diastolic pressure is seventy to eighty-four.
Normal mean arterial pressure is ninety-five to one hundred.
Pressure creeps up with age.
At the age of twenty, a typical reading is one hundred and ten over seventy.
At sixty, it is one hundred and forty over eighty, just past the upper limit of normal.
Measured at the brachial artery in men and women of different ages, pressure follows three trends.
Systolic pressure climbs throughout life, diastolic pressure rises until about fifty and then falls, and the mean rises moderately.
What drives the mean up is the increase in total peripheral resistance that comes with age.
The gap between systolic and diastolic pressure depends on compliance, which is how easily the aorta stretches to take each stroke volume.
With age, the aorta stiffens, and a stiffer aorta has two effects.
First, each beat pushes pressure higher, which raises systolic pressure.
Second, less stored blood remains to hold pressure up between beats, so diastolic pressure falls.
After about fifty, this second effect outweighs the rise in total peripheral resistance, which is why diastolic pressure starts to fall.
That fall may reduce coronary blood flow, because the left coronary artery fills mainly in diastole.
Over twenty-four hours, pressure dips at night, and this dip is often missing in hypertension.

## 5. The baroreceptor reflex

Finally, we come to the baroreceptor reflex.
Since cardiac output equals stroke volume times heart rate, mean arterial pressure equals stroke volume times heart rate times total peripheral resistance.
The body can thus keep pressure steady by adjusting any of these three factors.
Rearranged, the equation gives heart rate as mean arterial pressure divided by the product of stroke volume and total peripheral resistance.
Short-term control needs information on arterial pressure and blood volume.
Pressure is sensed by baroreceptors, which are nerve endings that fire when the artery wall stretches.
The baroreceptors sit at two sites.
One is the carotid sinus, a slight widening where the common carotid artery forks in the neck.
It signals through the glossopharyngeal nerve, cranial nerve nine.
The other is the aortic arch, which signals through the vagus nerve, cranial nerve ten.
Both report to the cardiovascular centre in the medulla.
The S A node, the heart's pacemaker, senses nothing, and only receives the reflex's commands.
Recordings from the carotid sinus nerve at lower, normal and elevated arterial pressure show that the higher the pressure, the faster the firing.
The impulses come in bursts, on each upstroke of pressure.
The cardiovascular centre receives this signal.
Through sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves, it drives four effector organs, namely the S A node, the cardiac muscle, the arterioles and the veins.
Each effector acts on pressure through heart rate, stroke volume or total peripheral resistance.
Together, they form a negative feedback loop, which pushes any change in pressure back towards normal.
The arterioles of the lungs are not effectors, as they are not part of total peripheral resistance.
When mean arterial pressure rises, the baroreceptors fire faster.
The centre responds by raising parasympathetic activity and lowering sympathetic activity, and this acts on each effector.
At the heart, the S A node slows, which lowers heart rate.
Contractility, the strength of contraction at a given filling, falls too, and with it the stroke volume.
The arterioles relax, which is vasodilation, and total peripheral resistance falls.
The veins, which hold most of the blood, also relax and hold more of it.
As a result, less blood returns to the heart, meaning venous return falls.
The ventricle fills less in turn, lowering its end-diastolic volume, and it ejects less.
Lower heart rate and stroke volume reduce cardiac output, and with total peripheral resistance also lower, mean arterial pressure falls.
When mean arterial pressure falls, for example after a haemorrhage or on standing up, the loop runs in reverse.
Heart rate and contractility rise, the arterioles constrict to raise total peripheral resistance, and the veins constrict to return more blood to the heart.
Standing up shows how this works.
When you stand, the column of blood below the heart adds about fifty millimetres of mercury to pressures in the thigh.
This is why, when the brachial artery reads one hundred and twenty over eighty, a femoral artery reads about one hundred and seventy over one hundred and thirty.
Blood pools in the easily stretched leg veins, which lowers venous return and cardiac output.
The reflex responds, helped by valves in the veins and by the skeletal muscle pump, in which contracting leg muscles squeeze blood upwards.
While you are standing, the carotid sinus, which guards the brain's blood supply, sits above the heart and reads a lower pressure.
Lying down suddenly reverses all this.
The legs lose their extra fifty millimetres of mercury, and venous return and cardiac output rise.
The carotid sinus is now level with the heart, so it senses a higher pressure.
The reflex therefore slows the heart and dilates vessels, and pressure at heart level barely changes.
The reflex handles only quick changes, though.
If pressure stays high for a few days, the baroreceptors reset to the new level and stop opposing it.
Something else must set pressure in the long run, and that job falls to the kidneys, which control blood volume.
When pressure is high, the kidneys excrete more salt and water, which is called pressure diuresis.
Blood volume falls, followed by end-diastolic volume, stroke volume, cardiac output and mean arterial pressure.
When pressure is low, the kidneys retain salt and water, and the chain runs the other way.
Pressure settles where the output of salt and water equals the intake.

## Summary

To sum up, the aorta stores seventy to eighty per cent of each stroke volume and recoils in diastole, turning stop-start ejection into continuous flow.
With a cuff, systolic pressure is the pressure at which the Korotkoff sounds first appear, and diastolic pressure is the pressure at which they disappear.
Pulse pressure is the difference between systolic and diastolic pressure.
Mean arterial pressure equals the diastolic pressure plus one third of the pulse pressure, since diastole lasts two-thirds of the resting beat.
At rest, normal systolic pressure is one hundred and ten to one hundred and thirty-nine millimetres of mercury, and normal diastolic pressure seventy to eighty-four.
The normal mean is ninety-five to one hundred.
With age, systolic pressure rises as the aorta stiffens, while diastolic pressure falls after about fifty.
Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch report to the medulla.
The medulla answers a rise in mean arterial pressure by slowing the S A node, reducing contractility, and dilating the arterioles and veins.
It answers a fall by doing the reverse.
Lying down suddenly raises venous return and the pressure at the carotid sinus.
The reflex then slows the heart and dilates vessels, and pressure stays almost level.
In the long term, though, control belongs to the kidneys.
And that's how blood pressure is measured, and how the body holds it steady.
