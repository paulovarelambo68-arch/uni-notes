## B Cells and Antibodies

The receptors that B cells carry on their surface to catch antigen, known as B cell receptors, are antibodies.
Once activated, B cells secrete antibodies with the same specificity as their receptor, meaning antibodies that recognise the same antigen.
Each antibody is a Y-shaped protein.
Its two arms, called the antigen-binding fragments, or F A B, catch the antigen.
Its stem, called the crystallisable fragment, or F C, attaches to other immune cells and switches them on.
Between them, the arms and the stem give antibodies three jobs.
They block toxins and microbes by binding them directly, which is called neutralisation.
They also target microbes to immune cells for destruction, and they activate complement.
Antibodies come in five classes, called isotypes, namely I G G, I G M, I G A, I G D and I G E, where I G stands for immunoglobulin.
Of these, I G A is the one abundant in saliva and other secretions.
Over the next few parts, we'll move from the receptor to the antibody's shape, on to its jobs, and finally to the five isotypes.

## 1. B cell receptors are antibodies

To begin, in the Respond step of adaptive immunity, B cells, or B lymphocytes, make antibodies.
These antibodies act in the blood and other body fluids, so antibody defence is called humoral immunity.
The name comes from humour, an old word for a body fluid.
Another name for antibodies is immunoglobulins, meaning immune globulins, a family of plasma proteins.
Immunoglobulin is shortened to I G, which is the I G at the start of I G G, I G A and the other antibody classes.
Antibodies first appear on the B cell itself, because B cell receptors are antibodies.
They are held in its membrane with their antigen-catching arms facing out.
When these receptors bind their antigen, the B cell is activated, with a boost from helper T cells.
Next, it divides again and again into a clone of identical B cells.
Some of these turn into plasma cells, which release antibodies into the blood.
These antibodies have the same specificity as the B cell receptor.
In other words, they recognise exactly the same antigen, because they carry the same antigen-catching tips as the receptor.

## 2. The shape of an antibody

Now, the receptor and the secreted antibody share the same Y-shaped design, built from protein chains.
A polypeptide chain is a single string of amino acids, and a protein can be made of one such chain or of several joined together.
An antibody has four polypeptide chains, two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains.
Here, heavy and light simply mean larger and smaller.
The two heavy chains run side by side to form the stem of the Y.
They then separate to form the inner half of each arm, while each light chain lies along the outer half of one arm.
Along its length, each chain has a variable region and a constant region.
The variable region is the end at the tip of an arm, and it differs from one B cell clone to the next.
Each clone makes its own version, shaped to fit that clone's antigen, and this is what gives the antibody its specificity.
The constant region, by contrast, is the rest of the chain, and it comes in only a few standard versions.
The variable regions give rise to the antigen-binding fragments, the two F A B arms.
The variable tips of each arm form an antigen-binding site shaped to fit the clone's own antigen, which gives highly specific binding to antigen.
Because the two arms are identical, one antibody has two antigen-binding sites.
Meanwhile, the constant regions give rise to the F C, the crystallisable fragment that forms the stem.
The F C binds to receptors on immune cells to trigger their activation.
These names come from experiments that cut antibodies into fragments, in which the stem fragment formed crystals.
In short, the arms recognise and the stem recruits.

## 3. What antibodies do

This division of labour gives antibodies three functions.
The first is neutralisation of toxins and microbes by direct binding, and it needs only the arms.
The arms cover the parts that a toxin or microbe uses to attach to and enter cells.
The second is targeting of microbes to immune cells for destruction, and this one needs the stem.
The reason is that immune cells carry F C receptors, which grip the F C of an antibody.
One route is opsonisation and phagocytosis.
Here, antibodies coat a microbe, a step called opsonisation, and a phagocyte grips their stems and engulfs it.
Another route is antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, or A D C C.
In this case, antibodies coat a target cell, such as an infected cell.
A natural killer cell, or N K cell, grips those antibodies and kills the target.
The third function is activation of complement, which antibodies on a microbe set off through the classical pathway.
Complement activation leads to lysis, or bursting, of the microbe.
It brings about phagocytosis of microbes opsonised with C three B, a complement fragment that phagocytes grip with C three B receptors.
On top of that, it causes inflammation, which draws in more immune cells.
Put simply, every route starts with the arms binding, and all but neutralisation then depend on the stem.

## 4. The five isotypes

Since the stem is made of heavy chains, the heavy chain decides what an antibody can do.
There are five types of heavy chain, called gamma, mu, alpha, delta and epsilon.
These give rise to five isotypes, the classes of antibody named after them, I G G, I G M, I G A, I G D and I G E.
I G G, I G E and I G D each have one Y unit, which makes them monomers.
I G A often has two, forming a dimer, and I G M has five, forming a pentamer.
As for how they are built, disulfide bonds, which are chemical bridges between sulfur atoms, hold each single Y together.
I G M is a star of five units with their stems at the centre.
The units are linked by disulfide bonds and by a joining chain, a small protein that holds units together.
I G A, for its part, is two units joined stem to stem by a joining chain.
It is wrapped in a secretory protein, which protects it from digestion in secretions.
Each isotype has its own role.
Take I G G, which makes up seventy to seventy-five per cent of circulating immunoglobulins and has four subclasses, I G G one to I G G four.
What's more, it is the only immunoglobulin transported across the placenta, so it protects the newborn, whose own immunity is immature.
After that comes I G M, the first antibody produced in response to a new infection.
As a monomer, it also sits with I G D on the surface of many B cells as a B cell receptor.
I G A is abundant in secretions, including saliva, where it stops microbes before they enter the body.
This is why exercise studies measure salivary I G A.
As for I G D, it makes up less than one per cent of serum immunoglobulin, and it serves with I G M as a B cell receptor.
Lastly, I G E features in allergic responses.
It is important, too, in defence against infection by helminths, or parasitic worms, working alongside mast cells and eosinophils.
Across a response, I G M comes first and I G G comes later.

## Summary

Pulling the threads together, B cell receptors are antibodies, otherwise known as immunoglobulins.
After activation and clonal expansion, plasma cells release antibodies with the same specificity as the B cell receptor.
Each antibody is a Y of two heavy and two light chains.
Its variable regions form two antigen-binding F A B arms, and its constant regions form the F C stem, which binds F C receptors on immune cells.
Antibodies neutralise toxins and microbes, target microbes for phagocytosis or A D C C, and activate complement by the classical pathway.
Five heavy chains give five isotypes.
Among them, I G G, I G E and I G D are monomers, I G A is often a dimer and I G M is a pentamer.
I G G, which accounts for seventy to seventy-five per cent of circulating immunoglobulin, is the only one to cross the placenta.
I G M comes first in infection, and on B cells it partners I G D, which makes up under one per cent of serum immunoglobulin.
I G A is abundant in saliva, and I G E features in allergy and helminth defence.
With that, we've reached the end of B cells and antibodies.
