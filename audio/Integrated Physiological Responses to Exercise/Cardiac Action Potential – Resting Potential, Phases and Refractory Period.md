## Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period

Gap junctions make the muscle cells of the ventricle act as one electrical unit, a functional syncytium.
At rest, each cell sits at about minus ninety millivolts, close to the potassium equilibrium potential of minus ninety-four millivolts.
When the cell fires, sodium entering makes a fast spike.
Calcium entering holds a plateau at zero to minus twenty millivolts, and potassium leaving ends it.
Because the sodium channels are locked shut during the plateau, the cell cannot fire again for about two hundred milliseconds.
That is why heart muscle cannot be tetanised.
In this note, we'll see how the cells are linked and what sets the resting potential, before following the five phases, the long plateau and the refractory periods.

## 1. A functional syncytium

Let's begin with how the heart's muscle cells come to act as one unit.
For the heart to pump, its muscle cells must contract together.
The electrical signal that triggers contraction, the action potential, therefore has to reach every cell.
The cells of the myocardium, which is the heart muscle, are interwoven and may branch at either end.
Where they meet, they are joined by intercalated discs.
These discs contain gap junctions, tiny tunnels that let current flow straight from one cell into the next.
Electrically, the cells thus behave like one giant cell, a functional syncytium.
This is how a stimulus arising anywhere in the ventricle leads to complete contraction of both chambers, an all-or-none contraction.
The route that the wave of excitation takes is set by the heart's conducting system.

## 2. The resting membrane

Turning to the resting membrane, every cell has a voltage across its membrane, called the membrane potential.
It is measured inside relative to outside, in millivolts.
In the contractile cells of the atria and ventricles, which are the heart's chambers, it rests at about minus ninety millivolts, meaning the inside is negative.
A rise towards zero or above is called depolarisation, and a fall back towards minus ninety millivolts is repolarisation.
This voltage depends on three positive ions.
Potassium is far more concentrated inside the cell, at about one hundred and forty millimoles per litre, against four outside.
Sodium and calcium, on the other hand, are more concentrated outside.
Ions can only cross the membrane through membrane channels.
How easily an ion crosses is its conductance.
By definition, conductance equals one over the membrane's resistance to that ion.
When the potassium, sodium or calcium conductance rises, that ion flows across.
This flow of charge is a current, named after its ion as the potassium, sodium or calcium current.
Since all three ions are positive, a single rule tells you what a current does.
Potassium leaving makes the inside more negative, whereas sodium or calcium entering makes it more positive.
It follows that an inward current depolarises the cell, and an outward current repolarises it.
At rest, permeability to calcium is very low.
Permeability to potassium, by contrast, is high, since many potassium channels are open.
This resting potassium current is called I K one.
Permeability to sodium is about ten per cent of that to potassium.
That lets a small inward background current, called I B, trickle in.
The resting potential is mainly due to two things, the high potassium concentration inside the cell and the high permeability to potassium.
Potassium leaks out, taking positive charge with it, and the inside turns negative, which pulls potassium back.
At one voltage, the outward push of the concentration difference and the inward pull of the charge balance exactly, and potassium stops moving overall.
That voltage is the ion's equilibrium potential, and for potassium it is minus ninety-four millivolts.
The resting potential sits close to the potassium equilibrium potential, given that the resting membrane passes mostly potassium.
The small sodium leak, the background current, keeps it a little less negative, at minus ninety millivolts.
In working heart muscle, this resting potential is stable, staying flat until excitation arrives.
That sets it apart from the voltage of pacemaker cells, which drifts upwards between beats.

## 3. The five phases

Now to the action potential itself, which the cell fires when excitation arrives through the gap junctions.
It is divided into five phases, numbered with the upstroke as phase zero and rest as phase four.
The sequence begins in phase four, the resting membrane potential.
It is the flat line between beats, held by the resting potassium current and the background current.
From there, phase zero is the upstroke, a sudden jump to a positive peak.
Phase one is early repolarisation, a small dip just after the peak.
Phase two is the plateau, a long, almost flat stretch near zero millivolts.
Last comes phase three, late repolarisation, which is the fall back to rest.
We'll follow the cell out of rest and back again.
The cell leaves rest when incoming excitation lifts its voltage to threshold, at about minus sixty-five millivolts.
At threshold, voltage-sensitive fast sodium channels open very quickly.
With that, the membrane's permeability to sodium suddenly rises, and sodium floods in.
This inflow, the sodium current, causes the depolarisation.
Within one to two milliseconds, the cell loses its negative charge and overshoots to about plus forty millivolts.
It stops short of the sodium equilibrium potential, because potassium is still flowing out at the same time.
The peak is brief, for two reasons.
First, the fast sodium channels are self-inactivating, which is to say they shut themselves a moment after opening.
Sodium conductance falls as a result.
Second, within a couple of milliseconds, a short-lived potassium outflow repolarises the membrane by a few millivolts.
This outflow is called the transient outward current, or I T O.
That brief dip is phase one, known as the notch.
Rather than falling back to rest, the voltage holds the plateau, phase two, which is a feature unique to cardiac muscle.
It sits just below the peak, at about zero to minus twenty millivolts.
The plateau is produced by a small yet sustained inward calcium current, through voltage-operated L-type calcium channels.
The L stands for long-lasting, which is why the current is labelled I calcium L.
These channels begin to open as the upstroke passes about minus thirty to minus thirty-five millivolts.
They open slowly compared with the fast sodium channels, though, leaving their current to take over only after the spike.
The inward calcium current almost balances the outward potassium current, keeping the voltage nearly flat.
Late in the plateau, the calcium channels begin to inactivate.
Part of the inward current is then carried by sodium, entering through the sodium-calcium exchanger.
This carrier swaps three sodium ions in for one calcium ion out, bringing in three positive charges and removing two.
The result is a net inward current, which sustains the late plateau while clearing calcium from the cell.
In phase three, as the calcium channels close, potassium conductance increases.
Potassium leaves the cell as the potassium current, and the membrane repolarises back to rest.
The whole action potential lasts about two hundred and fifty milliseconds.
As for the resting value, the one to learn is minus ninety millivolts.
Each phase is driven mainly by one current.
The upstroke belongs to the inward sodium current, which gives the rapid depolarisation.
At the notch, the transient outward current carries potassium out, for a brief early repolarisation.
The plateau is held by the inward L-type calcium current, which also triggers contraction, the process of excitation-contraction coupling.
On the way down, the outward potassium current repolarises the cell.
At rest, the outward resting potassium current sets the resting potential, alongside the small inward background current, carried mostly by sodium.

## 4. Why the plateau is long

Next, what is the point of such a long plateau?
It matters for two reasons.
The first is that it keeps the cell refractory, unable to be excited again, for two hundred to four hundred milliseconds.
Its contraction, meanwhile, is shorter, lasting from two hundred to two hundred and fifty milliseconds.
A new contraction cannot start until the last one is over, and so contractions can never fuse.
Such fusion, called tetanus, happens in skeletal muscle when rapid stimuli start each contraction before the previous one has relaxed.
A heart in tetanus could never relax to refill with blood, which would be fatal.
The second reason is that the plateau not only starts contraction but also sets its strength.
The calcium entering during the plateau is what triggers contraction.
More calcium entry thus gives a stronger contraction.

## 5. Refractory periods and the sodium-channel gates

Let's finish with the refractory time, which has two parts.
Throughout the plateau, while the voltage is still near zero millivolts, the heart is completely inexcitable.
No stimulus, however strong, can trigger a new action potential.
This is the absolute refractory period, which spans the upstroke and the plateau and lasts about two hundred milliseconds.
Excitability returns gradually during repolarisation, once the voltage is back to about minus forty millivolts.
This gradual recovery is the relative refractory period, which lasts about fifty milliseconds.
In this period, only a stronger stimulus than normal works, and the response is smaller.
A second action potential can be triggered at different moments, and the earlier it starts, the smaller and slower it is.
Only a second action potential triggered after full repolarisation is normal.
The cause of all this is the fast sodium channel, which has two gates in a row, like two doors.
Sodium passes only when both are open.
The M gate, or activation gate, opens when the membrane depolarises.
The H gate, or inactivation gate, shuts shortly afterwards, and it does not reopen until the membrane repolarises.
If we follow a single channel round this cycle, it starts at rest, closed but excitable, with the M gate shut and the H gate open.
On the upstroke, both gates are open and sodium flows in.
By the plateau, the H gate has swung shut, which inactivates the channel.
It becomes excitable again only after repolarisation.
This explains both refractory periods.
During the plateau, the H gates are shut, so the sodium channels are inactivated and no stimulus can open them.
Once the membrane repolarises past about minus forty millivolts, some H gates reopen.
A normal stimulus cannot open enough channels to overcome the outward potassium current, although a stronger stimulus can.
With fewer channels taking part, the upstroke is smaller and slower.
The same recovery rule explains why an electrical wave that loops back on itself, known as re-entry, can only re-excite muscle that has recovered.

## Summary

To sum up, gap junctions make heart muscle a functional syncytium that contracts all-or-none.
High potassium permeability holds the resting cell at about minus ninety millivolts, near the potassium equilibrium potential of minus ninety-four millivolts.
From a threshold of minus sixty-five millivolts, fast sodium channels drive the upstroke to plus forty millivolts.
The transient outward current trims the peak, and on the plateau the L-type calcium current balances potassium outflow.
Rising potassium conductance then repolarises the cell.
The H gates keep the sodium channels inactivated until repolarisation.
That leaves the cell absolutely refractory for about two hundred milliseconds, and relatively refractory for about fifty milliseconds.
The heart therefore cannot be tetanised, and the calcium that enters on the plateau sets the strength of each beat.
And that's the cardiac action potential, from rest back to rest.
