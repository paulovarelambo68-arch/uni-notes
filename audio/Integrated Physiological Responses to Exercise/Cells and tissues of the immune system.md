## Cells and tissues of the immune system

Here we look at the cells and tissues of the immune system.
Immune cells are formed and mature in the primary lymphoid organs, which are the bone marrow and the thymus.
They are then concentrated and do their work in the secondary ones, namely the lymph nodes, the spleen and the mucosal immune system.
Every one of them comes from a single stem cell in the marrow, through a process called haematopoiesis, which splits them into a myeloid family and a lymphoid family.
Some of these cells circulate, whereas dendritic cells, macrophages and mast cells are resident sentinels.
Only B cells and T cells are adaptive, so all the others, including neutrophils and natural killer cells, are innate.
We'll take it in order, from the organs, through the cells' origins and lifestyles, to the full cast sorted by branch.

## 1. Where immune cells are made and mature: the primary lymphoid organs

First, the cells of both branches of the immune system are produced and gathered in lymphoid tissues.
Lymphoid means made of, or housing, lymphocytes.
Lymphocytes are a kind of white blood cell, chiefly the B cells and T cells.
Other kinds of white blood cell, such as neutrophils, come up a little later.
The primary lymphoid organs, the bone marrow and the thymus, are where immune cells are formed and mature, that is, where they become ready to work.
All immune cells are produced in the bone marrow, and B cells also mature there.
T cells, on the other hand, leave the marrow early and mature in the thymus, a gland behind the breastbone.

## 2. Where immune cells gather and work: the secondary (peripheral) lymphoid organs

Once mature, the cells spread out to the secondary, or peripheral, lymphoid organs, where they are concentrated and do their work.
These are the lymph nodes, the spleen and the mucosal immune system.
Lymph nodes filter lymph, which is the tissue fluid draining back towards the blood, and they are where lymphocytes meet antigen.
The spleen, by contrast, filters the blood.
As for the mucosal immune system, it is lymphoid tissue lying under the mucosa.
The mucosa refers to the moist mucous membranes lining the gut, the airways and the urogenital tract, through which most microbes enter.
The mucosal immune system covers mucosa-associated, gut-associated and bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue.
Further examples are the tonsils and adenoids, and the urogenital lymphoid tissue.

## 3. Haematopoiesis: one stem cell, two families

Back in the marrow, this production has a name, haematopoiesis, which means the formation of blood cells.
All blood cells, immune cells included, come from one haematopoietic stem cell, which gives rise to two progenitors.
The first is the common myeloid progenitor, myeloid being the family of cells that are not lymphocytes.
It produces erythrocytes, or red cells, and platelets.
Beyond these, it produces the white cells called neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils and monocytes.
The monocytes, in turn, become macrophages.
The second is the common lymphoid progenitor, which produces the lymphocytes.
B cells end up as plasma cells, which secrete antibodies, and as memory cells, which remember an antigen.
T cells, meanwhile, end up as helper T cells, cytotoxic or killer T cells, and regulatory T cells.
An older name for regulatory T cells is suppressor T cells.
Natural killer cells, or N K cells, are lymphocytes too.
Dendritic cells and mast cells, the tissue guards we'll meet next, arise mainly from the myeloid family.
There are intermediate stages along the way, such as the myeloblast and the promonocyte, but we can skip those.
What matters is that every immune cell traces back to one stem cell.

## 4. Cells that circulate and cells that stand guard

When it comes to where they live, immune cells follow one of two lifestyles.
Some circulate between the blood and the tissues, while others are resident in the tissues.
The circulating cells, which can also enter tissues, are the neutrophils, the monocytes and macrophages, the eosinophils and the lymphocytes.
The resident ones are the dendritic cells, macrophages and mast cells.
These are sentinel cells, guards posted in the tissue to raise the alarm.
Macrophages appear on both lists because a monocyte in the blood becomes a macrophage once it settles in a tissue.
Each sentinel has its own job.
The dendritic cell detects pathogens and presents them to lymphocytes.
In other words, it catches disease-causing microbes and shows pieces of them to lymphocytes.
The macrophage performs phagocytosis, engulfing and digesting microbes.
Finally, the mast cell handles allergy and defence against helminths, or parasitic worms.

## 5. The cast by branch

With every cell in its place, the cast can now be sorted by branch.
The innate cells are the monocytes and macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, mast cells, dendritic cells and natural killer cells.
The adaptive cells are two kinds of lymphocyte, the B cells and T cells.
N K cells are therefore the odd ones out, lymphocytes by family but innate by branch.
Next, we can go through the jobs cell by cell.
Neutrophils are the most abundant white cell and the first to arrive at an infection, where they phagocytose microbes.
Being innate, they are present and functional from birth, they respond immediately, and they recognise microbes non-specifically.
However, they do not develop or adapt over a lifetime.
Monocytes and macrophages also present antigen.
Eosinophils and basophils act against parasites and in allergy.
N K cells kill virus-infected and cancerous body cells.
Turning to the adaptive side, B cells make antibodies.
T cells carry out cell-mediated immunity, meaning defence by cells rather than by antibodies.

## Summary

To recap, immune cells are made in the bone marrow, where B cells also mature, whereas T cells mature in the thymus.
Together, these are the primary lymphoid organs.
The cells work in the secondary lymphoid organs, which are the lymph nodes that filter lymph, the spleen that filters blood, and the mucosal immune system.
Haematopoiesis gives a myeloid family of erythrocytes, platelets, monocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils, and that family is the main source of dendritic and mast cells as well.
The other family is lymphoid, made up of B cells, T cells and N K cells.
The neutrophils, the monocytes and macrophages, the eosinophils and the lymphocytes all circulate, while dendritic cells, macrophages and mast cells are resident sentinels.
Only B cells and T cells are adaptive, so neutrophils, N K cells and the rest are innate.
That completes our tour of the immune system's cells and the tissues they call home.
