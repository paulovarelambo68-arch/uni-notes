## Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation

The heart pumps four to six litres of blood through two circuits in series, and into systemic beds in parallel.
These parallel beds keep resistance low and allow separate control of each organ's flow.
Mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance.
Flow is equal at every level, which makes blood slowest in the capillaries, whose combined area is largest.
The distensible veins, meanwhile, hold two-thirds of the blood.
In heavy exercise, most of the output is redirected to working muscle.
We'll now trace the blood through its circuits and vessels, unpack that equation, and see how the output is shared out.

## 1. What the system is for

Let's begin with what the cardiovascular system is for.
It exists to supply the tissues with oxygen and nutrients, and to remove carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions.
All of these are carried in the blood by bulk flow, meaning the whole fluid moves down a pressure difference.
They travel this way because diffusion is quick only over distances of micrometres, such as those in the lungs and tissues.
The system comprises the heart, arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules and veins.
Arterioles are small muscular arteries, and capillaries are thin-walled exchange vessels.
Altogether, the system holds four to six litres of blood.

## 2. Two circuits in series

This blood follows two circuits, the pulmonary and the systemic.
In the pulmonary circulation, the right ventricle pumps deoxygenated blood into the pulmonary arteries.
The pulmonary capillaries pick up oxygen and off-load carbon dioxide, and oxygenated blood returns to the left atrium in the pulmonary veins.
In the systemic circulation, the left ventricle pumps the blood into the aorta and its branches.
The tissue capillaries off-load oxygen and pick up carbon dioxide, and the systemic veins return deoxygenated blood to the right atrium.
These two circuits are in series, meaning one after the other rather than side by side, because each ends where the other begins.
The pulmonary circuit delivers blood to the left heart, which starts the systemic circuit.
The systemic circuit returns it to the right heart, which starts the pulmonary one.
All the blood therefore passes through both circuits in turn.
This means that both ventricles must pump the same volume per minute, though at very different pressures.
Otherwise, if one pumped more, blood would pile up in the circuit it feeds.
A separate lung circuit makes sense for two reasons.
One is that the lungs do not compete with other organs for flow.
The other is that they are close to the heart, with very low vascular resistance, so pumping blood through them takes little energy.
Blood in the pulmonary circuit arrives deoxygenated and is there for gas exchange.
For that reason, the airway walls, which do no gas exchange, are fed like any other tissue.
The bronchi receive oxygenated blood from the systemic circulation, through the bronchial arteries.
This is why the bronchi also appear among the systemic beds, which we turn to next.

## 3. Vascular beds in parallel

The systemic circuit itself is organised differently, with the left ventricle driving blood at high pressure into beds arranged mostly in parallel.
As a result, only part of the cardiac output passes through any one tissue at a time.
The beds are the brain, head and arms, the bronchi, the heart, the splanchnic circulation, the kidneys, and the trunk and legs.
The heart's bed is the coronary circulation, while the splanchnic circulation covers the liver, gastrointestinal tract and spleen.
Picture this layout as a ladder.
One side of the ladder is an arterial column, which carries blood out from the aorta.
The other side is a venous column, which brings the blood back to the heart.
Each organ is a separate branch running across from the arterial column to the venous one, like a rung.
Blood leaving the aorta takes just one rung and returns to the heart.
It does not pass through the brain, then the kidneys, then the legs.
This is what it means for organs to be in parallel.
They sit side by side, each fed directly from the arteries.
There are a few exceptions, which is why the beds are only mostly in parallel.
Blood from the gut and spleen, for example, flows on through the portal vein into the liver, crossing two organs in a row.
This parallel layout has two advantages.
The first is that total resistance is much lower in parallel than in series.
When blood must cross obstacles one after another, in series, their resistances add up, like toll booths on a single road.
The total is the sum of the individual resistances, written as R equals R one plus R two, and so on.
When paths sit side by side, in parallel, blood splits between them, so each extra path makes flow easier, like extra lanes on a motorway.
Here, it is the ease of flow, called conductance, that adds up.
Conductance is one over the resistance, and one over R equals one over R one plus one over R two, and so on.
Two organs that each have the same resistance thus give double that resistance in series, but only half that resistance in parallel, which is four times less.
The second advantage is that every organ gets the same arterial pressure, through its own arterioles.
These are small muscular arteries that act like taps, letting each organ's flow be turned up or down on its own, without affecting the others.

## 4. The three features and the one equation

Whatever the arrangement, a circulation has three features.
They are flow, pressure and resistance.
Flow is the cardiac output, pressure is the mean arterial pressure, and resistance is the total peripheral resistance.
One equation links them, stating that mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance.
In shorthand, M A P equals C O times T P R.
It is the hydrostatic equivalent of Ohm's law, which says that voltage equals current times resistance.
And, as the professor stresses, it is the most important formula in the cardiovascular system.
Taking the features one at a time, cardiac output is the volume ejected by either ventricle in one minute.
It equals stroke volume times heart rate.
Stroke volume is the number of litres ejected per beat, and heart rate is the number of beats per minute.
Cardiac output is five to six litres per minute at rest, and thirty to thirty-five litres per minute in severe exercise.
Mean arterial pressure, the second feature, is the arterial pressure averaged over a cardiac cycle.
It is estimated from a cuff reading as the diastolic pressure plus a third of the pulse pressure, which is the systolic minus the diastolic.
Blood flows from high to low pressure.
Hence, what drives it round the body is the pressure at the start of the systemic circuit minus the pressure at its end.
The pressure at the start is the mean arterial pressure, and the pressure at the end, in the right atrium, is the right atrial pressure.
As right atrial pressure is about zero millimetres of mercury, the driving pressure is simply the mean arterial pressure.
That explains why mean arterial pressure alone appears in the equation.
Resistance, the third feature, is how hard it is to push blood through the vessels.
By Ohm's law, pressure difference equals flow times resistance.
Rearranged, resistance equals pressure difference divided by flow.
For the whole systemic circulation, total peripheral resistance equals mean arterial pressure divided by cardiac output.
If we replace cardiac output with stroke volume times heart rate, mean arterial pressure equals stroke volume times heart rate times total peripheral resistance.
Pressure rises with each of the three, and the equation can be solved for any one term.
Cardiac output equals mean arterial pressure divided by total peripheral resistance.
Stroke volume equals mean arterial pressure divided by the product of heart rate and total peripheral resistance.
Heart rate equals mean arterial pressure divided by the product of total peripheral resistance and stroke volume.

## 5. Vessel numbers, cross-sectional area and velocity

Next, let's follow this flow through its tree of vessels.
A single aorta of about two point five centimetres divides into about one hundred and sixty million arterioles and five billion capillaries of eight micrometres.
Consequently, total cross-sectional area rises from the aorta to the arterioles, reaching about one thousand square centimetres at the capillaries.
The capillaries merge into ever fewer, wider veins, ending in two venae cavae of about three point two centimetres.
On average, the venous side has more and larger vessels than the arterial side.
As a result, total area is far higher in the venules and veins than in the arterioles and arteries.
This matters, since volume flow is the same at every level, with the same volume crossing the aorta and the capillaries each minute.
Flow equals velocity times area, which makes velocity equal to flow divided by area.
In other words, velocity is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area.
With the heart pumping in bursts, blood in the aorta shoots forward at up to one hundred and fifty centimetres per second during each contraction.
Between beats it almost stops, at close to zero, and its speed is therefore given as an average.
That average is about twenty centimetres per second in the aorta and arteries.
In the capillaries, it drops to zero point zero five centimetres per second, allowing time for diffusion across the capillary wall.
It rises again to about fifteen centimetres per second in the vena cava.

## 6. Where the blood is

Turning to where the blood is, the body's four to six litres are not shared evenly between the vessels at any point in time.
Approximate textbook values put about two-thirds in the systemic veins and venules, and about an eighth in the systemic arteries.
Only about a twentieth is in the capillaries.
Their total area is huge, but each capillary is very short.
About a tenth is in the pulmonary circuit, and the rest is in the heart.
The venous side has more and larger vessels precisely to store those two-thirds of the blood.
Numerous, wide, thin-walled veins are distensible, making them capacitance vessels.
They form a low-pressure reservoir, and sympathetic venoconstriction, a nerve-driven narrowing of the veins, can push their blood back towards the heart.
Venous pressure is below capillary pressure, and that gradient returns the blood.

## 7. How the cardiac output is shared out

Last of all, let's see how the cardiac output is shared out.
With the organs in parallel, it is shared according to need.
In heavy exercise, sympathetic nerves narrow the kidney and splanchnic arterioles, in what is called sympathetic vasoconstriction, and working muscle takes most of the output.
At rest, according to McArdle, the splanchnic circulation receives twenty-five per cent of the cardiac output.
The kidneys get twenty per cent, or one point one litres per minute, and the brain fifteen per cent.
Skeletal muscle gets fifteen to twenty per cent, the skin six per cent, and the heart four to five per cent.
During heavy exercise, splanchnic flow falls sharply, and flow to the kidneys falls to zero point two five litres per minute.
Flow to the brain stays essentially unchanged, whereas skeletal muscle's share rises to between eighty and eighty-five per cent.
Flow to the skin rises, for heat loss, and flow to the heart rises four to fivefold.
Renal blood flow far exceeds the kidneys' own metabolic needs, as its main job is to bring plasma to be filtered into urine.
The kidney cells thus extract only a small fraction of the oxygen the blood carries.
The lecturer says brain flow does not rise in exercise, although McArdle reports a rise of twenty-five to thirty per cent.

## Summary

To recap, the four to six litres of blood cross the pulmonary and systemic circuits in series, which means both ventricles pump the same volume per minute.
The systemic beds, by contrast, sit in parallel, where conductances add, and that keeps resistance low.
Since right atrial pressure is about zero millimetres of mercury, mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance.
Cardiac output equals stroke volume times heart rate.
It follows that heart rate equals mean arterial pressure divided by the product of total peripheral resistance and stroke volume.
Given that velocity equals flow divided by area, blood slows from twenty to zero point zero five centimetres per second in the capillaries.
Their total area is about one thousand square centimetres.
Blood speeds up again to fifteen centimetres per second in the vena cava.
Meanwhile, the veins hold two-thirds of the blood.
At rest, the kidneys get a fifth of the output, which far exceeds their metabolic need.
In exercise, muscle takes eighty to eighty-five per cent, renal and splanchnic flow fall, and brain flow does not rise.
And that's the circulation in outline, from its two circuits to its one equation.
