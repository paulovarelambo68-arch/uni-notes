## Complement and Natural Killer Cells

Besides phagocytosis, the innate branch has two more weapons, complement and natural killer cells.
Complement is a system of blood plasma proteins that a pathogen switches on.
It can be started by three pathways, which all meet at one central protein called C three.
From there, it acts in three ways, namely opsonisation, inflammation and cell lysis.
Natural killer cells, for their part, recognise the body's own virus-infected or cancerous cells and kill them.
The two share a memory hook, because both of these weapons kill by punching holes.
Complement punches them in microbes, while natural killer cells punch them in sick body cells.
We'll look at complement first, and after that at the natural killer cells.

## 1. What complement is

Alongside its cells, the innate branch uses complement, a set of about thirty plasma proteins made mostly by the liver.
These proteins normally circulate in an inactive state.
They become activated at sites of infection, in the presence of a pathogen.
Activation runs as a cascade, in which each activated protein activates the next one, mostly by splitting it.
That way, a small trigger is greatly amplified.
Once activated, the proteins trigger a series of inflammatory events, and C three is the central component.

## 2. Three ways in, one hub, three ways out

Now, C three is the most plentiful complement protein in the plasma.
Three routes, called pathways, can start the cascade, and all of them lead to C three.
First comes the classical pathway, which starts when antibodies stick to a microbe.
Antibodies are proteins made by B cells.
Second is the lectin pathway, which starts when a lectin, a plasma protein, sticks to sugar patterns on a microbe's surface.
And third is the alternative pathway, which starts spontaneously, because a little C three breaks down all the time in the blood.
Fragments that land on a microbe stick to it and set off the cascade.
So all three pathways converge on C three.
C three is then split into a small piece, C three A, and a large piece, C three B.
C three A floats away and drives inflammation.
C three B, on the other hand, sticks to the microbe's surface and does two jobs.
Its first job is opsonisation, which means coating the microbe so that phagocytes grab it more easily.
Its second job is to keep the cascade going into the final stage, known as the terminal pathway.
There, the last complement proteins join up into a pore in the microbe's membrane.
This pore is called the membrane attack complex.
Put simply, there are three ways in, one hub and three ways out.
The three ways out are inflammation, opsonisation and the membrane attack complex.

## 3. The three functions

That brings us to the three functions of complement, which match the three ways out we've just met.
They are opsonisation, inflammation and cell lysis.
The first function, opsonisation, may be the most important.
Activated complement proteins such as C three B and C four B, the large pieces of C three and C four, bind to the pathogen's surface.
Phagocytes have receptors that grip these proteins, so a coated pathogen becomes more amenable to phagocytosis.
Such proteins are called opsonins, because they mark a target for eating.
Moving on, the second function, inflammation, is driven by the small pieces, C three A, C four A and C five A.
These are mediators of inflammation, meaning the chemical signals that set it off.
They bind to receptors on neutrophils, macrophages, mast cells and basophils, and also on endothelial cells, the cells that line blood vessels.
As a result, phagocytes are drawn to the infection, mast cells and basophils release more inflammatory chemicals, and the vessel wall becomes leaky.
The third function, cell lysis, means bursting the pathogen, and that is the job of the membrane attack complex.
So how is the complex built?
It begins with C five B, the large piece of C five.
Next, C six, C seven and C eight join it.
Then several C nine molecules form a ring that passes through the membrane, leaving an open channel into the cell.
Water and ions rush in through this channel, which disrupts the pathogen's fluid balance.
In the end, the pathogen swells and bursts.
In other words, it lyses.

## 4. Natural killer cells

Complement cannot reach a virus hiding inside a body cell, and that is where natural killer cells, or N K cells, come in.
These cells are lymphocytes, making up ten to fifteen per cent of blood lymphocytes.
Even so, they belong to the innate branch, because they act at once, without earlier exposure.
N K cells recognise whether a cell has been invaded by a virus or has become cancerous, because such cells have lost or modified surface structures.
Healthy cells display self markers, which act as a brake on N K cells.
Infected or cancerous cells, by contrast, often lose these markers, which releases the brake.
They may also gain stress molecules that switch N K cells on.
Having recognised its target, the N K cell kills it by releasing two proteins.
Perforin punches holes in the cell membrane.
Granzyme enters through the perforin pore and induces apoptosis, the programmed cell death pathway.
This completes our memory hook, since the membrane attack complex punches holes in microbes, and perforin punches them in infected or cancerous body cells.
Cytotoxic T cells use the same perforin and granzyme.
However, they use them only against cells bearing the one antigen, or foreign molecule, that they are specific for.
Last of all, exercise also changes N K cell activity.

## Summary

To recap, complement is about thirty circulating plasma proteins, activated in the presence of a pathogen.
Its classical, lectin and alternative pathways converge on C three.
C three splits into C three A, which drives inflammation, and C three B, which coats the microbe and leads into the terminal pathway.
Complement's first function is opsonisation by C three B and C four B, and it is possibly the most important.
The second is inflammation, via C three A, C four A and C five A.
The third is lysis by the membrane attack complex, which is made of C five B through to C nine and disrupts fluid balance.
As for N K cells, they are innate lymphocytes, ten to fifteen per cent of blood lymphocytes.
They recognise virus-infected or cancerous cells by their lost or modified surface structures.
They kill them with perforin, which punches holes just like the membrane attack complex, and with granzyme, which induces apoptosis.
And there you have it, complement and natural killer cells, two innate weapons that both kill by punching holes.
