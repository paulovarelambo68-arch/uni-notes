## Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation

Breathing runs on its own, yet it speeds up the moment you start to move and slows again when you stop.
Centres in the brain set the rhythm, and sensors report what the blood needs.
Exercise calls for extra signals from the nerves, and these are what let breathing keep pace with the muscles.
In this note, we'll meet the brainstem centres and the chemical sensors, and then see how breathing is driven through the three phases of exercise.

## 1. Why breathing needs a controller

Breathing is adjusted all the time to match the body's metabolism.
The aim is to keep the oxygen and carbon dioxide pressures in the alveoli nearly constant from rest to hard exercise, which in turn holds the pressures in arterial blood steady.
A thermostat does the same job for a room.
It turns the heating up or down to hold one temperature, however cold it is outside.
The breathing controller turns ventilation up or down to hold the alveolar gases steady, however hard the muscles work.

## 2. The rhythm generators in the medulla

The control centres sit in the brainstem, the lowest part of the brain, just above the spinal cord.
Two parts of it matter, the medulla and the pons.
The medulla is the bottom section, and the pons sits just above it.
The medulla directly controls automatic breathing, the breathing you do without thinking.
It takes input from the pons, and from chemical sensors in the blood and brain.
It also takes input from higher brain centres when breathing is voluntary.
Within the medulla, two groups of neurons share the work.
The dorsal respiratory group fires rhythmic bursts of signals to the diaphragm and the inspiratory rib muscles, and in this way it sets the resting rhythm.
It is the pacemaker of quiet breathing.
The ventral respiratory group holds both inspiratory and expiratory neurons, though it is mainly concerned with expiration.
It stays quiet at rest.
In forceful breathing it becomes active and drives the abdominal wall, the intercostal muscles between the ribs, and the accessory muscles of the neck and chest.
The breathing cycle comes from these neurons switching on and off.
The inspiratory neurons fire, and air flows in.
Then they stop firing, partly because they limit themselves and partly because the expiratory neurons inhibit them, and exhalation begins.
At rest, exhalation is passive, since the stretched lung and chest spring back.
In exercise, the expiratory muscles push the air out, which costs energy.
As expiration goes on, the inhibition fades, and the inspiratory neurons fire again.
In terms of layout, the pons holds two areas, the pneumotaxic and apneustic areas.
The ventral and dorsal respiratory groups lie below them in the medulla oblongata, in a region known as the medullary rhythmicity area.
The pons and the medulla are the two levels of control.
The medulla makes the rhythm, and the pons adjusts it.

## 3. The fine-tuners in the pons

The pons adjusts the rhythm through two centres that pull in opposite directions.
The pneumotaxic centre times the switch from breathing in to breathing out.
It prevents over-inflation of the lungs, promotes expiration, and restrains the apneustic centre.
The apneustic centre acts on the dorsal respiratory group to prolong and deepen inspiration.
It stretches out the ramp of signals to the diaphragm, and the diaphragm keeps contracting for longer as a result.
A ramp is a signal that grows steadily over time.
The balance between the two centres sets how deep each breath is.

## 4. The sensors: chemoreceptors

The medulla needs information to act on.
At rest, the chemical state of the blood largely regulates breathing.
Control of this kind is called humoral, which means carried in the blood.
The sensors are the chemoreceptors.
These structures stimulate breathing when carbon dioxide, acidity or temperature rise.
They stimulate breathing, too, when oxygen or blood pressure fall.
Chemoreceptors come in two sets, central and peripheral.
Of all these signals, the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood is the most important breathing stimulus at rest.
The central chemoreceptors sit in the medulla itself.
They are bathed by the fluid around the brain, the cerebrospinal fluid.
Carbon dioxide crosses easily from the blood into this fluid.
There it reacts with water to make carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions.
The central receptors respond to those hydrogen ions, so they sense carbon dioxide through the acid it makes.
The reaction of carbon dioxide with water is the same one that carries carbon dioxide in blood.
The central receptors, and the respiratory centre they feed, respond to a rise in carbon dioxide but not to a fall in oxygen.
The peripheral chemoreceptors sit in the arteries, beside the arterial pressure sensors, but they sense chemistry rather than pressure.
The carotid bodies lie at the fork of the carotid artery in the neck.
That position lets them monitor arterial blood just before it reaches the brain.
The aortic bodies lie on the aortic arch, the curve of the great artery leaving the heart.
A fall in arterial oxygen raises ventilation only through these peripheral receptors.
They protect against thin air, and they matter most in lung disease and at altitude.
Let's return to carbon dioxide.
When arterial carbon dioxide rises, eighty per cent of the extra ventilation comes from the central receptors, and twenty per cent from the peripheral ones.
The peripheral response is weaker but about five times faster, owing to the carotid bodies sitting directly in the arterial bloodstream.
Signals from the peripheral receptors reach the medulla along the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves.
The medulla raises ventilation, more carbon dioxide is breathed out, and arterial carbon dioxide falls again.
This is negative feedback.
The response removes the signal that caused it, which holds the level steady.

## 5. Why chemistry alone cannot explain exercise breathing

At rest, chemical feedback is enough to explain breathing.
A fall in blood p H signals retained carbon dioxide and carbonic acid.
In response, the inspiratory neurons work harder to blow the carbon dioxide off.
Still, this feedback cannot explain the large rise in breathing during exercise, for three reasons.
First, there is no oxygen signal, given that alveolar oxygen does not fall as exercise gets harder.
In intense exercise, the huge volumes breathed even push alveolar oxygen above one hundred millimetres of mercury, which speeds oxygen loading in the lung.
Second, alveolar oxygen and carbon dioxide pressures stay near resting values across a wide range of intensities, so there is no carbon dioxide signal either.
The receptors see nearly the same blood as at rest, yet ventilation is many times higher.
Third, in light and moderate exercise, ventilation rises in step with oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production.
The match is so close that something must be anticipating the need, rather than correcting an error afterwards.
Consider how the gases behave as the workload rises.
As oxygen consumption climbs, the alveolar partial pressure of oxygen stays near one hundred millimetres of mercury, rising a little at the highest workloads.
Alveolar carbon dioxide stays near forty millimetres of mercury and dips slightly.
Meanwhile, mixed-venous carbon dioxide, the carbon dioxide in blood returning from the whole body, climbs from about forty-five to above seventy-five millimetres of mercury.
The lung keeps the alveolar air steady, even as the returning blood grows richer in carbon dioxide, thanks to ventilation rising to match.

## 6. The nervous drives

The missing signals come from the nervous system, in two forms.
The first is cortical influence.
Outflow from the motor cortex, which commands the muscles, also stimulates the respiratory neurons in the medulla.
The anticipation of exercise stimulates them too.
Together, they start the abrupt rise in breathing on the starting line, before any chemistry has changed.
Another name for this drive is central command.
The second form is peripheral influence.
These are sensory signals from the joints, tendons and muscles as they move, and they keep adjusting ventilation throughout exercise.
Core temperature is not one of the drives.
Except in extreme overheating, a rise in core temperature has little effect on breathing in exercise.
The reason is timing.
Ventilation rises at the start of exercise and falls in recovery far too quickly to follow a temperature that changes over minutes.
The same speed rules out chemistry, since blood takes time to carry carbon dioxide to the receptors, whereas a nerve signal arrives within a second.
Temperature does act strongly on the oxygen dissociation curve, which describes how readily haemoglobin lets go of oxygen.
That, though, is an effect on the blood, not on breathing.

## 7. Putting it together: the three phases

No single factor controls breathing in exercise.
The nervous drives and the chemical feedback act together, and their timing shows in three phases.
Phase one is the abrupt rise at the start, driven by the cortex and by signals from the moving limbs.
Phase two is the gradual climb to a steady level.
It is driven by central command, by the medullary neurons themselves, and by feedback from chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors.
Phase three is the fine tuning of that level by feedback, as temperature, carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions slowly rise.
We can follow these phases in minute ventilation, the total air breathed each minute.
As exercise starts, minute ventilation jumps from about seven to about twenty litres per minute, which is phase one.
It then climbs to a plateau near thirty litres per minute in phase two, and holds there in phase three.
Only part of the response builds slowly, and everything above that slow part is what the nerves add.
At the end of exercise, ventilation falls as fast as it rose, the sign of control by the nerves rather than by chemistry.
When ventilation is divided up by cause, the largest share comes from neural factors, the response to movement.

## 8. Ventilation against work: the ventilatory threshold

How much ventilation rises depends on how hard the work is.
At moderate steady workloads, ventilation rises in a straight line with oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production.
The extra air comes mainly from deeper breaths.
At higher steady workloads, it comes mainly from faster breathing.
The blood still has enough time in the lung capillaries to equalise fully with alveolar air, which is why the arterial gases stay steady.
Above a certain intensity, the straight-line relationship breaks.
The ventilatory threshold is the point in a graded exercise test where ventilation starts to rise faster than oxygen uptake.
A graded test steps the workload up every few minutes until exhaustion.
The threshold is due to the muscles now making lactate by anaerobic metabolism, the energy pathway that runs without oxygen.
Bicarbonate in the blood buffers this lactic acid, soaking up its hydrogen ions.
The buffering releases extra carbon dioxide that must be breathed off.
That extra carbon dioxide explains why ventilation climbs faster than oxygen uptake.
In one example graded test, ventilation keeps its straight-line rise until the ventilatory threshold, near an oxygen uptake of two point seven litres per minute.
Beyond that point, ventilation rises more steeply.
The ventilatory threshold lies close to the lactate threshold, the point where blood lactate starts to climb.
The onset of blood lactate accumulation, or O B L A, marks where lactate passes four millimoles per litre.
Later still comes respiratory compensation, a second and steeper rise in ventilation.
Here, breathing rises further to counter the falling blood p H of intense exercise.

## 9. Training and heat

Two further situations show the controller at work.
After endurance training, ventilation is lower at the same work rate.
The likely reason is that a trained person makes less lactate at a given load.
With less lactate, there is less acid and carbon dioxide to feed back to the chemoreceptors, which leaves less drive to breathe.
Heat does the opposite.
In prolonged submaximal exercise in hot, humid conditions, ventilation drifts upward while arterial carbon dioxide barely changes.
The extra breathing is therefore not a carbon dioxide signal.
Instead, it comes from the rising core temperature acting on the central chemoreceptors.
This is the one setting where temperature matters.
Temperature cannot explain the quick rise at the start of exercise, but after half an hour of heating it adds a slow drift.

## Summary

To recap, the medulla makes the breathing rhythm.
The dorsal respiratory group drives inspiration at rest, and the ventral group adds forceful expiration.
Meanwhile, the pons shapes each breath through its pneumotaxic and apneustic centres.
At rest, the blood's chemistry runs the show, and arterial carbon dioxide pressure is the most important stimulus.
Carbon dioxide is sensed as hydrogen ions by the central chemoreceptors in the medulla.
The peripheral carotid and aortic bodies give only twenty per cent of the carbon dioxide response, yet they respond five times faster.
And they are the only sensors of low oxygen.
In exercise, the alveolar gases stay near resting values while ventilation soars.
Nerves from the motor cortex and the moving limbs therefore drive breathing, and chemistry fine-tunes it.
This gives three phases, an abrupt rise driven by the nerves, a gradual climb to a plateau, and fine tuning by feedback.
Ventilation rises in a straight line with oxygen uptake until the ventilatory threshold.
At that point, bicarbonate buffering of lactate releases extra carbon dioxide.
Training lowers ventilation at a given load, whereas heat makes it drift upward.
And that's how the brainstem, the chemoreceptors and the nerves of moving muscle work together to keep breathing in step with the body's needs.
