## Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow

Because the heart squeezes its own vessels when it contracts, the left ventricle is fed mainly in diastole.
Right coronary flow, though smaller, continues in systole.
Skin blood flow serves body temperature, not the skin's own needs.
In exercise, the vessels of hairy skin widen through sympathetic nerves that release acetylcholine.
In the heat, wide skin vessels can cause fainting or heat exhaustion.
In this note, we'll follow blood through the heart's own vessels, and after that through the skin.

## 1. Coronary supply

We begin with the coronary supply.
Two coronary arteries leave the base of the aorta.
The right coronary artery supplies the right ventricle, the back wall of the left ventricle, and part of the septum, the wall between the ventricles.
The left coronary artery supplies the rest.
Its main branch, the left anterior descending artery, supplies part of the septum.
Cardiac veins drain through the coronary sinus into the right atrium.
Heart muscle removes seventy to eighty per cent of the oxygen from its blood, whereas most tissues remove about a quarter.
The heart can therefore get more oxygen only by increasing its blood flow, which rises up to fourfold in hard exercise.

## 2. Coronary flow through the cardiac cycle

The heart's own vessels run through a wall that squeezes them with every beat.
Two forces therefore set coronary flow.
The first is aortic pressure, the blood pressure inside the aorta.
It pushes blood into the coronary arteries, as they branch off the aorta's base.
Aortic pressure peaks at about one hundred and twenty millimetres of mercury in systole, and falls to about eighty by the end of diastole.
It never falls to zero, thanks to the recoil of the stretched aorta.
The second force is the contracting wall, which presses the vessels shut.
This squeeze, called intramural myocardial pressure, is the stronger force.
Over one heartbeat, the blood supply of the left ventricle goes through three stages.
First, as contraction starts in early systole, the wall squeezes hardest, especially its inner layer, the sub-endocardium, while aortic pressure is at its lowest.
Inflow stops completely.
Second, through the rest of systole, aortic pressure rises and a little blood gets in, but flow stays low.
Third, when the muscle relaxes in diastole, the vessels reopen and blood rushes in.
This is why the left ventricle is fed mainly in diastole.
The right ventricle squeezes far less, which keeps its flow from ever stopping.
Right coronary flow simply follows aortic pressure, and it is smaller than left flow, since the right ventricle has less muscle to feed.
The coronary veins show the mirror image.
The squeeze wrings blood out of them, so coronary sinus outflow surges in systole and falls in diastole.
Flow keeps up with demand in three steps.
To begin with, the heart beats faster and harder, using more oxygen.
Next, the working muscle releases chemicals, especially adenosine, and runs short of oxygen, a state called hypoxia.
Finally, these chemicals and the hypoxia widen the coronary arterioles and let more blood in, which is metabolic vasodilation.
Sympathetic nerves do not dilate the coronary arteries directly.
They only trigger the first step, the faster and harder beating.
With the left ventricle fed in diastole, two things cut its supply.
One is a fast heart rate, which leaves less time in diastole.
The other is a low diastolic pressure, which gives a weaker push and is common in old age.
The resulting ischaemia, too little blood for the muscle's needs, can trigger arrhythmias.

## 3. Skin blood flow is for temperature, not metabolism

Let's turn from the heart to the skin.
Skin is different, as its blood flow serves mainly temperature control.
Its outer layer, the epidermis, has no blood vessels, but the dermis below has many.
In muscle, gut, liver and brain, blood flow follows oxygen use.
That is not so in skin, which needs little oxygen.
In skin, temperature sets the flow instead, and it can vary more than a hundredfold.
At its lowest, it is one millilitre per minute per hundred grams.
It is normally ten to twenty, and it peaks at one hundred and fifty to two hundred.
So a rise in skin flow as the body warms is temperature control, not autoregulation.
Autoregulation means an organ holding its own flow steady when blood pressure changes.
The goal of temperature control is to keep the core, meaning the brain and the chest and abdominal organs, at thirty-seven to thirty-seven and a half degrees Celsius.
The aim is for the skin to lose heat as fast as the core makes it.
Unclothed, a person needs no extra effort to stay warm or cool in air at twenty-seven to twenty-eight degrees Celsius, with skin at about thirty-three degrees.
Blood carries heat to the skin, which loses it in three ways.
In radiation, skin gives off heat in proportion to how much warmer it is than its surroundings.
In conduction-convection, skin warms the air touching it, and air currents carry that air away.
In evaporation of sweat, water absorbs the latent heat of evaporation as it becomes vapour, and this cools the skin.
Blood brings both the water and the heat.

## 4. Two routes through the skin

Blood can cross the skin by two routes.
In the nutritive, or feeding, route, blood flows from an artery in the dermis through arterioles, which control resistance, and into capillaries.
These capillaries form loops under the epidermis, where they exchange gases, nutrients and wastes, and the blood leaves through venules and a vein.
In the thermoregulatory route, shortcuts called arteriovenous anastomoses send blood from arterioles straight into the venous plexus, skipping the capillaries.
This network of veins holds a large volume of blood and passes its heat up to the surface.
Skin comes in two kinds.
Glabrous skin is smooth and hairless.
It covers the fingers, toes, palms, soles, lips, nose and outer ear, which are exposed parts with a large surface for their size.
It has many arteriovenous anastomoses, which are coiled and muscular-walled, allowing them to open and close.
Glabrous skin loses or keeps heat by radiation and conduction-convection.
Non-glabrous, or hairy, skin covers the trunk, limbs and scalp.
It has some arteriovenous anastomoses, but far fewer.
What it has in abundance is sweat glands, and it sweats to lose heat by evaporation and conduction-convection.
Sweat glands deliver up to thirty grams per minute, and the maximum sweat rate is about two to four litres per hour.

## 5. Heat, cold and exercise

Outside heat and cold act directly on skin vessels, especially the arteriovenous anastomoses of glabrous skin.
Heat widens arterioles, venules and small veins, filling the skin with oxygen-rich blood.
More heat is lost as a result, and the face flushes.
Cold narrows these vessels, leaving the skin pale and painful.
During twenty minutes of ice cooling, glabrous skin flow first drops, which is cold-induced vasoconstriction.
Later, it rises again in waves.
This is paradoxical cold vasodilation, which flushes the skin, eases pain and prevents cold injury.
If the cold persists, the narrowing returns.
Cold has two further effects.
In extreme cold, skin flow can fall to one millilitre per minute per hundred grams, leaving the fat under the skin to insulate the core.
Cold on the face narrows skin and limb vessels as well, which strains the heart.
That is why heart attacks are commoner in cold weather.
In exercise, nerves adjust skin flow.
The sympathetic nervous system first narrows skin vessels.
As core temperature rises, warmth receptors in the hypothalamus switch this narrowing to widening.
The hypothalamus is linked to the cardiovascular centre in the brainstem.
Hairy skin widens actively, by sympathetic cholinergic vasodilation.
Its sympathetic nerves release acetylcholine, helped by nitric oxide, and sweating rises too.
This active widening is unusual.
Elsewhere, sympathetic nerves narrow vessels with noradrenaline, but in hairy skin they widen them with acetylcholine.
Glabrous skin, on the other hand, widens passively.
Its sympathetic nerves fire less, which lets its arteriovenous anastomoses relax.

## 6. When skin vasodilation lowers blood pressure

Wide skin vessels lower blood pressure, because mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance.
Take a soldier standing at attention in the heat.
Skin vasodilation causes a large fall in total peripheral resistance.
Blood also pools in the skin and legs, which lowers central venous pressure, the pressure of blood returning to the heart.
The heart fills less and, by the Frank-Starling mechanism, it pumps less.
With cardiac output and total peripheral resistance both down, mean arterial pressure drops.
The soldier faints, despite the baroreflex trying to restore the pressure.
Heavy exercise in the heat causes heat exhaustion in the same way.
Skin and muscle vasodilation both lower total peripheral resistance, while sweating shrinks the plasma volume.
Venous return and cardiac output fall as a result.
With total peripheral resistance and cardiac output both low, mean arterial pressure falls, and the person collapses.
Skin and muscle are supplied side by side, in parallel.
This means they compete for a cardiac output that cannot rise without enough venous return.

## Summary

To recap, the right coronary artery supplies the right ventricle and the back wall of the left ventricle.
The left coronary artery supplies the rest, and the veins drain through the coronary sinus into the right atrium.
Contraction squeezes the coronary vessels, with three results.
Left inflow stops in early systole and happens mainly in diastole, right flow continues in systole but is smaller, and venous outflow surges in systole.
Coronary flow follows the heart's oxygen needs through adenosine and hypoxia, not through direct sympathetic action.
A fast heart rate or a low diastolic pressure endangers the left ventricle, which is fed in diastole.
Skin flow serves temperature, and it ranges from a minimum of one to a maximum of two hundred millilitres per minute per hundred grams.
Glabrous skin uses its many arteriovenous anastomoses, hairy skin sweats, and prolonged cold brings paradoxical vasodilation.
In exercise, the vessels of hairy skin widen through sympathetic nerves releasing acetylcholine, whereas in glabrous skin, widening comes from a drop in sympathetic firing.
In the heat, falling total peripheral resistance and venous return lower blood pressure, since mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance.
The result can be fainting or heat exhaustion.
And that is how the heart feeds its own muscle, and how the skin's blood flow guards the body's temperature.
