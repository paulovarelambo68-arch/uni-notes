## Cytokines

Cytokines are small proteins that cells secrete to signal to themselves or to their neighbours, through specific receptors.
Signalling to the cell itself is called autocrine, and signalling to its neighbours is called paracrine.
Each immune cell's cytokine profile can determine its function.
Acting together as a network, cytokines drive and orchestrate immune responses.
Pro-inflammatory cytokines push inflammation up, whereas anti-inflammatory ones damp it down.
Here, we'll look at what a cytokine is, how these signals are grouped by their effect on inflammation, and how they link up into a network.

## 1. What a cytokine is

First of all, immune cells talk to each other through cytokines.
These are small proteins, between five and twenty kilodaltons in size.
Cytokines are secreted by cells, and they act either on the cell that made them, in an autocrine way, or on cells nearby, in a paracrine way.
Crucially, they only act on cells that carry their specific receptor.
It's important not to confuse cytokines with complement.
Complement is a set of plasma proteins that circulate in an inactive state until a pathogen activates them.
Cytokines, by contrast, are made by cells when needed, and they carry messages between cells.
The cytokines themselves fall into four families.
The first is the interleukins, which run from interleukin one to interleukin twenty-two.
The second is the interferons, namely interferon alpha, beta and gamma.
Third come the tumour necrosis factors, alpha and beta.
And the fourth is transforming growth factor beta.
The names record what each family was first seen to do.
So the interleukins were named for signalling between leukocytes, which are the white blood cells.
Likewise, the interferons were named for interfering with viruses, the tumour necrosis factors for killing tumours, and transforming growth factor beta for altering cell growth.
Whatever the family, each immune cell secretes a characteristic profile of cytokines, and that profile can determine the cell's function.
This makes cytokines important effectors of immune function, meaning that cells do much of their work through them.
They drive and orchestrate immune responses.
Put simply, they switch cells on, call them to a site, and tell them when to stop.

## 2. Pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines

Now, starting and stopping a response are opposite jobs.
For that reason, cytokines are usually grouped by their effect on inflammation.
Inflammation is the local reaction that brings fluid and leukocytes from the blood into injured or infected tissue.
Calling cells to a site falls to the chemokines.
These are cytokines that attract leukocytes to where they are needed.
Among the pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, interleukin six is the odd one out.
It has two faces, one pro-inflammatory and the other anti-inflammatory.
As for T cells, they are a clear example of how the cytokines a cell releases decide what it does.
Regulatory T cells suppress immune responses, and they make the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin ten.
Helper T cells, meanwhile, release cytokines that boost B cells, macrophages and other T cells.

## 3. The cytokine network

That leads on to the bigger picture, which is the cytokine network.
To give one example, macrophages call in neutrophils by releasing interleukin one, interleukin eight and tumour necrosis factor alpha.
The key message is that every cell type both sends and receives many cytokines.
As a result, they form a web rather than a chain.
Within that web, one activated cell can recruit and instruct many others.

## Summary

To recap, cytokines are small proteins of five to twenty kilodaltons that cells secrete.
They act in an autocrine or paracrine manner through specific receptors.
They come in four families, starting with the interleukins from one to twenty-two and the interferons alpha, beta and gamma.
The other two are the tumour necrosis factors alpha and beta, and transforming growth factor beta.
The pro-inflammatory ones are interleukin one, tumour necrosis factor alpha, interferon gamma and, usually, interleukin six.
On the other side, the anti-inflammatory ones are interleukin ten, the interleukin one receptor antagonist and transforming growth factor beta.
Chemokines, for their part, attract cells to a site.
Each cell's cytokine profile can determine its function.
Together, cytokines form a network that drives and orchestrates immune responses.
That's cytokines covered, from a single signal right up to the whole network.
