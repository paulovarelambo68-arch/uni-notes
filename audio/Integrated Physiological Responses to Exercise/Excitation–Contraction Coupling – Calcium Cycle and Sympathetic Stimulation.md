## Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation

Heart muscle contracts when calcium binds troponin C.
This moves tropomyosin out of the way, so that myosin can grab actin and pull.
On every beat, a little calcium enters through L-type calcium channels, supplying twenty-five per cent of what is needed.
That calcium triggers a much bigger release from the cell's store, the sarcoplasmic reticulum, which provides the other seventy-five per cent.
The process is called calcium-induced calcium release.
To relax, the cell clears the calcium away, mostly through a pump called SERCA, which takes about seventy per cent back into the store.
Sympathetic stimulation, through beta one receptors, cyclic A M P and protein kinase A, makes each beat stronger and quicker to relax.
Hypoxia, on the other hand, weakens the beat, because the cell runs short of adenosine triphosphate, or A T P.
In this note, we'll follow that calcium round a single beat, and then see what makes the beat stronger or weaker.

## 1. The contractile proteins

We begin with the contractile proteins themselves.
A heart muscle cell is packed with thin threads of actin and thick threads of myosin, lying side by side.
Small heads on the myosin, called cross-bridges, grab the actin, pull it a short way, let go and grab again, like hands pulling on a rope.
This draws together the zed lines that anchor the ends of the actin, which shortens the cell and produces force, or tension.
The threads slide past each other without getting shorter themselves, which is why this is called the sliding filament mechanism.
Calcium decides when the heads can grab.
At rest, a thin protein called tropomyosin lies along the actin and covers the myosin-binding sites on actin.
Attached to it is a small group of proteins, the troponin complex.
One part of the complex, troponin C, senses calcium.
When calcium binds troponin C, tropomyosin changes shape and shifts aside.
The sites are uncovered, and the heads can grab and pull.
When calcium leaves, the sites are covered again and the muscle relaxes.
Calcium does not just switch contraction on, though.
The amount of calcium sets how strong the contraction is.
In a normal heartbeat, there is only enough calcium to fill some of the troponin C molecules.
That leaves only some of the binding sites on actin uncovered, with only some myosin heads able to pull at the same time.
The force of a contraction depends on how many heads are pulling together, just as a rope is pulled harder when more people grab it.
More calcium therefore means more troponin C filled, more binding sites uncovered, more myosin heads pulling at once, and a stronger contraction.
Given that troponin C is far from full in a normal beat, the heart has plenty of room to strengthen its beat this way.
This settles a common exam trap.
Myosin binds actin, not tropomyosin, which only covers the binding sites.
Three other statements about cardiac contraction are true.
One is that it is all-or-none, which comes from the heart's cells being connected electrically, so all of them contract on every beat.
Another is that it increases active tension, which just means the force the muscle makes by contracting.
The third is that it is strengthened by more calcium entering from outside the cell, as the next section explains.
Since every cell already contracts on every beat, the heart cannot make a beat stronger by using more cells.
Your arm muscles can do that, bringing in extra fibres to lift something heavier.
The heart has two other ways to strengthen its beat.
It can be filled and stretched more before it contracts, which is Starling's law.
Or it can contract more strongly from the same stretch, which is a rise in contractility.
This note covers the calcium machinery behind contractility, and the note on the pressure-volume loop explains both ideas in full.

## 2. The calcium cycle during systole

Now let's follow calcium through systole, the contraction phase of the heartbeat, when the action potential has to get calcium onto troponin C.
This link between the electrical signal and contraction is called excitation-contraction coupling.
It uses calcium from two sources, the fluid outside the cell and a store inside the cell called the sarcoplasmic reticulum, or S R.
On every beat, the steps run in the same order.
First, the action potential spreads along the cell membrane and down the T tubules, tunnels of membrane that carry it deep into the cell.
It depolarises the membrane to threshold.
Next, L-type calcium channels open, the L standing for long-lasting, and calcium flows in.
On its own, this calcium is not enough to cause contraction, at only about twenty-five per cent of what is needed.
Rather, it acts as a trigger, opening the release channels of the S R, which are the ryanodine receptors.
These receptors sit right next to the T tubules, at a contact point called the diad.
At the diad, each L-type channel faces about ten ryanodine receptors, which is how a little calcium opens many of them.
In response, the S R releases the other seventy-five per cent, and the calcium level in the cell rises tenfold to a hundredfold.
At this point, calcium binds troponin C, tropomyosin moves, actin and myosin interact, and the muscle contracts.
Everything from the calcium entry through the L-type channels to the release from the S R is called calcium-induced calcium release.
A small calcium entry unlocks a much larger store, like a small key opening a big door.
After that, release stops, perhaps partly due to the high calcium level shutting the ryanodine receptors.
The trigger calcium enters during the plateau of the action potential.
That gives us the key rule.
More calcium entering during the plateau means more release from the S R, and a stronger contraction.
The professor flagged this calcium cycle, which has two halves.
One half is the systole sequence we have been following.
The other half is relaxation, when calcium is pumped back into the S R and out of the cell.
Pumping it back into the S R refills the store for the next beat, and that refilling is what restitution means.
The message is that calcium goes round a cycle on every beat.

## 3. Relaxation: three ways to remove calcium

Relaxation, the other half of the cycle, is what lets the heart refill with blood.
For the muscle to relax, the calcium must come off troponin C.
At the end of contraction, calcium stops entering, and the S R is no longer stimulated to release it.
The calcium already in the cell must be removed quickly, and there are three ways of doing it.
The main route takes most of it, about seventy per cent in humans, back into the S R.
The pump that does this is SERCA, the calcium pump of the S R, which runs on A T P.
Its full name is the sarco-endoplasmic reticulum calcium A T P ase.
Most of the rest is pushed out of the cell by the sodium-calcium exchanger in the outer membrane.
A small amount is pumped out by the cell membrane calcium pump, a calcium A T P ase, which, like SERCA, runs on A T P.
SERCA has a brake, a small protein called phospholamban.
While phospholamban is dephosphorylated, meaning it carries no phosphate tag, it slows calcium uptake into the S R.
We'll see in the next section what happens when a tag is added.
The sodium-calcium exchanger works differently, using no A T P.
Instead, it is powered by the sodium gradient.
There is far more sodium outside the cell than inside, so sodium flows in through the exchanger, three ions at a time.
Each time, it pushes one calcium ion out.
The gradient is kept up by the sodium-potassium pump, which uses A T P to pump sodium out of the cell.
It follows that if the sodium-potassium pump is blocked, sodium builds up inside, the exchanger slows, and calcium stays in the cell.
Digoxin works in exactly this way, and we'll meet it again at the end of the note.

## 4. Sympathetic stimulation: more force, faster, and faster relaxation

Let's bring in the sympathetic nervous system, which readies the body for action.
It speeds up both halves of this cycle, and a single contraction shows this.
Take a strip of heart muscle, held at a fixed length so that its contraction is isometric.
Its force is recorded over time, from the moment it is stimulated.
A normal contraction, the control, is compared with a contraction after adrenaline, that is, after beta-adrenergic stimulation.
Both times, the muscle starts from the same stretch, or preload.
After adrenaline, the contraction changes in three ways.
It peaks higher, at about ten grams instead of six, which is an increase in active tension.
It rises more steeply, which is an increase in the rate of tension development.
It falls more steeply and ends sooner, which is an increase in the rate of relaxation.
As the starting stretch was the same, the stronger beat is not Starling's law at work, but a true rise in contractility.
How does adrenaline do this?
The answer is a cascade that the professor would ask medical students to memorise.
One rule explains it.
Protein kinase means phosphorylation.
A protein kinase is an enzyme that sticks a phosphate tag, taken from A T P, onto other proteins.
The tag switches those proteins on or off, and every effect in the cascade is one of these tags.
The cascade begins when a catecholamine binds the beta one receptor on the cell membrane.
That catecholamine is either noradrenaline from sympathetic nerves, or adrenaline from the adrenal glands.
The receptor switches on adenylate cyclase, a membrane enzyme that turns A T P into cyclic A M P, a messenger inside the cell.
Cyclic A M P, in turn, activates protein kinase A.
From there, protein kinase A phosphorylates the L-type calcium channel, which then lets in more calcium each time it opens.
As a result, with each action potential, more trigger calcium enters, more is released from the S R, and more reaches troponin C and the threads.
This extra calcium increases the force of contraction, which is the positive inotropic effect.
Inotropic means affecting the force of contraction.
Protein kinase A has two other targets, phospholamban, which sits on SERCA, and troponin I.
The cell membrane holds the three transporters from the previous section, the sodium-calcium exchanger, the membrane calcium pump and the sodium-potassium pump.
The cardiac glycosides, the drug family that includes digoxin, act on the sodium-potassium pump.
Phospholamban and troponin I, the two other targets of protein kinase A, both speed up relaxation.
Phosphorylating phospholamban reduces its brake on SERCA.
Calcium is pumped back into the S R faster, and the muscle relaxes faster, which is the lusitropic effect.
Lusitropic means affecting relaxation.
Beyond speeding relaxation, phosphorylating phospholamban loads the S R with more calcium for the next contractions.
The next beats are stronger and build force faster as a result.
The lecture puts this by saying that phospholamban phosphorylation promotes the rate of contraction.
Phosphorylating troponin I, the inhibitory part of the troponin complex, makes troponin C hold calcium less tightly.
In the lecture's words, this is inhibition of calcium binding to troponin C.
Calcium still binds, which means the muscle still contracts.
The calcium simply lets go sooner, which makes relaxation faster.
The same pathway, through cyclic A M P and protein kinase A, also raises the heart rate, by acting on the heart's pacemaker, the sinoatrial node.

## 5. Inotropic agents and the mechanism of hypoxia

Last of all come the inotropic agents.
Every agent on the lecture's list acts on this calcium machinery.
Agents that raise contractility have a positive inotropic effect, and agents that lower it have a negative inotropic effect.
We'll include two changes that exams list beside the agents, a faster heart rate and a larger venous return.
Noradrenaline and adrenaline have a positive effect on contractility, since more calcium enters and more is stored in the S R, as the cascade showed.
Raised extracellular calcium is positive too, as more calcium outside means more enters with each action potential.
Digoxin is positive as well, and it works by blocking the sodium-potassium pump.
Sodium builds up inside, the sodium-calcium exchanger slows, and more calcium stays in the cell.
A faster heart rate is positive, with calcium entering more often than it can be removed.
The S R loads up, and each beat gets a little stronger, which is the staircase, or treppe, effect.
A larger venous return, meaning more blood filling the heart, has no effect on contractility.
The only reason the beat is stronger is that the muscle is stretched more, which is Starling's law, not contractility.
Hypoxia, or too little oxygen, is negative, and we'll come to why in a moment.
With calcium-channel blockers, less calcium enters through the L-type channels, making their effect negative.
Beta-blockers are negative, blocking the beta one receptor and leaving the cascade from the previous section unable to start.
The professor asks how hypoxia lowers contractility, and the answer comes down to energy.
Heart muscle makes its A T P using oxygen, and can make only a little A T P without it.
A hypoxic cell therefore soon runs short.
Everything that uses A T P slows down.
The myosin heads cycle more slowly.
SERCA refills the S R less, leaving relaxation sluggish and less calcium ready for the next beat.
On top of that, waste builds up.
Making A T P without oxygen produces acid, in the form of hydrogen ions, while the breakdown of energy stores releases phosphate.
The hydrogen ions compete with calcium for troponin C, and the phosphate weakens the pull of each myosin head.
The upshot is that the muscle makes less force from the same calcium and the same stretch, which is exactly what a fall in contractility means.
It is also why hypoxia counts as a negative inotrope.

## Summary

To sum up, heart muscle shortens by the sliding filament mechanism, and calcium binding troponin C is the switch that lets myosin grab actin.
On each beat, a little calcium, twenty-five per cent of what is needed, enters through L-type channels.
It triggers a large release of the remaining seventy-five per cent from the S R, called calcium-induced calcium release.
It follows that the more calcium enters during the plateau, the stronger the beat.
During relaxation, SERCA, held back by phospholamban, pumps about seventy per cent back into the S R.
The sodium-calcium exchanger and the membrane calcium pump remove the rest.
Sympathetic stimulation works through the beta one receptor, cyclic A M P and protein kinase A.
Protein kinase A phosphorylates the L-type channel, giving a stronger beat, which is the positive inotropic effect.
It phosphorylates phospholamban too, for faster relaxation, the lusitropic effect.
And by phosphorylating troponin I, it makes calcium let go sooner.
Adrenaline, raised calcium, digoxin and a faster heart rate all raise contractility.
A larger venous return strengthens the beat only through Starling's law.
Hypoxia lowers contractility, because A T P runs short, and acid and phosphate blunt the response to calcium.
That's the calcium cycle behind every heartbeat, along with what makes each beat stronger or weaker.
