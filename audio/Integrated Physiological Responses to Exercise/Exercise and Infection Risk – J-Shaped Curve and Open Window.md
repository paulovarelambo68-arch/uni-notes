## Exercise and Infection Risk – J-Shaped Curve and Open Window

Exercise acts on the whole body partly by modulating the immune system, and two classic theories ask whether that helps or harms.
The J-shaped curve says that moderate exercise lowers the risk of upper respiratory tract infection below average.
Very high workloads, according to the same curve, raise it above average.
The open window hypothesis says that prolonged exhaustive exercise depresses immunity for three to seventy-two hours.
This is most consistently seen as a fall in salivary I G A.
The curve remains debated, partly because only about a third of athletes' upper respiratory symptoms are infections.

## 1. Why exercise immunology exists

Exercise immunology began in nineteen oh two, when transient leukocytosis was observed in marathon runners.
Transient leukocytosis is a temporary rise in the white blood cell count.
Many studies since then show that exercise influences the differentiation, trafficking and function of immune cells.
The same goes for inflammatory mediators, the chemical signals of inflammation.
Hence the lecture's central statement.
A central pathway by which exercise exerts systemic, or whole-body, effects is through modulation of the immune system.
Historically, two theories have asked whether this modulation helps or harms.
The J-shaped curve is about the dose of exercise, whereas the open window hypothesis is about the hours after one hard bout.

## 2. The J-shaped curve

The first theory is Nieman's J-shaped curve, from nineteen ninety-four and nineteen ninety-five.
It relates the amount and intensity of exercise to the risk of upper respiratory tract infection, or U R T I.
That means infection of the nose, throat and upper airways, such as a cold.
The model links this risk to immunosurveillance, the immune system's continuous patrol for pathogens and abnormal cells.
Moderate workloads are associated with improved immunosurveillance and a decreased U R T I risk.
Unusually high workloads, in contrast, go with decreased immunosurveillance and an increased risk.

## 3. The evidence for the left arm: moderate activity

The left arm of the J reflects how consistent moderate-intensity training improves immunosurveillance and reduces inflammation.
It brings an increase in natural killer, or N K, cell activity.
It also brings a diminished age-related decrease in T cell function, so these adaptive lymphocytes decline less with age.
In practice, near-daily brisk walking, for forty-five minutes per session, was associated with significantly fewer days with U R T I symptoms.
This came from two studies of one hundred and twenty-six overweight women, randomised to exercise and non-exercise groups.
Combined, the studies show the walkers with about five and a half symptom days, against about ten for the sedentary women, roughly half.
The spreads around the two averages do not overlap, which is consistent with a significant difference.

## 4. The evidence for the right arm: intense training

The right arm comes from athletes, who experience a high incidence of U R T I during intense training, even for a short time.
Incidence, the share newly infected, exceeded forty per cent in three groups.
The first was competitive swimmers over four weeks of intense training, and the second was elite hockey players at a ten-day training camp.
The third was elite squash athletes over ten weeks of training.
In more than five hundred long-distance runners, there was a dose-response relationship between yearly training distance and U R T I risk.
This means the more of the exposure, the more of the effect.
Even so, not all studies support the J-shaped curve.
Many variables can influence susceptibility to infection, which makes it a complex issue.

## 5. The open window hypothesis

The second theory, the open window hypothesis, concerns the hours after one hard bout.
It holds that prolonged exhaustive physical activity can severely reduce the immune response to infection for three to seventy-two hours.
This is an open window, through which a pathogen can get in.
Within that window, the most consistent observation is decreased salivary secretion of I G A, the antibody of secretions.
This I G A stops inhaled microbes from entering the lining of the mouth and airways.
Less of it therefore weakens the first line of defence against upper respiratory tract infections.
There are changes, too, in the numbers of circulating immune cells and in the functional capacity of white blood cells.
Many studies were done in ultra-marathon runners, so the evidence comes from extreme exertion.
Plotted over time, immune function after moderate activity rises slightly and returns to its resting baseline.
After marathon-type exertion, it rises higher, plunges below baseline into the open window, and climbs back in steps.
Exhaustive exercise thus buys a bigger boost at the price of suppression, which moderate exercise avoids.
The length of that dip depends on the source.
The hypothesis and the lecture's summary slide give three to seventy-two hours.
The leukocyte data, however, put the deepest lymphocyte fall at two to six hours after exercise, and say the window lasts no longer than twenty-four hours.
Both figures can appear in an exam answer.

## 6. Infection or inflammation?

Both arms of the J rest on counted symptoms, but are those symptoms really infections?
A two thousand and eleven position statement divides their causes, or aetiology, in elite athletes into thirds.
Only about a third are infections, mostly viral, with bacteria behind about five per cent of cases.
Another third are identifiable inflammatory diseases with no infection behind them, and the cause of the last third is unknown.
Many of the colds counted in athlete studies are therefore airway inflammation rather than infection.
That is one reason the J-shaped curve is debated.

## Summary

To recap, exercise immunology began with transient leukocytosis in marathon runners in nineteen oh two.
Immune modulation is a central pathway of exercise's systemic effects.
Nieman's J-shaped curve puts sedentary people at average U R T I risk.
Moderate exercise brings the risk below average, through better immunosurveillance.
That shows up as more N K activity, and half the symptom days in brisk walkers.
Very high workloads raise the risk above average, with over forty per cent incidence in intense training, and a dose-response with distance.
The open window hypothesis says that exhaustive exercise reduces the immune response for three to seventy-two hours, or at most twenty-four hours by the leukocyte data.
It does so most consistently by lowering salivary I G A.
The curve remains debated, because not all studies support it, and only about a third of athletes' upper respiratory symptoms are infections.
And those are the two classic theories, the J-shaped curve and the open window.
