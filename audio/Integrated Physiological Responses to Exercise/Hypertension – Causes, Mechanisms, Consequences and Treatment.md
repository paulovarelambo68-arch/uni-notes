## Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment

Hypertension is a chronic, usually progressive rise in arterial pressure.
It is diagnosed when repeated resting readings exceed one hundred and forty over ninety millimetres of mercury under the age of fifty.
For people over fifty, the line is one hundred and sixty over ninety-five.
About ten per cent of cases are secondary, with a findable cause, and ninety per cent are essential.
Whatever starts it, a raised resistance in the small arteries holds the pressure up, and the kidney comes to need that pressure to excrete the day's salt.
Untreated, it silently damages the heart, brain and kidneys.
That is why treatment aims at a systolic pressure of one hundred and thirty-five to one hundred and forty-five, and a diastolic pressure of eighty-five.
In this note, we'll see what counts as hypertension, what causes it and keeps it going, what it does to the body, and how it is treated.

## 1. What counts as hypertension

We begin with what counts as hypertension.
Clinical hypertension is a chronic, usually progressive, raised arterial pressure, and the dividing line between normal and raised pressure is difficult to choose.
Most physicians diagnose it when repeated resting measurements exceed one hundred and forty over ninety millimetres of mercury under fifty years of age.
Over fifty, the threshold is one hundred and sixty over ninety-five.
Just below that is not safe either.
A high-normal pressure doubles the ten-year risk of cardiovascular disease.
High-normal means a systolic pressure of one hundred and thirty to one hundred and thirty-nine, and a diastolic pressure of eighty-five to eighty-nine.
Secondary hypertension, about ten per cent of cases, has an identifiable pathological cause.
Essential, or primary, hypertension makes up the other ninety per cent and has no obvious organic cause.

## 2. Secondary hypertension

The most common secondary cause is pre-eclamptic toxaemia, the hypertension of pregnancy.
It affects seven to ten per cent of pregnancies, and it remits on delivery.
Three other causes are uncommon.
Hyperaldosteronism, or Conn's syndrome, is an excess of aldosterone, the hormone that tells the kidney to keep sodium.
Its hypertension comes from excessive salt and water retention.
Renal artery stenosis, a narrowed artery to one kidney, raises pressure by activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.
A phaeochromocytoma is a tumour that secretes catecholamines.
Its adrenaline and noradrenaline raise pressure through alpha adrenoceptors, the receptors that constrict small arteries.

## 3. How extra fluid becomes high pressure

How, then, does keeping salt and water raise the pressure?
Salt holds water with it, so retaining sodium expands the fluid outside the cells, the plasma and the blood volume.
The fuller circulation drives more blood back to the heart.
The ventricle fills more and, by Starling's law, ejects a larger stroke volume, the volume pumped per beat.
Stroke volume times heart rate is the cardiac output.
Mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance, which is the resistance of all the small arteries together.
A larger output therefore raises mean arterial pressure.
The organs themselves add the rest.
Each organ keeps its own blood flow steady, which means the extra flow makes its arterioles constrict.
Constriction in many organs at once raises total peripheral resistance, and mean arterial pressure rises further.
In short, hypertension started by an increase in fluid volume is maintained by an increase in total peripheral resistance.

## 4. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system

Now for the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, which renal artery stenosis activates to raise pressure.
A narrowed renal artery is modelled in rodents by clipping one renal artery and leaving the other kidney alone.
The clip narrows the artery, and less blood reaches that kidney.
The kidney cannot tell this from a real fall in blood pressure, and it responds as if pressure were low.
Its juxtaglomerular cells, which are flow sensors in the walls of its small arteries, release the enzyme renin into the blood.
Renin cuts angiotensinogen, an inactive protein made by the liver, into angiotensin one.
Angiotensin-converting enzyme, or A C E, is found mainly in the lungs, and it cuts angiotensin one into angiotensin two, the active hormone.
Angiotensin two raises pressure in two ways.
Quickly, as a powerful vasoconstrictor, it narrows small arteries throughout the body, raising total peripheral resistance and with it mean arterial pressure.
More slowly, it makes the adrenal cortex secrete aldosterone.
The adrenal cortex is the outer layer of the adrenal glands, which sit above the kidneys.
Aldosterone tells the kidney tubules to reabsorb more sodium.
Water follows the sodium, so blood volume rises, and mean arterial pressure climbs further through the fluid chain we followed earlier.
This is why captopril, a drug for high blood pressure, works.
It blocks angiotensin-converting enzyme, which means that less angiotensin two and less aldosterone are made.
The small arteries relax, the kidneys keep less salt and water, and blood pressure falls.

## 5. Essential hypertension: benign, malignant, young and old

Most patients, though, have essential hypertension, which takes one of two courses.
Benign essential hypertension is the commonest.
It is slowly progressive and almost symptomless, and it is usually found at a routine check-up.
The rarer malignant hypertension is rapidly progressive.
It quickly causes cardiac failure with oedema, renal damage, and cerebral damage with papilloedema and retinopathy.
Papilloedema is a swollen optic disc, and retinopathy here means damaged retinal vessels.
The pattern also changes with age.
Compare patients aged seventeen to twenty-nine with patients aged sixty to sixty-nine, at rest and as exercise gets harder and oxygen uptake rises.
At rest, both groups have a high mean arterial pressure, but they reach it differently.
The young have a raised heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output, whereas in the old a raised peripheral resistance maintains the pressure.
In exercise, the young raise their stroke volume and heart rate, drop their resistance, and raise mean arterial pressure moderately.
The old raise stroke volume and heart rate less, and drop resistance less, and their mean arterial pressure rises markedly.
That is because stiff, narrowed arterioles cannot dilate freely.

## 6. What causes essential hypertension

Two theories explain what causes essential hypertension.
The first is neurogenic, blaming an over-active sympathetic nervous system.
In the young, it drives the heart, through the beta one adrenoceptors that speed and strengthen it.
This gives tachycardia, a fast heart rate, along with a raised stroke volume and a raised cardiac output.
In the old, it drives the vessels instead, causing peripheral vasoconstriction through alpha adrenoceptors.
This vasoconstriction comes with down-regulation of the beta two adrenoceptors through which adrenaline dilates those vessels.
Vasodilators reverse that constriction at first.
Persistent constriction, however, thickens and permanently narrows the walls of small arteries and arterioles, until the raised resistance no longer answers vasodilators.
Some of those vessels can be destroyed by the high pressure, a loss called rarefaction, which raises resistance further.
The causes of essential hypertension fall into two groups.
The first group is inherited predispositions.
These are sympathetic over-reactivity, an exaggerated pressure response from abnormal ion movement across cell membranes, and weak vasodilator, or depressor, systems.
Another is the metabolic syndrome of insulin resistance, obesity and abnormal blood fats, and family history belongs here too.
The second group is repeated environmental influences.
These are psychological stress, a high intake of salt, which is sodium chloride, physical inactivity with a high calorie intake, chronic alcohol intake, and smoking tobacco.
Both groups act through exaggerated sympathetic defence reactions.
These reactions constrict the kidney's vessels, and they fix the hypertension by remodelling the resistance vessels.
Cardiac hypertrophy belongs to neither group, being a consequence of hypertension, not a cause.
The second theory is faulty sodium handling, which explains why the pressure stays raised.
The most important mechanism for long-term pressure control is the renal body fluid system.
The kidney adjusts the body's fluid volume until output matches intake.
Its equilibrium point is the arterial pressure at which the intake of water and sodium equals the output.
This shows in renal output curves, which relate salt intake and output, in multiples of a normal daily intake, to arterial pressure.
The normal curve rises almost vertically near one hundred millimetres of mercury.
That is because a high arterial pressure produces a high output of salt and water, which is pressure diuresis.
On the normal curve, both a normal and a high salt intake are balanced near one hundred millimetres of mercury.
In hypertensives, whether salt-sensitive or salt-insensitive, the curve shifts to the right, towards higher pressures.
The reason is that renal vessels, remodelled as the pressure develops chronically, reduce pressure diuresis.
The same intakes are now balanced only at higher pressures, about one hundred and forty millimetres of mercury for a normal intake.
In other words, the hypertension is required to excrete sodium and water normally.
This is why the baroreflex cannot cure it.
Below that equilibrium point, the kidney retains fluid until the pressure returns.

## 7. What hypertension does

What makes hypertension dangerous is that it is virtually symptomless.
Contrary to popular belief, it rarely presents with nosebleeds or headaches.
Untreated, it damages the heart, brain and kidneys.
It causes cardiac hypertrophy, hypertensive heart failure, and atheromatous coronary artery disease, meaning fatty plaques in the heart's arteries.
Strokes, hypertensive retinopathy and chronic renal failure complete the list.
The outlook is grave.
About fifty per cent of untreated patients eventually develop heart failure, twenty-five per cent renal failure, and twenty-five per cent cerebral complications.
Hypertension must therefore be treated at the symptomless stage.

## 8. Ventricular hypertrophy

The first consequence of hypertension, cardiac hypertrophy, follows directly.
It occurs in response to the increased afterload, the pressure the ventricle must push against to eject.
The heart does more work, and its walls grow thicker.
The wall thickens without any increase in chamber size, growing inwards, which is concentric hypertrophy.
In endurance athletes, the thickening comes with a larger chamber instead, and this is called eccentric hypertrophy.
The hypertensive wall becomes stiffer and loses distensibility, which impairs relaxation and reduces diastolic filling.
That wall is also short of oxygen.
It is built of extracellular matrix and cardiac myocytes, which are the muscle cells.
The myocytes grow more in diameter than in length, while the capillaries stay unchanged in number and diameter.
As a result, the capillaries lie further apart, and the ratio of capillary surface area to myocyte volume falls.
Each cell gets less oxygen.
On the electrocardiogram, the thickened left ventricle gives tall R waves in leads V five, V six and A V L, and deep S waves in V one and V two.
The reason is that more electrical activity travels towards the leads that face the left.

## 9. Treatment

Since the damage is silent and the outlook grave, treatment starts early.
It aims to reduce systolic pressure to one hundred and thirty-five to one hundred and forty-five millimetres of mercury, and diastolic pressure to eighty-five.
This greatly reduces mortality and morbidity.
Four avenues do it, each attacking one side of the equation in which mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance.
The first avenue reduces total peripheral resistance with peripheral vasodilators.
These are calcium channel blockers, such as nifedipine, and alpha one adrenoceptor blockers, such as prazosin.
The second avenue uses diuretics to reduce the extracellular fluid volume, which is raised in hypertension.
Diuretics lower cardiac output by lowering blood volume.
The third avenue inhibits angiotensin-converting enzyme, with A C E inhibitors such as captopril.
These lower both total peripheral resistance and cardiac output, as less angiotensin two means less vascular tone, and less aldosterone means less fluid volume.
The fourth avenue reduces cardiac output with beta adrenoceptor blockers, such as propranolol and atenolol.

## Summary

To recap, hypertension means repeated resting pressures above one hundred and forty over ninety millimetres of mercury under the age of fifty.
Over fifty, the threshold is one hundred and sixty over ninety-five.
Ten per cent is secondary, with a findable cause such as pre-eclampsia, Conn's syndrome, renal artery stenosis or a phaeochromocytoma, and ninety per cent is essential.
Retained salt and water raise blood volume, stroke volume, cardiac output and pressure.
The organs then autoregulate by constricting, and a raised total peripheral resistance maintains the hypertension.
With a narrowed renal artery, the same job is done by a continuously activated renin-angiotensin system, acting through angiotensin two.
Essential hypertension is usually benign, slow and symptomless.
In the young, it is driven by a high cardiac output, and in the old by a high peripheral resistance, made permanent by vessel wall thickening and rarefaction.
The kidney holds the pressure there, as its renal output curve is shifted to the right, towards higher pressures.
A high pressure is needed to excrete a normal salt load.
Untreated, fifty per cent of patients develop heart failure, twenty-five per cent renal failure, and twenty-five per cent cerebral complications.
Meanwhile, the pressure-loaded ventricle undergoes concentric hypertrophy.
Its wall grows thicker and stiffer, its chamber stays the same size, and its capillaries lie too far apart to supply the enlarged cells.
Treatment aims at a systolic pressure of one hundred and thirty-five to one hundred and forty-five, and a diastolic pressure of eighty-five.
It uses vasodilators, diuretics, A C E inhibitors and beta blockers.
And that is how hypertension starts, why it stays, and how it is brought down.
