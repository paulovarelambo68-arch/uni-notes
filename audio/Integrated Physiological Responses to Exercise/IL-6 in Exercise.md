## IL-6 in Exercise

Interleukin six has two sources.
The first is immune cells in infection or injury, where it is usually pro-inflammatory.
The second is contracting skeletal muscle, which makes it a myokine.
In exercise, it rises up to a hundredfold after a marathon, more than any other cytokine.
At high intensity, lactate drives its release.
Short pulses of interleukin six from muscle are metabolic and anti-inflammatory, and they mobilise N K cells, neutrophils and lymphocytes.
Chronically elevated interleukin six, however, is harmful, and that is the interleukin six paradox.

## 1. Two sources, two faces

Interleukin six is one of the cytokines, and a well-established mechanism of exercise-induced immunomodulation.
That means it is a main route by which exercise alters immunity.
Several immune cells produce it, particularly in response to infection or injury.
There, it is usually thought of as a pro-inflammatory cytokine.
Exercise adds a second source, because contracting skeletal muscle produces interleukin six in high levels.
This makes it a myokine, a cytokine made and released by muscle.
The interleukin six gene is silent in resting muscle, but rapidly activated in exercise.
The size of the increase depends on exercise intensity, duration, the muscle mass involved, and endurance, meaning training status.
Intensity matters, because in high-intensity exercise, lactate causes an increase in interleukin six secretion.
Lactate generated during strenuous cycling tracked closely with an approximately fivefold increase in interleukin six.
In adults with obesity doing a single bout of cycling at eighty per cent of V O two peak, interleukin six rose roughly one point three fold.
That rise correlated with a roughly three point eight fold rise in lactate.
Whether lactate drives interleukin six release in lower-intensity exercise is unknown.

## 2. How much, and in what order

The muscle source is so large that interleukin six increases up to a hundredfold following a marathon.
That makes it the cytokine with the most significant rise in plasma during prolonged, intense endurance exercise.
In data from Pedersen and Toft, published in two thousand, interleukin six rises first and towers over the rest.
It peaks at about the end of exercise, and the interleukin one receptor antagonist peaks after it.
This antagonist blocks the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin one.
The chemokines interleukin eight and M I P one beta, cytokines that attract white cells, rise modestly.
Tumour necrosis factor alpha, interleukin one, and the soluble receptor that mops up tumour necrosis factor alpha all stay lowest.
So, unlike an infection, exercise produces interleukin six without a large wave of the pro-inflammatory pair, tumour necrosis factor alpha and interleukin one.
And the interleukin six peak is followed by anti-inflammatory signals.

## 3. Metabolic effects in acute exercise

In acute exercise, meaning a single bout, this muscle interleukin six has anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects.
Metabolically, it enhances glucose uptake by muscle.
It enhances fat oxidation too, to increase fat's availability as an energy source.
In this way, it acts as a metabolic regulator.
According to Petersen and Pedersen, writing in two thousand and five, contraction switches on interleukin six M R N A in the muscle fibre.
The released interleukin six then acts in several places.
In adipose tissue, it drives lipolysis, the breakdown of stored fat.
In the liver, it drives glycogen breakdown to glucose.
Back in the muscle, it drives fat oxidation.
And in the blood, tumour necrosis factor falls, while the interleukin one receptor antagonist, interleukin ten and soluble tumour necrosis factor receptor rise.
The message is that one muscle signal releases fuel from fat and liver, while tilting the blood towards an anti-inflammatory state.
Blood glucose benefits as well.
Acute elevations in interleukin six, and training, stimulate secretion of glucagon-like peptide one, a gut hormone that boosts insulin release.
It comes from intestinal cells and pancreatic alpha cells, and it improves insulin secretion and glycaemic control, that is, blood glucose control.
All these metabolic effects can indirectly support immune cell energy needs.

## 4. Immune effects of exercise-induced IL-6

Interleukin six also acts on immune cells, first by moving them into the blood.
It mobilises natural killer, or N K, cells.
Interleukin six dependent recruitment of N K cells into tumours supports anti-tumour immunity in B sixteen melanoma and four T one breast cancer.
Interleukin six also stimulates the mobilisation of neutrophils and lymphocytes into the circulation, which supports immune surveillance, patrolling for pathogens and abnormal cells.
The second effect is anti-inflammatory in acute exercise.
Interleukin six stimulates production of interleukin ten and the interleukin one receptor antagonist, which are anti-inflammatory cytokines.
At the same time, it decreases production of the pro-inflammatory tumour necrosis factor alpha.
The third effect reaches the liver, where interleukin six triggers production of acute-phase proteins.
These are plasma proteins that surge after infection or injury, such as C-reactive protein, the inflammation marker.
This is useful for the minor damage caused by exercise.

## 5. The IL-6 paradox

These benefits come from short pulses, and the reversal when interleukin six stays high is the interleukin six paradox.
Chronically elevated circulating interleukin six is associated with cardiometabolic disease, heightened autoimmunity and inflammation, cancer progression and increased all-cause mortality.
Such chronic disease involves sustained release from inflamed tissues, including visceral fat, the abdominal fat inflamed in obesity.
The same can happen with relentless hard training.
Interleukin six produced under chronic high-intensity exercise tends to be pro-inflammatory, and can contribute to inflammation, because it uses a different signalling mechanism.
Put simply, brief muscle pulses of interleukin six signal in a way that does not switch on tumour necrosis factor alpha and interleukin one.
Interleukin six that stays high, by contrast, is released by immune cells and inflamed tissue, and signals in a way that amplifies inflammation.
The rule to remember is that short-lived, muscle-derived pulses of interleukin six in exercise are anti-inflammatory and metabolic.
Chronic elevation, whether from inflamed tissue or from relentless high-intensity training, is harmful.

## Summary

To recap, interleukin six is usually a pro-inflammatory cytokine of infection and injury, but it is a myokine too.
Its gene switches on in exercising muscle.
Its release rises with intensity, duration, muscle mass and endurance, and, at high intensity, with lactate.
It rises up to a hundredfold after a marathon, and is followed by the interleukin one receptor antagonist rather than tumour necrosis factor alpha and interleukin one.
In acute exercise, it boosts glucose uptake, fat oxidation, lipolysis, liver glycogen breakdown and glucagon-like peptide one.
It mobilises N K cells, neutrophils and lymphocytes.
It raises interleukin ten and the interleukin one receptor antagonist, lowers tumour necrosis factor alpha, and triggers acute-phase proteins such as C-reactive protein.
In the interleukin six paradox, chronic elevation is pro-inflammatory.
That elevation may come from inflamed tissue such as visceral fat, or from chronic high-intensity training with its different signalling mechanism.
It is linked to cardiometabolic disease, autoimmunity, cancer progression and all-cause mortality.
So the same molecule helps in short pulses, and harms when it stays high.
