## Immune System Overview – Functions and the Two Branches

The immune system fights infection and cancer, promotes wound healing and tissue regeneration, and clears away cell debris.
It does all of this by telling self from non-self.
Anything that provokes an immune response is called an antigen.
The system has two branches.
The innate branch is inborn, acts within hours, and is non-specific, with no memory.
The adaptive branch, by contrast, develops throughout life, takes days, and is highly specific, with memory.
The two work together.
Innate cells switch on the adaptive branch, and antibodies call in innate defences.
In what follows, we'll look at what the system is for, how its branches differ, and how they cooperate over time.

## 1. What the immune system is for

First of all, the immune system has four functions.
These are defence against infectious diseases, fighting cancer, promoting wound healing and tissue regeneration, and eliminating cell debris.
Infections are caused by pathogens, which are microbes or other organisms that cause disease.
They include bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites such as helminths, which are worms.
Whatever the function, the immune system responds to antigens.
An antigen is any molecule or structure that provokes an immune response.
The name breaks down as anti and gen, meaning antibody generating, because antigens trigger the production of antibodies.
Antibodies are proteins that bind one particular antigen.
The whole system works by telling the body's own material, called self, from foreign material, called non-self, such as a microbe.

## 2. Two branches, one system

Now, these functions are shared by two branches, which act as one system.
The innate branch starts with physical barriers, such as the skin and lysozyme in secretions.
Lysozyme is an enzyme in tears, saliva and mucus that breaks down bacterial cell walls.
Behind these barriers lies phagocytosis, in which phagocytes engulf and digest microbes.
Then come natural killer cells, known as N K cells, which kill infected cells.
Alongside them is complement, a set of circulating plasma proteins that stimulate immune reactions when a pathogen activates them.
Innate cells also release inflammatory mediators, the chemical signals that set off inflammation.
Besides these, they release antimicrobial peptides, which are small proteins that kill microbes directly.
The adaptive branch relies instead on white blood cells called lymphocytes.
B cells, or B lymphocytes, make antibodies.
T cells, or T lymphocytes, carry out cell-mediated immunity, so called because the cells themselves do the work.
Every immune cell belongs to one of the two branches.
B and T lymphocytes are adaptive cells, and all the others are innate cells, such as neutrophils and dendritic cells.

## 3. Features of innate (natural) immunity

The two branches behave differently as well, and innate, or natural, immunity has six features.
The first is that we are born with it.
Second, it is always present and functional.
Third, it can respond immediately.
Fourth, it instructs the adaptive immune system, meaning that innate cells set the adaptive response going.
Fifth, it recognises non-self in a non-specific manner.
That is, it detects patterns shared by whole groups of microbes, rather than one particular microbe.
And sixth, it has no ability to remember pathogens or to improve with experience.

## 4. Features of adaptive (acquired) immunity

Adaptive, or acquired, immunity mirrors these features point for point.
Where innate immunity is inborn, we are not born with adaptive immunity.
Instead, it develops, or adapts, throughout a lifetime.
This happens as each antigen the body meets selects and multiplies the lymphocytes that recognise it.
That multiplication takes time, so unlike immediate innate immunity, adaptive immunity is slower to respond.
Innate recognition is non-specific, whereas adaptive immunity recognises antigens with high specificity.
In other words, each lymphocyte recognises one particular antigen.
Last of all, innate immunity has no memory, but adaptive immunity has a memory function.
Thanks to this memory, a second meeting with the same antigen brings a faster, stronger response.

## 5. Timescale: how the two branches work together

Let's turn now to timing, and to the course of a single infection, which shows the difference in speed.
Between zero and twelve hours after infection, the microbe meets epithelial barriers, phagocytes, dendritic cells, complement and N K cells.
Over the following days, from day one to day five, the adaptive steps take place.
B lymphocytes multiply and turn into antibody producers, and their antibodies coat the microbe.
Meanwhile, T lymphocytes are activated by a cell displaying the microbe, and they multiply into effector T cells.
Effector T cells are the working form, and they help a phagocyte destroy the microbe it has engulfed.
Put simply, the innate branch buys time while the adaptive branch is being built.
Each branch also depends on the other.
The innate branch instructs the adaptive one, since an innate cell, the dendritic cell, carries the antigen to the T lymphocytes.
In return, the adaptive branch borrows innate tools, as antibodies recruit complement and phagocytes.

## Summary

To recap, the immune system fights infection by pathogens and cancer, promotes wound healing and tissue regeneration, and clears cell debris.
It does so by telling self from non-self and by responding to antigens.
Innate immunity includes barriers, phagocytosis, N K cells, complement, inflammatory mediators and antimicrobial peptides.
It is inborn, immediate, non-specific and without memory, and it instructs the adaptive branch.
Adaptive immunity covers antibodies from B cells and cell-mediated immunity from T cells.
It develops through life, is slower and highly specific, and has memory.
Innate defences act within hours and buy time, while adaptive ones build over days.
The two cooperate, with dendritic cells carrying antigen to T lymphocytes, and antibodies recruiting complement and phagocytes.
That's the big picture of the immune system, and of how its two branches work as one.
