## Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP

Inflammation is a normal, healthy immune response that leads to microbial killing, wound healing and tissue regeneration.
It may be acute or chronic, and localised or systemic.
In acute inflammation, mediators dilate the local vessels, make them leaky and call in neutrophils.
The result is heat, redness, swelling, pain and loss of function.
Chronic inflammation, on the other hand, means that the immune system stays activated.
C-reactive protein, or C R P, is a non-specific blood marker of inflammation.
We'll go from what inflammation is, through the acute event and its treatment, to chronic inflammation and C R P.

## 1. What inflammation is

The lecture defines inflammation as a normal, healthy immune response, leading to microbial killing, wound healing and tissue regeneration.
It may be acute, meaning short-lived, or chronic, meaning persistent.
It may be localised, confined to one site like a swollen ankle.
Or it may be systemic, which means affecting the whole body through the blood.

## 2. The acute inflammatory event, step by step

An acute inflammatory event has a rapid onset, because its first responders are already in place.
Tissue injury, caused by infection, trauma, chemicals, heat and the like, is detected by the resident sentinel cells.
These are mast cells and macrophages, which stand guard in the tissues.
The sentinels then release chemical signals called inflammatory mediators.
Chief among them are cytokines, the immune system's protein messengers, along with histamine and prostaglandins.
Histamine is stored in mast cells, and it dilates vessels and makes them leaky.
Prostaglandins, meanwhile, are lipid mediators that cause pain, fever and vasodilation.
Complement, a set of circulating plasma proteins activated in the presence of a pathogen, joins them, and so do other mediators.
Together, the mediators cause vasodilation, which raises local blood flow.
They also increase the permeability of peripheral blood vessels, which lets fluid and plasma proteins leak out.
On top of that, they drive the recruitment of cells from the blood, mainly neutrophils.
Acute inflammation is therefore characterised by an infiltration of neutrophils.
These short-lived phagocytes engulf microbes by phagocytosis and release neutrophil extracellular traps.
All these players belong to the fast, non-specific branch of defence, which makes the response mainly innate.
Its five symptoms follow from the changes we've just described.
Heat comes from the increased blood flow.
Redness is that extra blood showing through the skin.
Swelling is fluid leaking out of the leaky vessels.
Pain comes from mediators such as prostaglandins acting on nerve endings, and from the pressure of the swelling.
Loss of function follows from the pain and swelling.
Once the stimulus is cleared, the neutrophils die by apoptosis, a tidy, programmed cell death.
The inflammation then usually resolves within a few days.

## 3. Helpful or harmful: to treat or not

So is inflammation helpful or harmful, and should it be treated?
On the helpful side, pain and loss of function protect against further tissue damage.
Increased blood flow and the infiltration of immune cells protect against microbial infection.
Finally, some inflammatory mediators promote wound healing and tissue regeneration.
Treatment should therefore ease the pain and swelling without abolishing these benefits.
The first option is cryotherapy, or ice, applied within twenty-four to forty-eight hours of injury.
It reduces the transmission of sensory pain signals.
It causes vasoconstriction too, narrowing the vessels to limit swelling.
It also reduces metabolism and oxidative stress, which is damage by reactive oxygen molecules.
That last effect reduces secondary ischaemic injury.
This is the extra tissue death around the original injury, when swelling and poor blood supply starve the surrounding cells of oxygen.
Cooled cells need less oxygen, so fewer of them die.
Anti-inflammatory medication, by contrast, targets the mediators.
Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, for example ibuprofen, sold as Nurofen, block the production of prostaglandins, easing pain and swelling.
They are meant for short-term use, either topical, on the skin over the injury, or oral.
On balance, both measures help when used early and briefly, but hinder if prolonged.
Inflammation can injure healthy tissue as well as protect it, partly through the enzymes and reactive oxygen molecules that neutrophils release.
Curbing pain, swelling and secondary damage is worthwhile for that reason.
Used for longer, however, ice would cut the blood flow that delivers immune cells, and the drugs would mask protective pain.
That is why the lecture limits both to the short term.

## 4. Chronic inflammation

Acute inflammation normally resolves, but chronic inflammation occurs when the immune system remains activated.
A persistent stimulus, such as an ongoing infection, can cause this.
So can a failure of regulation, as in autoimmune disease, where the immune system attacks the body's own tissues.
One example is the joints in rheumatoid arthritis.
Metabolic dysfunction, such as obesity, can cause it as well.
That's because adipose, or fat, tissue is an immunologically active tissue, containing macrophages and other immune cells.
Unlike acute inflammation, chronic inflammation involves both innate and adaptive immune responses.
Consider visceral adipose tissue, the fat around the abdominal organs, in a lean person and in an obese person.
In the lean person, the tissue is insulin-sensitive, with small fat cells, called adipocytes, among anti-inflammatory cells.
These are mainly M two macrophages, the anti-inflammatory state of the macrophage.
Alongside them sit regulatory T cells, which restrain immune responses, and eosinophils.
Together they release interleukin ten, interleukin four and interleukin thirteen.
In the obese person, the tissue is insulin resistant, meaning it responds poorly to insulin.
Its enlarged fat cells are crowded by M one macrophages, the pro-inflammatory state.
With them come inflammatory T helper one cells and C D eight T cells, plus neutrophils and B cells.
The M one macrophages and these other cells release interleukin one beta, interleukin six, tumour necrosis factor alpha, interferon gamma and free fatty acids.
The take-home of this switch from M two to M one is that obesity turns fat into a source of chronic low-grade inflammation, mild but constant.
That is why fat reappears in the interleukin six paradox.
There, interleukin six helps during exercise, but harms when inflamed fat keeps it raised.

## 5. C-reactive protein

Both kinds of inflammation raise the blood level of C-reactive protein, a protein synthesised in the liver in response to injury.
It is an acute-phase protein, one of the plasma proteins the liver pours out when interleukin six and other cytokines signal injury.
C R P functions as a pattern recognition receptor, a molecule that recognises structures common to microbes.
Having bound a microbe, it activates complement.
It functions as an opsonin, coating the microbe so that phagocytes grab it more easily.
And it regulates inflammatory responses.
As a marker of inflammation, C R P is used to screen for infection, chronic inflammation, heart disease and others.
It is not specific to any disease, showing that inflammation exists but not where or why.
For that reason, it is used to monitor and manage a condition already diagnosed.
The C R P molecule is a ring of five identical units.
It is read on a graded scale, from healthy to severely elevated, rather than as a yes-or-no test.
In sport, a persistently elevated baseline C R P, meaning a resting value that stays high, can indicate chronic overload, systemic stress or inadequate recovery between intense training blocks.
Regular moderate training, by contrast, lowers C R P.

## Summary

To sum up, inflammation is a normal, healthy immune response, acute or chronic, localised or systemic.
In acute inflammation, mast cells and macrophages release mediators that cause vasodilation, leaky vessels and an influx of neutrophils.
This produces heat, redness, swelling, pain and loss of function, in a mainly innate response that resolves within a few days.
Because these symptoms are protective, treatment is kept limited.
That means ice within twenty-four to forty-eight hours, and short-term non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, which block prostaglandins.
Chronic inflammation stems from a persistent stimulus, failed regulation or obesity.
In obese fat, the macrophages shift from anti-inflammatory M two to pro-inflammatory M one.
Lastly, C R P is a liver-made acute-phase protein and a non-specific marker.
In athletes, a persistently raised baseline signals overload or poor recovery.
And that's inflammation, from a swollen ankle to a blood test.
