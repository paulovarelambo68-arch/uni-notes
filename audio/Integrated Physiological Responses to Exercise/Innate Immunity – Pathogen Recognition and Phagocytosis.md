## Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis

When a microbe invades, innate cells first recognise it non-specifically.
That's because their receptors bind structures shared by whole groups of microbes.
Neutrophils and macrophages, the most important phagocytes, then destroy it by phagocytosis.
This process has three steps, recognition, ingestion and digestion, which unfold as seven phases.
Afterwards, neutrophils commonly die tidily by apoptosis.
Some, however, release their D N A as neutrophil extracellular traps, which catch pathogens.
Let's look at each stage, beginning with how innate cells pick out an invader.

## 1. How innate cells know an enemy

First, how does an innate cell know it's facing an enemy?
The answer is that pathogen recognition by innate cells is non-specific.
To recognise microbes, these cells express pathogen recognition receptors, otherwise known as pattern recognition receptors.
Among them are the Toll-like receptors.
These receptors bind pathogen-associated molecular patterns.
In other words, they bind structures that whole groups of microbes share, but that our own cells do not make.
The classic example is lipopolysaccharide, a fat joined to a long sugar chain.
It is present on all Gram-negative bacteria.
The term Gram-negative comes from the Gram stain, a dye test that sorts bacteria by the structure of their wall.
Gram-positive bacteria keep its purple dye, while Gram-negative bacteria lose it.
The molecules of each wall are exactly the patterns that these receptors read.
So each group of bacteria wears its own surface molecules, and those molecules are its pathogen-associated molecular patterns.
Because these molecules are shared, one receptor serves against many microbes.
Identical Toll-like receptors recognise a common structure on different microbes, and that is what non-specific means.
What's more, the receptors are inherited ready-made, so nothing has to be learned and the innate system needs no memory.
This explains why innate cells such as neutrophils are present and functional from birth, and respond immediately.
Unlike lymphocytes, though, they do not develop or adapt over a lifetime.

## 2. Phagocytosis: who does it and the three steps

Once it has been recognised, a microbe can be destroyed by phagocytosis.
The word literally means cell eating, and it describes a cell engulfing the microbe whole and digesting it.
The most important phagocytes are neutrophils and macrophages.
Following infection, both can leave the blood and travel to the site of infection.
Macrophages make the journey as monocytes, which mature in the tissue.
Whichever cell is involved, phagocytosis comes down to three steps, recognition, ingestion and digestion.
Handily, their initials spell the word rid.
Recognition relies on the receptors we've just met, and also on opsonins.
Opsonins are molecules that coat a microbe to mark it for eating, and complement proteins provide them.

## 3. The seven phases

Now, those three steps can be broken down further into seven phases, whether the target is a microbe or some other particle.
The process begins with chemotaxis and adherence, which make up phase one.
Chemotaxis is movement up a chemical gradient.
Put simply, the phagocyte crawls towards rising concentrations of attractants, such as microbial products and some cytokines.
It keeps crawling until the microbe adheres to it, and this contact is the recognition step.
Ingestion comes next, in phase two.
Here the cell wraps pseudopods, or false feet, around the microbe.
Pseudopods are arm-like extensions of the cell.
When the pseudopods fuse, the microbe is sealed inside a phagosome, and this is phase three.
A phagosome is a membrane-bound vesicle, also called a phagocytic vesicle.
Digestion follows.
The phagosome fuses with a lysosome, a vesicle full of digestive enzymes.
Together they form a phagolysosome, in phase four.
In phase five, the enzymes inside it digest the microbe.
Anything indigestible is left in a residual body, which is phase six.
Finally, in phase seven, the residual body discharges the waste at the cell surface.
If we map the three steps onto the seven phases, recognition happens at phase one, and ingestion covers phases two and three.
Digestion and clean-up take up phases four to seven.

## 4. What happens to neutrophils afterwards: NETs

The fate of the neutrophil rounds off the story.
After phagocytosis, neutrophils have a short lifespan, and they commonly undergo apoptosis.
Apoptosis is a programmed, tidy cell death.
The cell packages itself up for macrophages to clear, without spilling its contents.
This quiet removal contributes to the resolution of inflammation.
That means the local inflammatory reaction settles once the microbes are cleared.
Alternatively, some neutrophils undergo a process called netosis.
During netosis, their nuclear chromatin is released into the extracellular space.
Chromatin here means the D N A of the nucleus, together with the proteins it is wound around.
Outside the cell, it forms a matrix, or mesh, known as neutrophil extracellular traps.
These traps catch pathogens, which may then be phagocytosed by other neutrophils and by macrophages.
The neutrophil itself may or may not die after netosis.
Through this trapping, netosis can contribute to pathogen killing.
Excessive netosis, on the other hand, contributes to thrombosis, autoimmunity and cancer progression.
Thrombosis is clotting inside blood vessels, and autoimmunity is an immune attack on the body's own tissues.

## Summary

To recap, innate recognition is non-specific, since pattern recognition receptors, such as the Toll-like receptors, bind pathogen-associated molecular patterns shared by whole groups of microbes.
One such pattern is lipopolysaccharide, present on all Gram-negative bacteria.
As a result, identical Toll-like receptors recognise a common structure on different microbes, and no memory is needed.
Neutrophils and macrophages, the most important phagocytes, leave the blood and destroy microbes by recognition, ingestion and digestion.
The steps run as seven phases, from chemotaxis and adherence through to the discharge of waste, with digestion taking place inside a phagolysosome.
Afterwards, neutrophils commonly undergo apoptosis, which helps inflammation resolve.
Some undergo netosis instead, releasing their chromatin as neutrophil extracellular traps that catch pathogens.
Netosis helps kill pathogens, but in excess it contributes to thrombosis, autoimmunity and cancer progression.
And with that, we've followed the innate response from first contact with a microbe to the fate of the neutrophil.
