## Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold

This note asks whether breathing itself ever holds back exercise.
In most healthy people it does not, thanks to cheap breathing and lungs with capacity to spare.
The lung does fall behind in three cases, namely the elite athlete, the smoker, and exercise in cold air.
In this note, we'll weigh the cost of breathing against the lung's reserve, and then meet these three exceptions.

## 1. What breathing costs – compliance, airway resistance and the oxygen cost of breathing

Breathing is work, and we begin with what sets its price.
In a healthy person, the breathing muscles take only a small share of the body's oxygen, even in exercise.
Two things decide the cost of each breath.
The first is compliance, which is how easily the lungs and chest stretch to let air in.
A stiff lung, like a new balloon, takes more effort to inflate.
Three conditions reduce compliance, because they stiffen or scar the lung.
They are acute respiratory distress syndrome, fibrosis, and smoking-related chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, known as C O P D.
The second thing is airway resistance, which is how much the airways resist the flow of air.
Asthma and C O P D raise airway resistance by narrowing the airways.
Resistance follows the fourth-power rule, the same law that governs blood vessels.
Halving an airway's diameter raises its resistance sixteen-fold, since two to the power of four is sixteen.
With lung disease, breathing can take up to forty per cent of the oxygen used in exercise.
In healthy people the numbers are far smaller.
At ventilations up to ten litres per minute, each litre of air breathed costs between one point five and two point zero millilitres of oxygen.
The cost per litre stays near this until ventilation reaches roughly one hundred litres per minute.
In moderate exercise, breathing takes three to five per cent of total oxygen uptake.
At V O two max, the highest rate at which the body can use oxygen, it takes eight to eleven per cent.
The share keeps climbing in highly trained endurance athletes, who breathe one hundred and fifty litres per minute or more.
For them, the cost of exercise hyperpnoea, the deep and rapid breathing of exercise, reaches fifteen per cent or more of total oxygen uptake.
As ventilation rises from ten to seventy litres per minute, total oxygen uptake can be split into two parts.
The larger, non-respiratory part is the oxygen used by everything other than breathing, mostly the working muscles.
The smaller, respiratory part is the oxygen used by the breathing muscles.
At ten litres per minute, the respiratory part is a sliver, which makes breathing almost free.
By seventy litres per minute it has grown, the reason being that pushing air faster through the same airways costs more per litre.
Breathing is cheap until it is not.

## 2. Competition for blood between the breathing muscles and the limbs

The rising cost of breathing matters for one reason.
The breathing muscles have no private blood supply.
The cost of breathing can be read either as a percentage of total oxygen uptake or as millilitres of oxygen per litre of air.
Both climb steeply at high ventilations.
At the top end, the breathing muscles and the limb muscles become rivals.
They are vascular beds in parallel, meaning side branches fed from the same arteries.
They therefore share one cardiac output, the volume of blood the heart pumps each minute.
In intense exercise, the two compete for oxygen and blood flow.
Eventually the breathing muscles take oxygen that the limbs could have used.
This happens most of all in elite athletes, whose ventilation is very large.

## 3. The breathing reserve – why ventilation does not limit healthy people

Since breathing has a cost, it is fair to ask whether the lungs set the upper limit on how much oxygen a healthy person can use.
Three facts show that they do not.
First, training raises V O two max a great deal.
Yet aerobic training changes the lung far less than it changes the heart, blood and muscles.
If the lung were the limit, V O two max could not rise until the lung itself improved.
It follows that the lung must already have more capacity than the body needs.
Second, in strenuous effort healthy people hyperventilate.
This means they breathe more than is needed to clear the carbon dioxide they produce.
The extra fresh air flushes carbon dioxide out of the alveoli and tops up their oxygen.
Alveolar carbon dioxide falls as a result, and alveolar oxygen rises slightly.
Third, there is a measurable breathing reserve.
Maximum voluntary ventilation, or M V V, is the most air a person can move in fifteen seconds of the fastest, deepest breathing possible.
That volume is then scaled up to a full minute.
Even at V O two max, ventilation reaches only sixty to eighty-five per cent of M V V.
As a result, part of the lung's pumping capacity is never used.
The conclusion is that, in healthy people, ventilation does not normally limit aerobic power.

## 4. The exception – exercise-induced arterial hypoxaemia

The breathing reserve has limits, and in some people the lung does fall behind in intense exercise.
Exercise-induced arterial hypoxaemia, or E I H, is a fall in arterial oxygen saturation during intense exercise.
Saturation is the share of the blood's haemoglobin that is carrying oxygen.
A fall means the blood leaving the lung is not fully loaded.
It happens above all in endurance athletes.
Their training has built an exceptional heart and muscles while leaving the lung almost unchanged.
Three possible causes are recognised.
The first is ventilation-perfusion inequality, where air and blood are matched unevenly across the lung.
As a consequence, some alveoli receive blood but too little air.
The second is shunt, where some blood passes from the venous to the arterial side without meeting ventilated alveoli.
Having bypassed the ventilated alveoli, that blood never picks up oxygen.
The third is the transit-time problem.
A very high cardiac output pushes blood through the alveolar capillary so fast that it leaves before equilibrating with alveolar gas.
Whatever the cause, the sign of E I H is a widening alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient at maximal exercise, with a fall in arterial oxygen pressure.
The gradient is the difference in oxygen partial pressure between alveolar air and arterial blood.
Partial pressure is the measure of how much oxygen is present.
Normally alveolar and arterial oxygen stay close, as blood equilibrates with air.
In E I H they separate, because hyperventilation raises alveolar oxygen while arterial oxygen falls.
The working muscles make the fall worse.
They extract more oxygen, leaving the venous blood that returns to the lung carrying less oxygen than at rest.
For that reason, any blood that misses the air through mismatch or shunt drags the arterial value down further.
In athletes exercising from rest to maximum, four measurements tell the story.
Oxygen pressure in the alveolar air rises from about one hundred to above one hundred and ten millimetres of mercury, driven by hyperventilation.
Oxygen pressure in the arterial blood holds near ninety millimetres of mercury until about sixty per cent of V O two max.
After that, it falls below eighty millimetres of mercury.
The widening gap between alveolar and arterial oxygen is the alveolar-arterial gradient, and it is exercise-induced hypoxaemia in action.
Meanwhile, acid building up in the blood drives p H down from about seven point four zero to seven point three zero.
Arterial carbon dioxide pressure falls from about forty millimetres of mercury to the mid-thirties, blown off by hyperventilation.

## 5. What training changes in the lung, and why women reach their limit sooner

The lagging lung raises the question of what training changes in the respiratory system at all.
Training makes no structural change in the lung.
What adapts is the pump around it.
The diaphragm and chest muscles gain endurance and strength, like any other trained muscle.
In elite athletes, this can raise vital capacity by twenty-five to thirty per cent.
Vital capacity is the largest breath a person can take in and blow out.
What makes swimmers gain most is that they breathe in against the weight of water pressing on the chest.
The ratio of ventilation to oxygen uptake in maximal effort also rises, reflecting how much more air a trained athlete can move.
Still, lung function tests do not separate elite endurance athletes from healthy people.
Many professional players have below-average vital capacities.
Lung size is largely genetic, and the tests are used to spot illness rather than fitness.
Women meet the same fixed lung with a smaller starting size.
Compared with men, women have smaller lungs and narrower airways.
They also have a smaller diffusion surface and lower lung function measures.
Airways have a ceiling on how fast air can flow out, and in maximal exercise a woman's airways reach it.
This is called expiratory flow limitation.
At that point, a woman works harder to breathe, and uses up the ventilatory reserve that a man would keep.
That can hurt oxygen transfer from alveoli to blood, most of all in highly trained women.

## 6. Smoking – acute effects of one cigarette and chronic damage

Smoking is the commonest way a healthy lung becomes a limiting one, and its effects come in two layers.
The acute effects follow a single session of fifteen puffs over five minutes.
Airway resistance rises threefold for about thirty-five minutes, in smokers and non-smokers alike.
The cause is a vagal reflex.
The vagus nerve tightens the smooth muscle of the airways.
Here the reflex is triggered by nicotine acting on nicotinic receptors, and probably by smoke particles irritating the airway lining.
This narrowing matters little in light exercise.
At high intensity, though, it raises the oxygen cost of breathing from about nine per cent to as much as fifteen per cent of oxygen uptake.
At similar ventilations, each litre of air costs more oxygen after smoking than without it.
The airway effects reverse within twenty-four hours.
Smoke also delivers carbon monoxide, which binds to haemoglobin and blocks oxygen from binding.
After one day without cigarettes, the exercise heart rate is five to seven per cent lower.
Smoking also slows the cilia of the mucociliary escalator, both acutely and chronically.
The mucociliary escalator is the moving carpet that sweeps mucus out of the airways.
The chronic effects build over years and work down the respiratory tract.
In the mouth, larynx and pharynx, hot gas and particles cause constant irritation, giving hoarseness, cough and wheeze.
In the bronchi, chemicals and particles stop the cilia, letting mucus clog the airways.
This is chronic bronchitis, the first stage of C O P D.
Deeper still, the alveoli lose their elastic tissue and break down, which cuts the amount of oxygen they transfer.
The result is emphysema.
C O P D then limits exercise tolerance, until strenuous exercise is impossible for many.
In the lung's blood vessels, tobacco causes atherosclerosis, which raises the pressure in them.
This raised pressure is called pulmonary hypertension.
Finally, smoking blunts the rise of heart rate in exercise.
A smoker can therefore look fit on a heart-rate test.

## 7. Cold air – oxygen uptake, ventilation, airway water loss and endurance

Cold is the last limit, and it acts on the whole body as much as on the lung.
In the cold, resting oxygen uptake rises, and so does oxygen uptake in submaximal exercise.
Heat flows from warm to cold at a rate set by the temperature difference, called the thermal gradient.
Cold air widens that gradient between skin and surroundings.
The wider gradient drives more heat loss in two ways.
One is conduction, which is direct contact.
The other is convection, which is moving air carrying heat away.
The body burns extra fuel to replace that heat, and the fuel needs oxygen.
Wet clothing makes it worse, given that water carries heat away far faster than air.
Sudden cold raises ventilation, and submaximal ventilation stays higher than usual.
Only at high intensity do the responses become similar to normal.
Cold air rarely damages the airways, as they warm it quickly on the way in.
Warm air, however, holds far more moisture than cold air.
That difference forces the airways to moisten each breath as they warm it.
In doing so they lose water and heat, most of all in strenuous exercise.
This fluid loss contributes to dehydration, a dry mouth, a burning throat and irritation of the airways.
Endurance falls in the cold for reasons that add up.
Keeping warm raises the metabolic cost of everything.
A cooled body reaches a lower maximal heart rate, which pulls maximal cardiac output and V O two max down with it.
Cold also shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the left.
This left shift makes haemoglobin hold its oxygen more tightly and unload less in the muscle.
Blood flow to the working muscle falls as well.

## Summary

To recap, the price of breathing is set by compliance, how easily the lungs and chest stretch, and by airway resistance.
Airway resistance obeys the fourth-power rule, so halving the diameter raises resistance sixteen-fold.
Breathing takes three to five per cent of oxygen uptake in moderate exercise, and eight to eleven per cent at V O two max.
In elite athletes, it takes fifteen per cent or more.
Even then, ventilation does not limit healthy people.
The reason is that at V O two max they hyperventilate and use only sixty to eighty-five per cent of M V V.
The exception is exercise-induced arterial hypoxaemia in endurance athletes.
In them, the trouble lies in ventilation-perfusion mismatch, shunt, or too short a transit time.
Any of these widens the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient and lowers arterial saturation.
Training changes no lung structure, only the breathing muscles, which is why lung function tests cannot tell athletes from healthy people.
Women's smaller lungs and airways reach their flow limit sooner.
One cigarette triples airway resistance for thirty-five minutes by a vagal reflex.
Chronic smoking brings chronic bronchitis, emphysema and pulmonary hypertension.
Cold air raises oxygen uptake, dries the airways, and lowers maximal heart rate, cardiac output and V O two max.
It also shifts the dissociation curve to the left.
And that's how breathing, cheap and well in reserve for most people, can still hold back the elite athlete, the smoker and the exerciser in the cold.
