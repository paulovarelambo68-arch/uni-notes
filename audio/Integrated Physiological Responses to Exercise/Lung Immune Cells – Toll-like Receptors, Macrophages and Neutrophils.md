## Lung Immune Cells – Toll-like Receptors, Macrophages and Neutrophils

Every breath pulls microbes into the lungs, and the barriers of the airways stop most of them.
The cells in this note deal with the rest.
There are three characters.
First come the sensors that notice a microbe.
Then comes the macrophage, which fights or repairs.
Last comes the neutrophil, which kills and dies.
In this note, we'll follow these three characters from the first alarm to the end of the fight.

## 1. Who guards the lung

Three characters guard the lung, and we'll meet them in the order they act.
Pattern recognition receptors are protein sensors on and inside immune cells.
They notice a microbe and raise the alarm.
The alveolar macrophage, a large eating cell that lives in the air sacs, answers the alarm.
It deals with whatever slipped past the airway barriers.
The neutrophil then pours in from the blood, kills with chemicals and dies.

## 2. How innate cells recognise a microbe

Before any cell can act, it must tell a microbe apart from the body's own tissue.
The theory that explains how cells do this was proposed by Janeway in nineteen eighty-nine.
Innate cells carry microbe-spotting proteins called pattern recognition receptors.
These receptors are encoded in the germline.
That means their genes are inherited ready-made, so everyone is born with the same set rather than learning it.
There are about one hundred different pattern recognition receptors.
Each has a fixed specificity, recognising one kind of pattern and never varying.
Examples are the Toll-like receptors and the Nod-like receptors.
Toll-like receptors sit on the cell surface or inside the cell, while Nod-like receptors sit only inside.
Both recognise pathogen-associated molecular patterns, structures shared by whole classes of microbes.
Peptidoglycan from bacterial walls is one example, and lipopolysaccharide, or L P S, from the outer wall of some bacteria is another.
More than a thousand of these patterns are known, and they make good targets for two reasons.
They are usually essential to the microbe's survival, leaving it unable to drop them.
They are also never found on the body's own cells, and for that reason the alarm never fires at healthy tissue.

## 3. Toll-like receptors: structure and signal

The Toll-like receptors are the best-studied sensors, and their build explains how the alarm works.
A Toll-like receptor has three parts.
Outside the cell is a horseshoe made of eighteen to twenty-five copies of a leucine-rich repeat.
A leucine-rich repeat is a short protein motif rich in the amino acid leucine.
The inner curve of the horseshoe grips the microbial pattern.
A single helix crosses the membrane.
Inside the cell hangs the T I R domain, named after the Toll and interleukin one receptors, which both carry it.
Take a pair of these sensors, Toll-like receptor one and Toll-like receptor two, joined in the cell membrane as a heterodimer, a pair of two different receptors.
Their two leucine-rich horseshoes curve around the bound molecule, a tri-acylated lipopeptide, which is a fatty pattern from bacterial surfaces.
Two helices cross the membrane, and the two T I R domains hang inside the cell.
The alarm goes off the moment the horseshoes close around the pattern, because closing brings the two T I R domains together.
Only the pair can start the signal, like two keys that must be turned at once.
When a pattern binds, two receptors usually pair up, which is called dimerising.
Their T I R domains recruit adaptor proteins, mainly My D eighty-eight and T R I F.
The adaptors start a cascade of proteins activating other proteins, with two results.
One is phagocytosis, the swallowing of the microbe.
The other is the activation of transcription factors, proteins that enter the nucleus and switch particular genes on.
Chief among them is nuclear factor kappa B, known as N F kappa B.
The genes switched on make cytokines, the immune system's messenger molecules.
They also make co-stimulatory molecules, the second signals needed to activate a T cell.
One receptor gripping one pattern thus ends in a cell that eats, calls for help and can wake the adaptive system.
An immune cell keeps its Toll-like receptors in two places.
On the plasma membrane sit Toll-like receptors one and two as a pair, and Toll-like receptor two on its own.
Toll-like receptors four and five sit there too, along with the pair of two and six.
Together they are matched to bacterial lipopeptides, peptidoglycan, L P S, and flagellin, the protein of the bacterial tail.
Inside the cell, in the membrane of the endosome where swallowed material is broken down, sit Toll-like receptors three, seven, eight and nine.
These recognise double-stranded R N A, single-stranded R N A and C P G D N A, which a virus exposes only once it is inside.
About ten sensors therefore cover bacteria outside and viruses inside.
For the lung, the example to follow is Toll-like receptor four, or T L R four.
It is found on monocytes, the blood cells that become macrophages, and in many tissues.
T L R four recognises lipopolysaccharide, the L P S in the outer wall of Gram-negative bacteria.
Among these bacteria, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumoniae cause pneumonia.
This makes T L R four a front-line sensor of lung infection.
T L R four also recognises damage-associated molecular patterns, such as H M G B one and S-one-hundred-A-eight.
These molecules stay inside healthy cells and spill out when the body's own cells are injured.
As a result, the same receptor answers injury as well as infection.
A bruised lung with no microbe in it still raises an inflammatory alarm.
That helps clear the damage, but it can also keep inflammation going when there is nothing to kill.

## 4. The macrophage: two forms

The first cell to answer the alarm is usually a macrophage, and the lung has two kinds.
Alveolar macrophages are residents that live in the air spaces, and they are the first cells to meet an inhaled microbe.
Peripheral macrophages are recruits.
They begin as blood monocytes, which leave the vessels during an infection and mature into macrophages in the tissue.
A recruit's journey starts with a stem cell in the bone marrow.
The stem cell gives a monocyte in the blood, which settles in a tissue as a macrophage.
Resident macrophages follow the same journey, but end as the interstitial and alveolar macrophages that already live in the lung.
Organs range from those guarded mainly by tissue-resident macrophages to those guarded mainly by monocyte-derived ones.
The lung sits in the middle, so its defence is a mix of residents and recruits.
Either kind can polarise, meaning it settles into one of two working states depending on the signals around it.
Think of a soldier and a repair crew.
The soldier is the M one macrophage, which is broadly pro-inflammatory.
Four signals switch it on.
They are interferon gamma, L P S, tumour necrosis factor alpha, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor.
These are signals that mean a microbe is present.
In response, the M one macrophage makes the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin one beta, interleukin six and interleukin eight.
Its other pro-inflammatory products are tumour necrosis factor alpha and interferons.
Together, these cytokines call in and rouse other cells.
The M one macrophage kills by making nitric oxide through the enzyme inducible nitric oxide synthase.
It also kills with reactive oxygen species, aggressive forms of oxygen.
It carries out more phagocytosis than a resting cell.
Its surface markers, C D eighty, C D eighty-six and H L A D R, are the molecules that present antigen and activate T cells.
In this way, it also hands the fight to the adaptive system.
The repair crew is the M two macrophage, which is broadly anti-inflammatory and has several sub-types.
It is switched on by interleukin four, interleukin thirteen, interleukin ten, and transforming growth factor beta.
Adenosine and the steroid hormones called glucocorticoids switch it on too.
These signals mean the fight is ending or must be calmed.
The M two macrophage makes interleukin ten, transforming growth factor beta, and vascular endothelial growth factor, or V E G F.
Interleukin ten and transforming growth factor beta dampen inflammation.
V E G F is a growth factor that grows new blood vessels into healing tissue.
M two carries out even more phagocytosis than M one, because clearing dead cells is part of repair.
The M two macrophage's markers are C D one hundred and sixty-three and C D two hundred and six.
Macrophages in fat tissue settle into the same two states.
A healthy response starts with M one to kill and ends with M two to repair.

## 5. The neutrophil

When the macrophage's cytokines reach the blood, the third character arrives.
The neutrophil is a granulocyte, a white cell packed with granules of killing proteins.
It is the most common white blood cell, making up as much as seventy per cent of all white cells.
A microlitre of blood holds between two thousand five hundred and seven thousand of them.
Their total count is a critical indicator of health.
Neutrophils circulate in an inactive state.
They live only hours to days, and the bone marrow constantly replaces them.
Injury switches them on in three steps.
First, the neutrophil finds the site.
Damaged tissue and activated macrophages release chemical signals that spread outward as a chemotactic gradient.
The gradient is a trail that grows stronger towards its source.
G-protein-coupled receptors on the cell surface sense the trail and rearrange the cell's actin skeleton, its internal scaffold.
The cell then crawls up the gradient and out of the blood vessel.
Second, at the site, the neutrophil becomes fully active.
It releases its granule proteins and swallows the microbes by phagocytosis.
It also throws out neutrophil extracellular traps, webs of its own D N A that snare bacteria.
Third, it dies.
Within hours it enters apoptosis, a tidy, programmed death.
This limits the damage its chemicals would otherwise do to healthy tissue.
Macrophages in the liver, bone marrow and spleen clear the dead cells.

## 6. The oxidative burst

The neutrophil's main weapon is the oxidative burst, also called the respiratory burst.
It is a sudden production of reactive oxygen species.
The second name has nothing to do with breathing.
It comes from the sharp rise in the cell's oxygen use when the burst starts.
The burst happens in the phagosome, the pouch that closes around a swallowed microbe.
The phagosome then fuses with lysosomes, the cell's bags of digestive enzymes, and turns acidic.
The enzyme N A D P H oxidase, also known as Nox two, sits in the phagosome membrane.
It makes superoxide, the first reactive oxygen species.
Two oxygen molecules take electrons from the carrier N A D P H and become two superoxide ions.
As a reaction, two oxygen molecules and one N A D P H give two superoxide ions, one N A D P plus and one hydrogen ion.
Here, N A D P H is the electron donor, and N A D P plus is the spent donor.
Superoxide is then converted to hydrogen peroxide.
The neutrophil also turns hydrogen peroxide and chloride into hypochlorous acid, the active ingredient of household bleach.
These chemicals kill by oxidising the microbe.
They strip electrons from its proteins, fats and D N A until it falls apart, like bleach on a worktop.
The body's own tissue resists them better than the pathogen does, which is why such a crude weapon can be used.
Even so, the burst is double-edged.
Neutrophils also release reactive oxygen species outside the cell.
They need this release to cut a path through the extracellular matrix, the protein mesh between cells, but the same release damages nearby tissue.
The burst is used as a test of neutrophil health.
It is also the function that chronic hard training impairs.
The burst is not perfect either, since some bacteria, Staphylococcus aureus among them, survive inside the phagosome and escape.

## 7. When neutrophils run out

Neutrophils die so quickly that a long infection can exhaust the supply.
In the late phase of pneumonia, they are used up faster than the bone marrow can replace them.
The result is neutropenia, too few neutrophils in the blood.
With its front-line killers gone, the lung is open to a second infection about a week after the illness began.
The risk is treated with preventive, or prophylactic, antibiotics.
It is also treated with drugs that stimulate the neutrophil's precursor cells in the marrow.
The same neutropenia follows acute radiation sickness, from radiotherapy or a nuclear event.
Radiation kills dividing cells, and the marrow cells that make neutrophils never stop dividing.

## Summary

To recap, the lung's cellular defence starts with pattern recognition receptors.
There are about one hundred of these inherited sensors, and they recognise pathogen-associated molecular patterns.
Those patterns are shared by classes of microbes and absent from the body's own cells.
A Toll-like receptor has a leucine-rich horseshoe outside, a helix through the membrane, and a T I R domain inside.
Two receptors pairing around a pattern activate N F kappa B, which switches on cytokine genes.
T L R four recognises L P S from Gram-negative bacteria, and also damage-associated molecular patterns from injured cells.
This lets it answer injury as well as infection.
Macrophages polarise into two states.
M one is the pro-inflammatory killer, switched on by interferon gamma and L P S.
It makes interleukin one beta, interleukin six, tumour necrosis factor alpha, nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species.
M two is the anti-inflammatory repairer, switched on by interleukins four, thirteen and ten.
It makes interleukin ten, transforming growth factor beta and V E G F.
The neutrophil is the most common white cell, at between two thousand five hundred and seven thousand per microlitre.
It follows a chemotactic gradient to the site, degranulates, carries out phagocytosis, throws out extracellular traps, and dies by apoptosis within hours.
Its oxidative burst uses N A D P H oxidase to make superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and hypochlorous acid.
These kill the microbe but also damage tissue.
In late pneumonia the marrow cannot keep up, and neutropenia follows.
The second infection, about a week in, is prevented with antibiotics and marrow stimulation.
And that's how the lung's sensors, macrophages and neutrophils work together, from the first alarm to the final clean-up.
