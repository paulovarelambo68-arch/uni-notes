## Lung Volumes and Capacities – Spirometry, FEV1 and FVC

The chest works as a pump, and this note measures that pump.
It covers how much air moves with each breath, how much never leaves the lungs, and how fast air can be blown out.
Those measurements tell healthy lungs from diseased ones, and they show what exercise does to each breath.

## 1. Measuring a breath: the spirogram

We begin with how a single breath is measured.
A spirometer is an instrument that records the volume of air breathed in and out over time.
Its output is called a spirogram.
Every value that follows is only a guideline, since lung volumes vary with body size, sex and age.
The normal breath comes first.
Tidal volume is the air moved in or out in one normal breath, about five hundred millilitres.
After a normal breath in, about three litres more can still be breathed in, and this extra air is the inspiratory reserve volume.
After a normal breath out, about one point two litres more can still be pushed out, and this extra air is the expiratory reserve volume.
Adding tidal volume and the two reserve volumes together gives the biggest breath a person can take.
That biggest breath is the forced vital capacity, or F V C, of about four point seven litres.
As a formula, forced vital capacity equals tidal volume plus inspiratory reserve volume plus expiratory reserve volume.
The forced vital capacity spans from the fullest the lungs can get to the emptiest they can get.
Even at that emptiest point, the lungs still hold the residual volume, the air that never leaves.
Total lung capacity spans everything from no air at all up to that fullest point.
Men and women have separate average lung volumes, which show the range that the guideline values sit in.

## 2. The air that never leaves: residual volume

Even the hardest breath out leaves some air in the lungs, and that air is the residual volume.
In healthy young women, residual volume averages zero point eight to one point two litres.
In young men, it averages zero point nine to one point four litres.
A spirometer cannot measure residual volume, as that air never comes out of the mouth.
Residual volume rises with age.
The cause is a loss of elastic tissue, which is replaced by stiffer, scar-like tissue, a change called fibrosis.
The lung then springs back less at the end of a breath out, leaving more air behind.
This leftover air has a job.
The alveoli never empty, like a bath that is never fully drained.
This means that blood passing them between breaths always finds air to exchange gases with.
In this way, residual volume keeps gas exchange going between breaths.
As a result, blood levels of oxygen and carbon dioxide stay steady, instead of swinging with each breath.
Now, back to the size of the lungs.
Adding residual volume to the biggest breath gives the whole lung, the total lung capacity, of about five point nine litres.
Total lung capacity equals residual volume plus forced vital capacity.
Exercise also changes residual volume, in a way that is easy to get backwards.
During exercise, residual volume dips slightly.
For a while after a bout of exercise, though, residual volume rises.
There are two reasons for this.
The first reason is that small airways deep in the lung shut early.
These tiny airways have no stiff wall of their own.
They are held open only by the pull of the stretched lung tissue around them.
At the end of a full breath out, the lung is small, which weakens that pull.
The squeeze from the chest can then flatten the airways, like a soft straw pinched shut.
After exercise, these airways flatten sooner than usual, trapping air in the alveoli behind them before it can leave.
The second reason is that extra blood stays in the vessels in and around the lungs for a while after exercise.
That blood takes up space inside the chest.
The lungs therefore cannot be squeezed down as far as usual, so a little more air is left behind at the end of the breath out.

## 3. How fast, not just how much: dynamic volumes and FEV1

The volumes we've met up to now are static.
Static volumes say how much air the lungs hold, not how fast it moves.
Air moving over time, by contrast, is dynamic.
This dynamic capacity depends on both the size of the biggest breath and the speed of breathing.
Speed is tested with a timed, forced breath out.
The person fills the lungs and blows out as hard and as fast as possible.
The air blown out in the first second is the forced expiratory volume in one second, or F E V one.
Dividing F E V one by F V C gives the fraction of the vital capacity emptied in one second.
The ratio of F E V one to F V C indicates the lung's capacity for airflow.
A high ratio needs strong expiratory muscles to push the air, and airways that are open to let it through.
For this reason, the ratio reflects expiratory power and airway resistance.
In healthy people, the ratio is about eighty-five per cent.
A ratio of seventy per cent or less indicates airway obstruction, meaning that narrowed airways slow the air out.
Four diseases cause this kind of obstruction.
The first is chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or C O P D.
C O P D is long-term narrowing and damage of the airways, mostly from smoking.
The second is asthma, a reversible narrowing of inflamed airways.
The third is bronchiectasis, in which the airways are permanently widened and fill with mucus.
The fourth is cystic fibrosis, a genetic disease whose thick, sticky mucus blocks the airways.
When air leaves slowly, the lungs cannot empty before the next breath is due.
The result is that air is trapped, the lungs over-inflate, and oxygenation suffers.

## 4. Narrow airways or stiff lungs: obstructive versus restrictive disease

The ratio of F E V one to F V C is interpreted alongside the forced vital capacity itself, because lung disease comes in two broad kinds.
Obstructive disease is a problem of breathing out, in which narrowed airways let air leave slowly.
Restrictive disease is a problem of breathing in.
Stiff lungs cannot fill, which leaves them holding less air.
Stiffness means low compliance, and compliance is the ease with which the lung stretches.
Obstruction is like squeezing a full bottle through a narrow neck, which empties slowly.
Restriction is like squeezing a small, rigid bottle, which empties fast but never held much.
Consider a single forced breath out, timed second by second, in a normal lung and in a lung with each kind of disease.
In each case, F E V one is the air blown out in the first second, and F V C is the whole breath.
In the normal lung, air rushes out, and four of its five litres are out after one second.
That gives a ratio of eighty per cent, close to the healthy eighty-five per cent.
The obstructive example is bronchial asthma, where air leaves slowly and the lungs are still emptying at the end of the forced breath.
Narrowed airways let only one point three litres out in the first second.
The whole breath comes to three point one litres.
That is a ratio of forty-two per cent.
The restrictive example is pulmonary fibrosis, where the lungs are scarred.
Here, air leaves fast and soon stops, with only a small volume breathed out.
The scarred lungs held only three point one litres.
Yet their airways are open, so two point eight litres leave in the first second, a ratio of ninety per cent.
Both diseased lungs share the same small F V C, which is why the ratio tells them apart.
Obstruction shows as a low ratio, whatever the F V C.
Restriction shows as a low F V C with a normal or high ratio, because what little air there is leaves fast.

## 5. What one bout of exercise does to each volume

The volumes measured at rest all shift once exercise begins.
Two other measures shift as well, the breathing rate and the minute ventilation, which is the total air breathed in one minute.
At rest, tidal volume is five hundred millilitres per breath, and in exercise it can rise to as much as four times that.
The breathing rate, or frequency, is twelve to fifteen breaths per minute at rest, and in exercise it may rise to sixty breaths per minute.
Minute ventilation is six litres per minute at rest.
During exercise, minute ventilation rises to as much as one hundred and seventy-five litres per minute in large, aerobically trained athletes.
At rest, the inspiratory reserve volume is three litres, and the expiratory reserve volume is one point two litres.
In exercise, both reserve volumes decrease.
Vital capacity is four point seven litres at rest.
Residual volume, the air that never leaves the lungs, is one point two litres at rest.
Total lung capacity is five point nine litres at rest.
In exercise, all three decrease slightly.
The pattern is simple.
Each breath grows by eating into both reserves, which shrink while tidal volume swells to as much as four times its resting size.
The growing breath takes more from the inspiratory reserve, since there is far more spare room for breathing in than for breathing out.
That spare room is three litres for breathing in, against one point two litres for breathing out.
The capacities barely change, given that the size of the lungs and chest is fixed.
Exercise only changes how much of that space each breath uses.

## Summary

To recap, a spirometer records lung volume over time, and a capacity is the sum of two or more volumes.
Tidal volume is about five hundred millilitres.
The inspiratory reserve is about three litres, and the expiratory reserve about one point two litres.
Together, these three volumes make the forced vital capacity, about four point seven litres.
The air that can never be breathed out is the residual volume, about one point two litres.
Residual volume keeps gas exchange going between breaths, rises with age through fibrosis, and rises for a while after exercise.
F E V one is the air blown out in the first second of a forced exhalation.
The ratio of F E V one to F V C is about eighty-five per cent in health.
A ratio of seventy per cent or less means obstruction, as narrowed airways slow the air out.
Obstruction is found in C O P D, asthma, bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis.
Restriction gives a low F V C with a normal or high ratio.
The reason is that stiff lungs cannot fill, but what they hold leaves fast.
In exercise, tidal volume rises to as much as four times its resting size by eating into both reserves.
Breathing rate can reach sixty breaths per minute, and the capacities change only slightly.
And that's how a few simple breaths, measured for size and speed, reveal how well the chest's pump works in health, in disease and in exercise.
