## Mechanisms of Exercise-Induced Immune Changes

Exercise reaches immune cells through three messengers.
These are the catecholamines, meaning adrenaline and noradrenaline, along with cortisol and cytokines.
Catecholamines act directly on the cells' adrenergic receptors, and cortisol acts on their glucocorticoid receptors.
Meanwhile, a higher cardiac output washes white blood cells off vessel walls into the circulation.
After a hard bout, neutrophils keep rising, while lymphocytes and natural killer, or N K, cells fall to low levels.
This is an open window for infection, which lasts no longer than twenty-four hours and is deepest two to six hours afterwards.
Cells leave the blood by sticking to and squeezing through vessel walls.
For this, they use cell adhesion molecules, which catecholamines increase on lymphocytes.

## 1. Three messengers

The two stress systems familiar from physiology carry the news of exercise to the immune system.
The sympathetic nervous system, which runs the fight-or-flight response, stimulates the adrenal glands to release adrenaline and noradrenaline.
These two are grouped together as the catecholamines.
The second system is the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, or H P A axis.
In it, the hypothalamus prompts the pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone, or A C T H.
This hormone in turn makes the adrenal cortex release cortisol, and this cortisol release is increased in exercise.
Cortisol is a glucocorticoid, one of the adrenal steroids named after their ability to raise blood glucose.
These hormones also act on immune cells, which carry receptors for them.
Immune cells have adrenergic receptors for adrenaline and noradrenaline, and glucocorticoid receptors for cortisol.
The third messenger does not come from the brain at all.
Cytokines, small signalling proteins, are released from immune cells in inflammation, and from skeletal muscle during activity.
The muscle-derived ones are called myokines.

## 2. The big picture

Walsh and colleagues, in two thousand and eleven, placed these messengers in a map of the possible mechanisms whereby exercise can affect immune responses.
The map shows four groups.
These are nervous and hormonal signals, a circulatory flush, other immune mediators, and changes in the immune cells themselves.
Exercise first acts on the brain.
The brain's sympathetic nerve fibres reach the heart and the adrenal medulla, which releases catecholamines.
The brain's neuroendocrine, or nerve-to-hormone, route is the H P A axis, which uses A C T H to make the adrenal cortex release cortisol.
The heart, for its part, raises cardiac output, and with it shear stress, the drag of flowing blood on vessel walls.
This causes demargination.
Leukocytes, or white blood cells, parked against the walls of vascular pools, are washed into the circulating blood.
These pools are perhaps the spleen, lung, liver and active muscles.
Other immune mediators act too, namely cytokines, chemokines and heat shock proteins.
Chemokines are cytokines that attract cells, and heat shock proteins are protective proteins that cells release when under strain.
All of these feed into the peripheral circulation.
There, they change cell trafficking, the movement of cells into and out of the blood.
They do so through adhesion molecules, surface proteins that make cells stick to one another, and through apoptosis, or programmed cell death.
Next comes the possible preferential mobilisation of cells with an altered effector phenotype.
This means that the cells swept into the blood are mainly those ready to act.
After that come their effector functions, microbial killing and cytokine expression.
Finally, tissue migration, or homing, carries cells to the lung mucosa, which is the airway lining, and to the skin and gut.
Exercise therefore changes both where immune cells are and what they do.

## 3. What the cell counts do during and after a hard bout

Pedersen and Toft, in two thousand, summarised the clearest result of all this, the change in blood cell counts with strenuous exercise.
Neutrophil concentration increases during and after exercise.
Lymphocytes increase during exercise, but fall to low levels after intense, long-duration exercise.
N K cells, the innate killers of infected cells, do the same.
Once exercise stops, the neutrophil and lymphocyte counts part company.
The dip of the lymphocyte count below its resting value is the open window.
It lasts no longer than twenty-four hours after exercise, with the most pronounced decrease two to six hours post-exercise.
The three to seventy-two hours quoted by the open window hypothesis is no contradiction.
That's because the window's length depends on the immune measure followed, and these data follow cell counts.

## 4. Where the cells come from and how they cross the wall

Where do these extra cells come from, and where do they go afterwards?
With exercise, cells are recruited into the circulation from the spleen, thymus, lymph nodes, bone marrow and lung.
Most of these are organs where immune cells are made, mature or are stationed.
And the lung is one of the vascular pools from the map.
Cells leave the blood again by transmigration through vessel walls into the tissues.
To leave, a cell swept along by the blood must first catch hold of the wall.
It does so with cell adhesion molecules, which work in pairs.
One on the lymphocyte binds a partner on the endothelial cells, the flat cells lining the vessel.
That is why cell adhesion molecules on lymphocytes and endothelial cells play a crucial role in lymphocyte trafficking.
The catecholamines act at exactly this point.
Lymphocytes express high numbers of beta two adrenergic receptors, and these are upregulated with exercise.
As a result, the cells carry even more of them just when adrenaline and noradrenaline are rising.
When the hormones activate these receptors, the lymphocyte increases its expression of cell adhesion molecules.
With more of them on its surface, it grips the vessel wall more readily.
Picture one T lymphocyte, carried by the blood over the endothelium.
An adhesion molecule called C D forty-four sits on both the lymphocyte and the endothelium.
On the endothelium, it holds hyaluronan, a long sugar chain.
The crossing then takes three steps.
In rolling, the lymphocyte's C D forty-four catches loosely on the hyaluronan.
The bonds are weak, so the blood keeps breaking and remaking them, and the cell tumbles along the wall like a ball across Velcro.
In adhesion, further adhesion molecules latch firmly onto the wall.
The cell stops and flattens, so the blood can no longer sweep it away.
In diapedesis, a Greek word meaning leaping through, the cell squeezes between two endothelial cells into the tissue.
This is the same three-step machinery that brings neutrophils into an inflamed tissue.

## Summary

In summary, exercise signals to immune cells through three routes.
Catecholamines act on adrenergic receptors, cortisol acts on glucocorticoid receptors, and cytokines come from immune cells and muscle.
Walsh and colleagues' map adds demargination by raised cardiac output.
It shows all these signals converging on cell trafficking, effector function, and homing to the lung mucosa, skin and gut.
After strenuous exercise, neutrophils rise during and after the bout.
Lymphocytes and N K cells, by contrast, rise and then fall to low levels.
This open window lasts no longer than twenty-four hours, and it is deepest at two to six hours.
The cells come from the spleen, thymus, lymph nodes, bone marrow and lung.
They leave the blood by rolling, adhesion and diapedesis, as neutrophils do in inflammation.
To do so, they use cell adhesion molecules, which catecholamines increase through upregulated beta two adrenergic receptors.
And that's how a hard bout moves immune cells around the body.
