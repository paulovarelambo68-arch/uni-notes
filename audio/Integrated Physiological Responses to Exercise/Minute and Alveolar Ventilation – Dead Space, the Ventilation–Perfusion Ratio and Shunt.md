## Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt

Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs.
Yet only the air that reaches the air sacs can load oxygen into the blood.
In this note, we'll count how much of the air we move each minute does useful work, and how that air is matched to the blood flowing past.
We'll end with what one bout of exercise asks of the lung.

## 1. Two ways to count breathing

We begin with ventilation, the moving of air in and out of the lungs.
Ventilation can be counted in two ways.
The first count includes all the air moved in and out of the whole respiratory tract in a minute.
The second count includes only the air that reaches the alveoli each minute.
The alveoli are the tiny air sacs where oxygen passes into the blood.
This second count is the one that matters for oxygenating the blood, since air that never reaches an alveolus cannot give up oxygen.
It is, however, hard to measure directly.
For that reason, the first count is the one usually measured, at the mouth.

## 2. Minute ventilation and its numbers

The first way of counting breathing is called minute ventilation, the air moved per minute.
Minute ventilation equals breathing rate times tidal volume.
Breathing rate is the number of breaths per minute, and tidal volume is the volume of one breath.
Like a bucket chain, breathing delivers more by passing more buckets, or by using bigger buckets.
At rest, about twelve breaths per minute, each of half a litre, give about six litres per minute.
In strenuous exercise, a healthy adult breathes thirty-five to forty-five times per minute, with breaths of two litres to two and a half litres.
That comes to about one hundred litres per minute.
Male endurance athletes reach about one hundred and sixty litres per minute, and some people go above two hundred litres per minute.
Both rate and depth rise to get there.
At the extreme, the rate reaches forty-eight to sixty breaths per minute, up from twelve to fifteen at rest.
Each of those breaths is two and a half litres or more.
Alongside these changes, a single bout of exercise shifts each of the lung volumes.

## 3. How trained athletes grow the breath first

Breathing faster and taking bigger breaths are the two ways of raising minute ventilation, and they are not used equally.
In moderate exercise, well-trained athletes keep up their alveolar ventilation, the air reaching the alveoli each minute, mainly by taking bigger breaths.
Their breathing rate rises only a little.
With rising effort, tidal volume levels off at about sixty per cent of forced vital capacity.
Forced vital capacity is the most air a person can force out after breathing in as deeply as possible.
Beyond the point where tidal volume levels off, minute ventilation keeps climbing, but only through a rising rate, without any conscious decision.
Let's look more closely at each breath.
Not all of a breath does useful work.
Minute ventilation is made up of two parts.
One is the air reaching the alveoli each minute, called alveolar ventilation.
The other is the air wasted in the airways on the way, called dead-space ventilation.
Minute ventilation equals alveolar ventilation plus dead-space ventilation.

## 4. Dead space and alveolar ventilation

The air wasted in the airways sits in the anatomic dead space.
It is made up of the conducting airways, where air travels without any gas exchange taking place.
The dead space holds from about one hundred and fifty up to two hundred millilitres of each breath, usually rounded to one hundred and fifty millilitres.
That air never reaches the alveoli.
Alveolar ventilation, by contrast, is the portion of inspired air that reaches the alveoli and takes part in gas exchange.
It sets the gas levels at the alveolar-capillary membrane, the thin wall between air and blood.
Of a five hundred millilitre resting breath, about three hundred and fifty millilitres reaches the alveoli and mixes with the air already there.
Alveolar ventilation is therefore found by taking the dead space away from the tidal volume, and multiplying by the breathing rate.
At rest, five hundred millilitres minus one hundred and fifty millilitres leaves three hundred and fifty millilitres per breath.
Multiplied by twelve breaths per minute, that gives about four point two litres per minute.
That's four point two out of the six litres per minute moved at the mouth.
The dead-space toll is paid on every breath, big or small, which makes fast shallow breathing a poor way to ventilate.
Suppose someone takes twenty-four breaths per minute, each of two hundred and fifty millilitres.
Minute ventilation is still six litres per minute.
Now, though, each breath loses one hundred and fifty millilitres and delivers only one hundred millilitres.
As a result, alveolar ventilation falls to only two point four litres per minute.
In other words, deeper breathing ventilates the alveoli better than faster breathing, since it pays the dead-space toll fewer times.
That is why trained athletes grow the breath before the rate.

## 5. Matching air to blood: the ventilation–perfusion ratio

Let's turn now to the blood that meets the air in the lungs.
Fresh air in the alveoli is only useful if blood flows past to collect the oxygen.
It follows that the air supply must match the blood supply.
Blood flowing through the lung's capillaries is called perfusion.
The match between air and blood is the ventilation-perfusion ratio, which is alveolar ventilation divided by pulmonary blood flow.
At rest in a healthy adult man, about four point two litres of air ventilates the alveoli each minute.
Over the same minute, about five litres of blood flows through the pulmonary capillaries.
That five litres is the whole cardiac output, because all the blood the heart pumps passes through the lungs.
The average ratio is thus about zero point eight four.
That works out at zero point eight four litres of fresh alveolar air for every litre of blood.
Why does the match matter?
If a region of the lung has too little air for its blood, it exchanges gas poorly and sends that blood on short of oxygen.
In light exercise, the ratio stays at about zero point eight, as ventilation and blood flow rise together.
In intense exercise, the ratio rises above five, driven by ventilation rising far more than blood flow.
For example, there could be one hundred litres per minute of alveolar ventilation against twenty litres per minute of blood.
The lung, then, has air to spare.
This is one reason the respiratory system itself is rarely the limiting factor in athletic performance.

## 6. Blood that misses the air: pulmonary shunt

The match between air and blood can also fail the other way, when blood passes through the lung without meeting fresh air.
A pulmonary shunt is blood that passes from the right side of the heart to the left without taking part in gas exchange.
Blood flowing past poorly ventilated alveoli leaves the lung without picking up oxygen.
Exercise can add to this.
As blood flow to the lungs rises, the higher pressure and faster flow can force vessels that were closed at rest to open.
Some of these are shortcut vessels that bypass the alveoli altogether, letting the blood in them pass through without picking up oxygen.
Shunted blood is still low in oxygen when it mixes with the oxygen-rich blood from well-ventilated alveoli.
Consequently, the oxygen pressure in arterial blood falls.
This pressure is known as the partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood.
At the same time, the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient widens.
This gradient is the difference between the oxygen pressure in alveolar air and in arterial blood.
A wider gap means the lung is transferring oxygen less well.
A large shunt is life-threatening in a patient who needs oxygen support.
The reason is that extra oxygen in the air cannot help blood that never meets the air.
Outside the lung, some congenital heart defects shunt blood through a hole in the heart.
In healthy exercise, though, the fall in blood oxygen seen in some athletes comes mostly from two things.
One is uneven matching of air to blood, and the other is blood rushing past the alveoli too fast.

## 7. What acute exercise asks of the lung

Everything we've covered comes together at the start of exercise.
The working muscles use more oxygen, which makes their blood vessels dilate, or widen, to let more blood through.
The muscles extract more oxygen from each litre of blood as well.
To supply them, cardiac output rises.
All of that blood passes through the lungs, so pulmonary blood flow rises by the same amount.
How does the lung handle all this extra blood?
Around each alveolus lies a net of capillaries.
In the normal resting lung, some of these vessels are open and others are closed.
The closed ones stay shut because blood pressure in the lung is low at rest, too low to push every vessel open.
Part of the exchange surface therefore goes unused.
The first way the lung copes is called recruitment.
Exercise raises blood flow, and the pressure becomes high enough to open the closed capillaries.
This spreads the blood over more of the alveolar wall.
Distension takes over next, once most vessels are already open and the pressure keeps rising.
The thin capillary walls stretch and the open capillaries widen, allowing each one to carry more blood.
Both recruitment and distension are normal, healthy responses to exercise.
They let the lung take several times its resting blood flow without a large rise in pressure in its blood vessels.
They also keep the blood from rushing past the air too fast to load oxygen.
Ventilation rises to match, keeping fresh air arriving beside all the new blood.
Once breathing and blood flow have caught up with the effort, the oxygen that the whole body uses each minute equals the oxygen that it takes from the air.
The oxygen taken from the air is the oxygen breathed in minus the oxygen breathed out.
This amount is called the oxygen uptake.

## Summary

To recap, minute ventilation is breathing rate times tidal volume.
It is about six litres per minute at rest and about one hundred litres per minute in strenuous exercise.
Endurance athletes reach one hundred and sixty litres per minute or more.
Each breath loses about one hundred and fifty millilitres to the dead space.
That leaves only about three hundred and fifty millilitres of a five hundred millilitre resting breath to reach the alveoli.
Alveolar ventilation is about four point two litres per minute.
The dead-space loss on every breath explains why deep breathing beats fast shallow breathing.
Trained athletes grow the breath first, until tidal volume levels off at about sixty per cent of forced vital capacity.
After that, the breathing rate takes over.
The ventilation-perfusion ratio is alveolar ventilation divided by pulmonary blood flow.
It is zero point eight four at rest, and it stays at about zero point eight in light exercise.
In intense exercise, ventilation rises far more than blood flow, which pushes the ratio above five.
A shunt is blood that crosses from the right side of the heart to the left without exchanging gas.
Blood that misses the air in this way lowers the partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood, and widens the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient.
In acute exercise, cardiac output and pulmonary blood flow rise.
The lung copes by recruiting closed capillaries and distending open ones, while ventilation rises to match.
And that's how much of each breath does useful work, and how the lung keeps that air matched to the blood flowing past.
