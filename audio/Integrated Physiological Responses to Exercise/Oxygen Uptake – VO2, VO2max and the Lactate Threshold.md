## Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold

Muscles burn oxygen to release energy, and the harder they work, the more they burn.
Here we'll see how that oxygen use is measured, and what its upper limit says about fitness.
We'll also see how much of that limit a person can really use.

## 1. Oxygen used equals oxygen taken in

Oxygen is used deep inside the muscles, yet it can be measured at the mouth.
Measuring oxygen use at the mouth works in submaximal steady state, meaning a constant effort below maximum that the body has settled into.
In that state, the body is neither storing up oxygen nor drawing on its stores.
Whatever oxygen the lungs take up, the tissues must therefore be using.
This balance between uptake and use is written as Q O two equals V O two.
Here, Q O two is oxygen utilisation by the tissues, and V O two is oxygen uptake at the lungs, also called oxygen consumption.
Likewise, the carbon dioxide the cells produce, called Q C O two, equals the carbon dioxide breathed out, called V C O two.
The lung is also assumed to be sealed except at the airway, so oxygen enters and carbon dioxide leaves only by the mouth or nose.
As a result, external respiration, the gas exchange measured at the mouth, represents internal respiration.
Internal respiration is the oxygen used and the carbon dioxide made by the cells.
This is why a mouthpiece can measure the oxygen the muscles use, which is the whole basis of fitness testing.
Measured at the mouth, V O two is about zero point two five litres per minute at rest.
In hard exercise, V O two rises to about three to four litres per minute in an untrained person.

## 2. Measuring VO2 from the air breathed

V O two is measured with a mouthpiece and a gas analyser, a method called open-circuit spirometry.
The body keeps some of the oxygen it inhales.
For that reason, V O two is the oxygen breathed in minus the oxygen breathed out, per unit of time.
As a formula, V O two equals the volume breathed in times its oxygen fraction, minus the volume breathed out times its oxygen fraction, all divided by the time.
Fresh air is twenty point nine per cent oxygen, giving it an oxygen fraction of zero point two zero nine.
Expired air holds about sixteen per cent oxygen at rest, since the body has kept some.
During exercise, expired air holds about twelve to fourteen per cent oxygen.
The volumes breathed in and out are nearly equal.
Suppose, then, that a resting person breathes six litres in and six litres out in one minute.
At twenty point nine per cent, that air brings in about one point two five litres of oxygen.
At sixteen per cent, the air breathed out takes about zero point nine six litres of oxygen back out.
The body keeps the difference, about zero point two nine litres per minute.
That is close to the typical resting V O two of zero point two five litres per minute.

## 3. The ceiling: VO2max and how it is tested

As exercise gets harder, V O two rises until it reaches a ceiling, called V O two max, or maximal oxygen uptake.
V O two max is the maximum capacity of the body to transport and use oxygen during incremental exercise, which gets harder in steps.
It is a standard measure of aerobic, or oxygen-powered, fitness.
It is given in litres per minute, or in millilitres per kilogram of body weight per minute.
A larger body uses more oxygen simply because it has more muscle.
That is why dividing by body weight lets athletes of different sizes be compared.
Roughly, ordinary young adults score thirty-five to forty-five millilitres per kilogram per minute.
Elite endurance athletes score roughly seventy to eighty-five millilitres per kilogram per minute.
V O two max is measured with the cardiopulmonary exercise test, or C P X test, a graded test in which the workload rises in steps.
It is run on a treadmill or on a cycle ergometer, which is a stationary exercise bike.
For example, the treadmill slope may rise every two minutes.
The slope steps up through zero, three, six and eight per cent, over eight and a half minutes.
Meanwhile, the volume of air breathed, called ventilation, is measured.
The oxygen and carbon dioxide in the inhaled and exhaled air are measured too.
The test must be long and hard enough to fully tax the aerobic system.
The reason is that V O two max is reached only at the plateau.
The plateau is the point where oxygen consumption stops rising, even though the workload keeps increasing.
Estimates from easier, submaximal tests are less accurate.
V O two max measures aerobic endurance potential.
Even so, it does not necessarily predict performance, given that long races are run below that ceiling.

## 4. How much of the ceiling you can use: the lactate threshold

Now let's look at how much of the V O two max ceiling a person can use.
The share of V O two max a person can sustain is marked by the lactate threshold.
Lactate is made whenever muscle breaks down sugar, or glucose, faster than oxygen can finish the job.
At low intensity, blood lactate stays at or near resting levels.
Other tissues, such as the heart, burn lactate for fuel, and in doing so they clear it as fast as it is made.
As intensity increases, there comes a break point where production outruns clearance, and blood lactate rises sharply.
This point, at which blood lactate accumulates above resting levels, is the onset of blood lactate accumulation, or O B L A.
It is also called the lactate threshold.
Strictly, though, the two names are often kept apart.
In that case, the lactate threshold is the first rise in blood lactate.
O B L A is then a slightly later point, when blood lactate reaches four millimoles per litre.
The break marks the shift from mainly aerobic to mainly anaerobic energy production.
Anaerobic production uses no oxygen, and it releases far less energy from each molecule of glucose.
The same break shows up in breathing as the ventilatory threshold, where breathing starts to outpace oxygen uptake.
On top of that, lactate comes with acid, which the body must neutralise.
In healthy untrained people, the lactate threshold occurs at fifty-five to sixty-five per cent of V O two max.
In highly trained endurance athletes, it can be at eighty per cent of V O two max or more.
The gap between the lactate threshold and V O two max is the room an athlete has to improve without changing V O two max.
Endurance training often moves the threshold higher without raising V O two max.
This happens because trained muscles make less lactate and clear it faster.
As a result, two people with the same V O two max can perform very differently.
The threshold predicts endurance performance better than V O two max does.

## Summary

To recap, in submaximal steady state the tissues use exactly the oxygen the lungs take up, which means Q O two, tissue oxygen use, equals V O two, lung oxygen uptake.
It follows that external respiration represents internal respiration.
V O two is the oxygen breathed in minus the oxygen breathed out, per unit time.
In formula terms, it equals the volume breathed in times its oxygen fraction, minus the volume breathed out times its oxygen fraction, divided by the time.
At rest, it is about zero point two five litres per minute.
V O two max is the maximum capacity of the body to transport and use oxygen.
It is given in litres per minute or, for comparing people of different sizes, in millilitres per kilogram per minute.
Measured with a graded C P X test, it is reached at the plateau, when oxygen consumption stops rising although the workload keeps increasing.
The lactate threshold, or O B L A, is the break point where lactate production outruns clearance and blood lactate starts to climb.
It lies at fifty-five to sixty-five per cent of V O two max in untrained people, and at eighty per cent or more in trained endurance athletes.
Training can raise it without raising V O two max.
What's more, it predicts endurance performance better than V O two max does.
And that's how the oxygen the muscles burn is measured at the mouth, what its ceiling tells us, and how much of that ceiling a person can really use.
