## Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve

Every cell needs a steady supply of oxygen, and working muscles need far more.
A protein in the blood carries that oxygen from the lungs to wherever it is needed.
In this note, we'll see why dissolved oxygen falls short, how haemoglobin is built, and how it loads and unloads oxygen.

## 1. Why Dissolved Oxygen Cannot Supply the Body

We begin with dissolved oxygen, and why it cannot supply the body on its own.
Oxygen dissolves poorly in water, so plasma, the watery part of blood, can hold very little of it.
Only about one point five per cent of the blood's oxygen travels dissolved.
The other ninety-eight point five per cent is bound to haemoglobin, a protein in the red blood cells.
How much oxygen dissolves depends on its partial pressure, known as the P O two.
The P O two is the pressure exerted by oxygen on its own, and it is measured in millimetres of mercury.
For every millimetre of mercury of P O two, one hundred millilitres of blood holds only zero point zero zero three millilitres of dissolved oxygen.
Blood leaving the lungs has a P O two of about one hundred millimetres of mercury.
Each hundred millilitres of this blood therefore holds just zero point three millilitres of dissolved oxygen.
That comes to three millilitres per litre.
Small as it is, the dissolved oxygen does a job that bound oxygen cannot.
Oxygen bound to haemoglobin exerts no pressure, which is why it counts as hidden.
It follows that the dissolved oxygen alone sets the P O two in blood and tissue fluid.
And what drives diffusion is the difference in P O two from one place to another, not the amount of oxygen dissolved.
Dissolved oxygen is also the doorway into and out of haemoglobin.
In the lungs, oxygen first dissolves in the plasma, and only then does it enter the red cells.
Haemoglobin takes up this dissolved oxygen almost as soon as it arrives, keeping the plasma from ever filling up with it.
As a result, the plasma P O two stays below the P O two of the air in the lungs, and oxygen keeps diffusing in.
The diffusion continues until haemoglobin is nearly full.
At that point, the plasma P O two catches up with the P O two of the air, and the flow stops.
As a way of carrying oxygen, though, the dissolved form falls far short.
At rest, the heart pumps about five litres of blood a minute, which is the cardiac output.
With three millilitres in each litre of blood, dissolved oxygen alone would deliver only fifteen millilitres of oxygen a minute.
But the resting body uses about two hundred and fifty millilitres of oxygen a minute.
That is why the blood needs a carrier.

## 2. Haemoglobin – Carrying Capacity, Structure and the Route of Oxygen

The blood's oxygen carrier is haemoglobin, an iron-containing protein that binds oxygen loosely.
Each gram of haemoglobin binds about one point three four millilitres of oxygen.
Men have about fifteen to sixteen grams of haemoglobin in each hundred millilitres of blood, enough to carry about twenty millilitres of oxygen.
Compared with the zero point three millilitres that dissolves, haemoglobin raises the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity about seventy times.
Women have between five and ten per cent less haemoglobin, about fourteen grams per hundred millilitres.
The difference comes from testosterone, which boosts the production of erythropoietin, or E P O, a hormone made by the kidneys.
E P O drives the production of red blood cells in the bone marrow.
The process of making red blood cells is called erythropoiesis.
More red cells carry more oxygen.
Training at altitude raises E P O as well.
Now to the haemoglobin molecule itself, which is made of four protein chains called globins, two alpha and two beta.
Each chain holds a small, flat group called a haem.
At the centre of each haem sits one iron atom, in its iron two plus form.
The iron acts like a magnet for one oxygen molecule, which gives each haemoglobin four seats for four oxygen molecules.
The bond is weak, fully reversible, and leaves the haem unchanged.
This lets oxygen load in the lungs and come off in the tissues.
Because the four chains are packed tightly together, a change of shape in one is passed on to the others.
Each red blood cell holds about two hundred and eighty million haemoglobin molecules.
This means one red cell can carry more than a billion oxygen molecules.
Haemoglobin also gives blood its colour.
Blood is bright red when its haemoglobin is loaded with oxygen, and dark red when it is not.
Arterial blood is therefore bright and venous blood dark, except in the pulmonary artery and veins, where it is the other way round.
Let's follow oxygen's route from lung to tissue.
In the lungs, oxygen from the alveolus, the lung's tiny air sac, first dissolves in the plasma.
Next, it enters the red blood cell to form oxyhaemoglobin, which is haemoglobin loaded with oxygen.
In the tissues, the route runs backwards.
Oxygen moves through the plasma and the fluid around the cells to the mitochondria, the cells' power stations.
At both ends, oxygen passes through the dissolved form, making dissolved oxygen the go-between.

## 3. Cooperativity – Four Seats That Help Each Other

Let's see how haemoglobin's four seats work together.
They do not fill independently.
When the first oxygen binds to empty haemoglobin, known as deoxyhaemoglobin, the whole molecule changes shape slightly.
This shift in shape is a conformational change.
The new shape grips oxygen more strongly, meaning it has a higher affinity.
Thanks to this, the second oxygen binds more easily, and the third and fourth more easily still.
This teamwork is called co-operativity.
Since binding at one site changes how the other sites behave, the change of shape is called an allosteric change.
Co-operativity works in reverse, too.
Once oxyhaemoglobin, the oxygen-loaded form, releases its first oxygen, the others leave more easily.
Besides oxygen, haemoglobin carries some carbon dioxide.
As haemoglobin loads oxygen in the lungs, it drives that carbon dioxide off, which is called the Haldane effect.
Above all, co-operativity makes the oxygen held by haemoglobin follow an S shape, called sigmoid, as P O two rises.
The S shape is what lets haemoglobin fill almost completely in the lungs, and still release a lot of oxygen in the tissues.
Without co-operativity, the shape would simply bend over gradually.
In that case, a carrier that loaded well in the lungs would hold on to its oxygen too tightly in the tissues.

## 4. The Oxygen Dissociation Curve and What Its Shape Does

The S-shaped relationship between P O two and the oxygen held by haemoglobin is called the oxygen dissociation curve.
It links the surrounding P O two to haemoglobin's percentage saturation, which is the share of its seats holding oxygen.
As P O two rises, saturation climbs steeply up to a P O two of about fifty millimetres of mercury.
After that, it climbs more slowly, and finally it levels off.
Two reference values are worth knowing.
In the systemic arteries, which carry blood from the heart to the body, haemoglobin is ninety-eight per cent saturated at one hundred millimetres of mercury.
In the systemic veins, which return blood from the tissues, it is still seventy-five per cent saturated at forty millimetres of mercury.
The difference between these two saturations is the oxygen the tissues take at rest.
Arterial and venous blood sit on different parts of the S shape.
Arterial blood sits on the flat top, where saturation has levelled off.
That flat top acts as a safety margin.
Saturation stays high even when the P O two in the lungs falls quite a lot.
At sixty millimetres of mercury, for instance, it is still about ninety per cent.
The safety margin protects the loading of oxygen at altitude.
The tissues sit on the steep middle of the S shape, where a small fall in P O two releases a lot of oxygen.
At rest, they take only about a quarter of the load, and the remaining oxygen returns to the heart as a reserve.
Because working muscle uses oxygen fast, its P O two falls further down the steep middle, and the blood hands over much more of that reserve.
In addition, conditions in working muscle shift the whole oxygen dissociation curve, in a way that makes haemoglobin let go of oxygen more easily.

## Summary

To recap, oxygen dissolves poorly, so only one point five per cent of the blood's oxygen travels dissolved.
Yet that small share alone sets the P O two that drives diffusion.
The rest rides on haemoglobin, whose four globin chains each hold a haem.
Each haem has an iron atom that binds one oxygen molecule reversibly.
Haemoglobin binds one point three four millilitres of oxygen per gram.
With about fifteen grams in every hundred millilitres of blood in men, it raises carrying capacity about seventy times.
Each oxygen that binds changes haemoglobin's shape, making the next one bind more easily.
This co-operativity gives the oxygen dissociation curve its S shape.
Haemoglobin is ninety-eight per cent saturated at one hundred millimetres of mercury in the arteries.
In the veins, it is still seventy-five per cent saturated at forty millimetres of mercury.
The flat top of this S shape keeps arterial blood saturated when P O two falls.
The steep middle releases much more oxygen when working muscle lowers its P O two.
And that's how haemoglobin, with dissolved oxygen as its go-between, carries oxygen from the lungs to wherever the body needs it.
