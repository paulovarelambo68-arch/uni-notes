## Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate

Pacemaker cells fire by themselves, since their voltage climbs to threshold instead of resting.
The fastest of them lie in the sinoatrial node, or S A node.
They fire about one hundred times a minute and set the heart rate.
Sympathetic nerves raise that rate, and the vagus lowers it.
A transplanted heart rests near one hundred beats per minute, but it still speeds up in exercise.
In this note, we'll see how the S A node fires on its own, how the nerves change its pace, and what happens when those nerves are cut.

## 1. The excitation-conduction system

We begin with the excitation-conduction system, and with the fact that the heart starts its own beats.
Excitation, the electrical signal that triggers each contraction, is generated within the heart by pacemaker cells.
These are specialised muscle cells that fire with no outside trigger.
Ordinary heart muscle cells sit at a steady resting voltage until something excites them.
Pacemaker cells, on the other hand, have unstable resting potentials.
After each impulse, their voltage climbs again until it reaches threshold, the level at which the cell fires.
They belong to the excitation-conduction system, which spreads each beat so that the chambers contract in a coordinated order.
The signal begins in the sinoatrial node, or S A node, and travels on through the atrioventricular node, or A V node.
From there it passes through the bundle of His and the left and right bundle branches to the Purkinje fibres.

## 2. The SA node action potential

Let's turn to the S A node, whose cells show how a pacemaker fires by itself.
Their action potential has only three phases, numbered four, zero and three.
In phase four, the voltage climbs slowly from about minus sixty millivolts.
Phase zero is a small upstroke to about plus twenty millivolts, and phase three is the fall.
Unlike a ventricular cell, an S A node cell has no flat resting level and no plateau.
Phase four, the climb, is called the pre-potential, or pacemaker potential.
It starts at about minus sixty to minus seventy millivolts, and it rises for two reasons.
First, a special channel lets sodium leak into the cell.
This inward sodium current is called the funny current, or I F, where the I stands for current.
Second, the membrane's permeability to potassium gradually falls.
With less potassium leaking out, the potassium current falls, and the funny current increasingly wins.
In the last third of the climb, calcium starts flowing in too, adding a calcium current.
Phase zero begins at threshold.
Cells of the S A node and the A V node lack working fast sodium channels, the channels that give ordinary muscle its sharp upstroke.
Their upstroke is therefore slow and small.
It is carried mainly by calcium flowing in, as calcium channels open and the membrane becomes more permeable to this ion.
An outward potassium current partly opposes it.
In phase three, the potassium current brings the voltage back down, and the climb begins again.

## 3. Autonomic control of heart rate

Now for autonomic control of heart rate, which works through that climb.
Because the cell fires whenever its climb reaches threshold, the steepness of phase four sets the heart rate.
This steepness is what the nerves adjust.
Left alone, the S A node fires one hundred times a minute.
The resting rate, though, is sixty to seventy, and heavy exercise can take it to one hundred and eighty.
The vagus nerve, which is parasympathetic, slows the node, while the sympathetic cardiac nerves speed it up.
A resting rate below one hundred shows that the vagus brakes the node constantly, and this is called vagal tone.
Sympathetic nerves steepen the climb, so threshold comes sooner and the node fires more often.
Their transmitter, noradrenaline, binds beta one adrenergic receptors on the node.
This raises cyclic A M P, a second messenger inside the cell.
Cyclic A M P acts in two ways.
It boosts the funny current directly, making the membrane more permeable to sodium and letting more sodium in.
It also activates protein kinase A, an enzyme that phosphorylates calcium channels, which is to say it adds a phosphate to them.
In turn, the membrane becomes more permeable to calcium, and more calcium enters.
A faster heart needs shorter beats as well, since each beat must end before the next one starts.
For that reason, protein kinase A phosphorylates potassium channels too, in the pumping muscle cells, where the same signalling chain strengthens contraction.
The potassium current rises and the action potential shortens, which stops its long duration from limiting the heart rate.
At the S A node, the vagus does the opposite of what sympathetic nerves do.
When it is briefly stimulated, the node's voltage changes in two ways.
At first, the voltage dips, which is hyperpolarisation.
That dip stretches the gap between beats from seven hundred to one thousand two hundred and fifty milliseconds.
After that, the voltage climbs more gently, with a reduced slope, which keeps the gaps near nine hundred milliseconds.
Both changes delay threshold, which is why the heart slows.
Behind these changes is acetylcholine, which the vagus releases onto muscarinic M two receptors.
Acetylcholine opens acetylcholine-sensitive potassium channels, which raises the membrane's potassium permeability.
More potassium flows out, and the voltage drops lower.
Acetylcholine also lowers cyclic A M P, which weakens the sympathetic effect and reduces the funny current and the calcium current, flattening the climb.
For exams, the effect of acetylcholine is an increased potassium current in the S A node.
It never raises cyclic A M P, calcium entry or the slope of the climb, in either the S A node or the A V node.
Nor does it ever depolarise the node.
Comparing the two nerves side by side brings out one more difference.
The vagus makes the starting voltage of the climb more negative, whereas sympathetic nerves leave it unchanged.

## 4. Why the SA node is the pacemaker, and the heart without nerves

Last of all, why is the S A node the pacemaker, and what does a heart do without its nerves?
As the fastest pacemaker, the S A node sets the pace, which is why the nerves act on it.
Every part of the conduction system can fire on its own.
The S A node fires about one hundred times a minute, the A V node forty to sixty times, and the Purkinje fibres twenty to forty.
Impulses from the S A node thus fire the slower cells before their own climb reaches threshold.
If those impulses cannot reach the ventricles, as in complete A V block, a slower pacemaker lower down takes over.
The ventricles then beat slowly.
A transplanted heart has had its nerves cut, which means it shows the S A node working alone.
With no vagal brake, it rests at about one hundred beats per minute.
In exercise, its rate still rises, but later and by less.
The reason is that only adrenaline from the adrenal glands can reach its beta one receptors, through the blood, and that takes time.
Meanwhile, the heart pumps more per beat, as extra returning blood stretches the ventricle and makes it contract harder.
The baroreflex, which steadies blood pressure through these nerves, cannot change a transplanted heart's rate.

## Summary

To recap, pacemaker cells have unstable resting potentials, with a voltage that climbs to threshold after every impulse.
In this way, the heart makes its own beats, led by the fastest pacemaker, the S A node, at about one hundred times a minute when left alone.
The S A node's action potential has only phases four, zero and three.
The climb is driven by the funny sodium current and a falling potassium current.
The small upstroke is carried by calcium, because node cells lack fast sodium channels.
The potassium current then brings the voltage back down.
The steepness of the climb sets the heart rate, and vagal tone holds it at sixty to seventy at rest.
Noradrenaline, acting through beta one receptors and more cyclic A M P, steepens the climb by boosting the funny current and the calcium current.
Acetylcholine, acting through M two receptors, increases the potassium current, which hyperpolarises the node, and lowers cyclic A M P, which flattens the climb.
A transplanted heart rests near one hundred beats per minute, and in exercise it speeds up later and less, through circulating adrenaline.
And that's how the heart sets its own pace, and how its two nerves change it.
