## Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling’s Law and Contractility

Cardiac muscle contracts more strongly when it is stretched, up to an optimum length.
As a result, a larger end-diastolic volume gives a larger stroke volume, which is Starling's law.
Each beat traces an anticlockwise pressure-volume loop, with the valve events at its corners and the stroke volume as its width.
Contractility, the stroke volume for a given end-diastolic volume, is raised by sympathetic activity.
It is measured as d P d t max, the fastest rise of ventricular pressure.
Over the coming sections, we'll go from length and tension in heart muscle to the loop and Starling's law, ending with what contractility means and how it is measured.

## 1. Length and tension in cardiac muscle

Our starting point is that stroke volume, the volume of blood ejected in a single beat, depends on how far the ventricular muscle is stretched before it contracts.
The muscle's force comes from sarcomeres, the contractile units between zed lines, which are discs anchoring the thin actin filaments.
Inside each sarcomere, cross-bridges of the thick myosin filaments pull actin inwards.
This is the sliding-filament mechanism.
A strip of cardiac muscle, held at fixed lengths and stimulated, shows how that force varies with length.
Measured at each length, its tension has two parts, passive and active.
Passive tension, the elastic recoil of the stretched resting muscle, rises steeply at long lengths.
Active tension, the extra force of cross-bridge cycling, rises with length to an optimum and then falls.
Total tension is the sum of the two.
What happens inside the sarcomeres explains the peak of active tension.
At the optimum length, the overlap between the filaments allows the most cross-bridges.
Stretch the muscle well beyond it, and few myosin heads reach actin.
Resting length sits just below the optimum, and a slight stretch therefore raises total tension.
The key idea is that this ability to increase contractile force with a slight increase in length underlies the heart's ability to control stroke volume.
Past the optimum, an over-stretched muscle, as in a dilated heart, produces less active tension.
That is why an overly dilated heart is not a good pump.

## 2. The pressure–volume loop

Now for the pressure-volume loop, which takes the left ventricle's pressure and volume traces from the Wiggers diagram and plots pressure against volume.
Each beat thus traces one anticlockwise loop.
This loop usually carries more than one exam question.
Its two vertical sides are the isovolumetric phases, when all the valves are shut, so the volume cannot change.
Pressure changes all the same, because contracting means developing tension, not necessarily shortening.
With the blood trapped and incompressible, the fibres stay at the same length.
It is like gripping a capped bottle hard without your fingers moving.
As a result, the fibres' rising tension becomes rising pressure.
When they relax, tension and pressure fall together, still at constant volume.
Ejection cannot begin until pressure reaches about eighty millimetres of mercury, since the aortic valve opens only when ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure.
It shuts when ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure.
Along the bottom of the loop runs the passive compliance curve.
It is the same passive tension we met in the muscle strip, now seen in the whole ventricle.
Compliance means how easily the relaxed ventricle stretches as it fills.
The left-hand side of the loop marks the end-systolic volume, and the right-hand side the end-diastolic volume.
The width of the loop is the stroke volume, the end-diastolic volume minus the end-systolic volume, which in this loop is eighty millilitres.
The area inside the loop is the stroke work of one beat.
The exam shows this same loop with letters in place of labels, and asks which letter marks a given event.
Going anticlockwise from the bottom left, the letters run as follows.
Point A is the bottom-left corner, where the mitral, or atrioventricular, valve opens and filling begins.
Point B lies along the bottom, partway through filling.
Point C is the bottom-right corner, where the mitral valve closes and systole begins.
Point D is the top of the right-hand side, where the aortic valve opens and ejection begins.
Point E is the top of the loop, the highest pressure during ejection.
Point F is the top-left corner, where the aortic valve closes.
Two of these answers are easy to get wrong.
The second heart sound is heard at point F, as it is the sound of the aortic and pulmonary valves closing.
Valves make no sound when they open, and the first heart sound, from the atrioventricular valves closing, is at point C.
Systole begins at point C, not at point D, because the isovolumetric contraction between points C and D is already systole.
Atrial contraction, at the very end of filling just before point C, still counts as diastole.
A second loop comes from Bray's textbook, and uses slightly larger volumes.
It has its own letters, which are not the exam's.
In Bray's loop, filling runs from point A to point B, and the aortic valve opens at point C and closes at point D.
Its stroke volume is one hundred and thirty minus sixty millilitres, which is seventy millilitres.
That gives an ejection fraction, the stroke volume divided by the end-diastolic volume, of about fifty-four per cent.
Around this loop, two curves give the range of pressure the ventricle can have at each volume.
The lower one, the diastolic curve, is the ventricle's pressure when relaxed, so filling always runs along it.
The upper one, the maximum systolic curve, is the most pressure it can produce if the aortic valve stays shut.
At one hundred and thirty millilitres, that maximum is point C star, far above the eighty millimetres of mercury needed at Bray's point C to open the aortic valve.

## 3. Starling's law of the heart

Starling's law takes what we saw for length and tension in heart muscle and applies it to the whole heart.
Preload is the stretched condition of the heart muscle at the end of diastole, and it is set by the end-diastolic volume.
The law itself is a chain.
More venous return, the blood returning to the heart, raises the end-diastolic volume, which raises preload, which raises stroke volume.
On the pressure-volume loop, a larger end-diastolic volume moves the right-hand side further right, giving a wider loop that ejects more.
Needing no nerve or hormone, the law keeps the outputs of the two ventricles equal, beat by beat.
If the right ventricle pumps a little more, the left ventricle's end-diastolic volume rises, and it pumps more too.
A transplanted heart, whose nerves have been cut, relies on this law first when venous return rises in exercise.
Preload is the load on the muscle before it contracts, whereas afterload is the load it contracts against.
Afterload is the pressure the ventricle must exceed to eject, in practice the aortic diastolic pressure.
That is why the aortic valve opens at about eighty millimetres of mercury.
A higher afterload, as in hypertension, means the right-hand side of the loop must climb higher before ejection can begin.
Plotting stroke volume against end-diastolic volume gives the ventricular function curve, or Starling curve.
At rest, the heart sits at a stroke volume of about seventy millilitres, for an end-diastolic volume of one hundred and thirty to one hundred and forty millilitres.
An increase in end-diastolic volume moves the heart up the curve and increases stroke volume.
A decrease moves it down and lowers stroke volume.
The curve rises, then flattens as the fibres approach their optimum length.
Two statements about this curve are worth keeping in mind.
One is that contractility is the stroke volume for a given end-diastolic volume.
The other is that every point on the Starling curve has the same contractility.

## 4. Contractility: what it actually means

Those two statements are the key to what contractility actually means, and the idea behind them is simple.
There are two ways to make the heart eject more blood per beat.
The first is to fill it more, which stretches the muscle and moves the heart along the same Starling curve.
The second is to make it contract more strongly with the same filling, which lifts the heart onto a higher curve.
Only this second way is a rise in contractility.
Seen this way, the stroke volume for a given end-diastolic volume measures how strongly the muscle contracts.
Moving along one curve changes only the filling, whereas a change in contractility moves the heart to a different curve.
Put precisely, contractility is the force the muscle develops at a given length, in other words, at a given preload.
It is a property of the muscle itself, independent of preload, how much the heart is filled, and of afterload, the pressure it pumps against.
For the same reason, more venous return is not an inotropic effect, meaning one that changes contractility.
It only fills the heart more, raising stroke volume by stretch, along the same curve.
On the pressure-volume loop, a more contractile ventricle empties further, to a smaller end-systolic volume from the same end-diastolic volume.
The loop therefore widens to the left.
Back on the Starling curves, each level of sympathetic activity has its own curve.
At the same end-diastolic volume of one hundred and thirty millilitres, stroke volume rises from about fifty to about one hundred and ten millilitres as sympathetic activity increases.
Sympathetic activity thus increases contractility, since a higher curve gives a higher stroke volume for the same end-diastolic volume.

## 5. Measuring contractility

Finally, how is contractility measured?
As stroke volume also depends on preload and afterload, contractility is measured when neither can interfere.
During isovolumetric contraction, ventricular pressure climbs from its filling level to about eighty millimetres of mercury.
A stronger heart makes it climb faster.
The fastest rate of that climb is called d P d t max, meaning the change in pressure per change in time.
It is a good measure of myocardial contractility.
With all valves shut, fibre length is fixed, and no blood is yet being pushed out against aortic pressure.
That leaves the speed of the rise mainly reflecting how strongly the muscle contracts.
On a pressure trace, d P d t max is the slope of the steepest part of the upstroke.
The slope is steeper in a hyperdynamic, or more forceful, heart, as with noradrenaline, and shallower in a failing one.
The sympathetic effect on contractility is one arm of the baroreflex.

## Summary

To sum up, active tension peaks at an optimum length just above resting length.
Hence a slight stretch raises force, while an overly dilated heart is not a good pump.
The anticlockwise pressure-volume loop fills from forty to one hundred and twenty millilitres.
It ejects once pressure passes about eighty millimetres of mercury, and peaks near one hundred and twenty millimetres of mercury.
On the exam's version, the atrioventricular valves open at point A, and systole begins at point C.
At point F, the aortic valve closes, with the second heart sound.
Preload, set by the end-diastolic volume, moves the heart along a Starling curve.
The curve's resting point is a stroke volume of about seventy millilitres, at an end-diastolic volume of one hundred and thirty to one hundred and forty millilitres.
Contractility moves the heart to another curve, as sympathetic activity does, and it is measured as d P d t max.
And there you have the working heart, from one stretched strip of muscle to the whole pressure-volume loop.
