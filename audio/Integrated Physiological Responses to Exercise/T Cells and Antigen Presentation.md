## T Cells and Antigen Presentation

T cells must be introduced to antigen by antigen-presenting cells.
These are dendritic cells, macrophages and neutrophils.
They phagocytose a microbe and display its fragments on the major histocompatibility complex, presenting them to highly specific T cell receptors.
This is how the innate branch instructs the adaptive branch.
Once activated, helper T cells release cytokines, while cytotoxic, or killer, T cells release perforin and granzyme, and regulatory T cells release interleukin ten.
Coming up, we'll see why T cells need this introduction, and after that what each of the three kinds does once it is switched on.

## 1. T cells need an introduction

Let's begin with how a T cell, or T lymphocyte, recognises antigen.
It does so with its T cell receptors, surface proteins that are highly specific, each one fitting a single antigen only.
Yet a T cell receptor cannot recognise a free microbe.
It only recognises a fragment displayed by another cell.
That is why, for pathogen recognition, T cells need to be introduced to antigen by an antigen-presenting cell.
The antigen-presenting cells are dendritic cells, macrophages and neutrophils.
Each of them engulfs the pathogen by phagocytosis and digests it.
Then it uses the major histocompatibility complex, or M H C, to display fragments of the digested antigen on its surface.
M H C is a family of surface proteins that work like display stands.
Each one holds up a small piece of antigen to T cells, and that piece is called a peptide.
And it is this antigen, presented by M H C, that the T cell receptor recognises.
Helper T cells are switched on in this way, and so are the other types of T cell.
Because antigen-presenting cells are innate cells and T cells are adaptive ones, this hand-over is how the innate branch instructs the adaptive branch.
It also explains why the dendritic cell is described as the cell that detects pathogens and presents them to lymphocytes.
From there, the activated T cell multiplies into a clone by clonal selection.

## 2. Three kinds of T cell

What the clone does next depends on which of the three main types of T cell it belongs to.
First, helper T cells produce cytokines, which are small signalling proteins.
These cytokines increase the activity of B cells, macrophages and other T cells, so helper T cells are the organisers.
Since macrophages are innate cells, this help reaches back into the innate branch.
Where helper T cells organise, cytotoxic, or killer, T cells kill foreign cells or infected host cells.
They do it by releasing perforin and granzyme, the same weapons that natural killer cells use.
The difference is that a cytotoxic T cell is specific for one antigen and must first be presented with it.
A natural killer cell, on the other hand, needs neither.
Killer T cells kill by direct contact, and they also target body cells that turn cancerous.
The third type works in reverse, because regulatory T cells suppress the immune response.
They produce the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin ten, which calms immune cells rather than stirring them up.
Put simply, regulatory T cells are the brakes.

## Summary

To sum up, T cell receptors are highly specific, but they cannot recognise a free microbe.
For that reason, antigen-presenting cells, namely dendritic cells, macrophages and neutrophils, phagocytose it and present its fragments on M H C.
This is how the innate branch instructs the adaptive branch, and the activated T cell then multiplies by clonal selection.
Helper T cells release cytokines that boost B cells, macrophages and other T cells.
Cytotoxic, or killer, T cells kill foreign, infected or cancerous cells with perforin and granzyme.
Lastly, regulatory T cells suppress the response with the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin ten.
And that is T cells, from their introduction to antigen to the three jobs they go on to do.
