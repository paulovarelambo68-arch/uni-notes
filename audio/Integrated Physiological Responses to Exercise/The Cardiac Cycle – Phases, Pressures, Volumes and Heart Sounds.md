## The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds

A cardiac cycle is one contraction of the ventricles, called systole, and one relaxation, called diastole.
At rest, systole lasts zero point three four seconds and diastole zero point six six seconds.
Valves that open and close passively divide the cycle into five steps.
Each ventricle fills to about one hundred and thirty millilitres and empties to about sixty, ejecting seventy millilitres.
These volumes are the same on both sides, with the right side working at lower pressure.
Closure of the atrioventricular, or A V, valves makes the first heart sound, known as S one.
Closure of the semilunar valves makes the second, S two.
We'll meet the valves, time the cycle, follow its five steps and compare both sides, before finishing with the heart sounds, the electrocardiogram and murmurs.

## 1. The pump and its valves

Let's start with the pump itself and its valves.
The heart is a muscular organ that sits inside the pericardium, its enclosing sac.
Its wall of cardiac muscle cells is called the myocardium.
The heart has a right and a left side, each with an atrium and a ventricle.
Its valves are held in a fibrotendinous ring.
Between each atrium and its ventricle lies an atrioventricular valve, the tricuspid valve on the right and the mitral valve on the left.
The free margins of each valve's cusps are tied by tendinous cords, the chordae tendineae, to the papillary muscles.
These are projections of the ventricular muscle.
When the ventricle contracts, the papillary muscles contract with it and pull the cords taut.
That prevents eversion, which is to say it stops the cusps being forced back into the atria.
As a result, blood can pass only from atrium to ventricle.
The semilunar valves are the aortic valve, leading from the left ventricle to the aorta, and the pulmonary valve, from the right ventricle to the pulmonary artery.
They let blood into the arteries in systole, when the ventricles contract, and stop back-flow in diastole, when they relax.
All the valves open and close passively, on the pressure difference across them.
Together, they make flow through the heart one-way.

## 2. What a cardiac cycle is

Now for the timing of a single cycle.
Its length in seconds is simply sixty divided by the heart rate in beats per minute.
A heart rate of sixty beats per minute therefore gives a cycle of one second, and a rate of one hundred and eighty gives zero point three three seconds.
A rate of one hundred and twenty per minute gives zero point five seconds.
A cycle of only zero point two five seconds would need two hundred and forty beats per minute.
Between rest and the maximum rate, diastole loses zero point five three seconds, but systole only zero point one four seconds.
The reason is that the two phases are timed differently.
Systole lasts roughly as long as the ventricular action potential and the contraction it triggers, and these shorten only modestly as the rate rises.
Diastole, by contrast, is simply the time left before the next beat begins.
Most of the time saved by beating faster thus comes out of diastole.
Since diastole is when the ventricles fill, this costs filling time.
Little is lost at first, as most filling happens quickly in early diastole.
At the highest rates, though, the ventricle fills less, and so it ejects less with each beat.

## 3. Diastole in three steps

Our walk through the cycle starts with diastole, which has three steps.
Diastole begins with isovolumetric, or isometric, ventricular relaxation.
This is a very short phase, lasting zero point zero five seconds, in which all the valves are shut.
That leaves the relaxing ventricle with no flow and no change in volume, and hence none in fibre length.
The two names say just that, isovolumetric referring to the unchanging volume, and isometric to the unchanging fibre length.
The ventricle's volume is at its minimum here, the end-systolic volume, or E S V.
Atrial pressure is still lower than ventricular pressure.
Returning blood, however, fills the atrium until its pressure exceeds ventricular pressure.
At that point the atrioventricular valve opens passively, and ventricular filling begins.
Filling lasts about zero point five seconds at rest, and about eighty per cent of it is passive, down the pressure gradient.
Late in diastole, the atria depolarise, and this is the P wave of the electrocardiogram, or E C G.
The atria then contract, raising atrial pressure to about five millimetres of mercury and pushing in the final twenty per cent.
Ventricular volume peaks at the end-diastolic volume, or E D V, which is about one hundred and thirty millilitres at rest.
C V S six uses one hundred and forty millilitres instead.
Coming at the end of filling, atrial contraction is the last event of diastole, not the start of systole.

## 4. Systole in two steps

Systole follows, in two steps.
It starts as the ventricles depolarise and contract, their depolarisation marked by the Q R S complex.
Their tension raises left ventricular pressure above atrial pressure, forcing the mitral valve to close rapidly.
This closure gives the first heart sound, S one, and begins isovolumetric, or isometric, contraction, which lasts zero point zero five seconds.
With the atrioventricular and semilunar valves all closed, the volume cannot change.
Pressure therefore rises steeply, and never falls, until it can overcome aortic pressure.
The second step, ejection, lasts zero point three seconds.
It starts when left ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure and the aortic valve opens.
The opening of the semilunar valves is what marks the start of ejection.
Blood entering the aorta raises arterial volume and pressure.
Left ventricular and aortic pressures rise in parallel, to a maximum at mid-systole.
About two-thirds of the blood leaves in the first third of ejection, a stage called rapid ejection.
In late systole, known as reduced ejection, both pressures fall.
The ventricles repolarise, which is the T wave, and volume reaches the end-systolic volume.
Thus, isovolumetric contraction, at zero point zero five seconds, is far shorter than ejection, at zero point three seconds.

## 5. Closing the loop

To close the loop, the semilunar valves shut when ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure.
Their closure gives the second heart sound, S two, which has an aortic and a pulmonary component, known by the letters A and P.
This closure marks the end of systole and the beginning of diastole.
From here the cycle re-enters isovolumetric relaxation, at an end-systolic volume of about sixty millilitres.
Followed round the whole cycle, ventricular volume is maximal at the end-diastolic volume.
It falls during ejection to its minimum at the end-systolic volume, and it stays constant in both isovolumetric phases.
Ventricular pressure is lowest at the start of diastole, and rises a little with filling and atrial contraction.
It rises dramatically in isovolumetric contraction, reaching a maximum in ejection.
After that, it falls in late systole, and falls dramatically in isovolumetric relaxation.
Arterial pressure is lowest at the end of diastole, and this minimum is the diastolic pressure.
It peaks with ventricular pressure during ejection, and that peak is the systolic pressure.
When the aortic valve closes, arterial pressure dips at the incisura, or dicrotic notch.
This is a brief dip, as back-flow snaps the valve shut.
Arterial pressure then falls through diastole to its diastolic minimum.
The two volumes give the stroke volume, which is the end-diastolic volume minus the end-systolic volume.
That comes to one hundred and thirty minus sixty, or seventy millilitres.
The ejection fraction, the stroke volume divided by the end-diastolic volume, is about fifty-four per cent.

## 6. The numbers for both sides

Next come the numbers for both sides of the heart.
The right side, made up of the right atrium and ventricle that pump blood to the lungs, runs essentially the same events as the left.
The resting numbers show what differs.
Left ventricular pressure is zero millimetres of mercury in diastole and one hundred and twenty in systole.
Aortic pressure is eighty in diastole and one hundred and twenty in systole.
That gives an arterial pulse pressure, systolic minus diastolic, of forty millimetres of mercury.
Right ventricular pressure is zero in diastole and twenty-five in systole.
Pulmonary artery pressure is eight in diastole and twenty-five in systole.
Ventricular volumes are the same on both sides, with an end-diastolic volume of one hundred and thirty millilitres and an end-systolic volume of sixty.
The circuits are in series, which is why right ventricular volumes equal left.
Pulmonary resistance, on the other hand, is much lower than the total peripheral resistance of the systemic circulation.
As a result, right ventricular and pulmonary artery pressures are far lower than left ventricular and aortic pressures.
In other words, both sides process the same volumes at very different pressures.

## 7. Heart sounds and the ECG on the same timeline

Now to the heart sounds, which share this same timeline, because each valve closure is audible.
S one is mitral closure before tricuspid closure, M one before T one, and it is a low, long lub.
S two is aortic before pulmonary closure, A two before P two, and it is a short, high-pitched dub.
The third sound, S three, comes from passive filling in early diastole.
It is not heard in adults, yet it may be heard in the young.
The fourth, S four, comes from rapid filling after atrial contraction, and it is not heard.
Both are detected by phonocardiography, which is recording with a microphone.
On the E C G, each electrical event precedes its mechanical one.
The P wave, atrial depolarisation, precedes atrial contraction.
In the same way, the Q R S complex, ventricular depolarisation, precedes ventricular contraction.
The T wave is ventricular repolarisation.
The Wiggers diagram puts all of this on one time axis, from zero to one second, which lets you read every measurement at a single instant.
Alongside the valve events and phases, it tracks three pressures, aortic, left ventricular and atrial.
It also tracks ventricular volume, aortic flow velocity, the phonocardiogram and the E C G.
Aortic pressure runs from eighty to one hundred and twenty millimetres of mercury, and it shows the incisura.
Atrial pressure has three waves.
The A wave is atrial contraction.
The C wave is the closing atrioventricular valve bulging back into the atrium.
The V wave is the atrium filling against the closed atrioventricular valve in systole.
Ventricular volume moves between the end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes, and the difference between them is the stroke volume.
Aortic flow velocity is above zero only during ejection.
The lesson is that every valve event is a crossing of two pressures.
Rising past atrial pressure, ventricular pressure shuts the mitral valve, and rising past aortic pressure, it opens the aortic valve.
Falling back past them, it shuts the aortic valve and opens the mitral valve.

## 8. Murmurs

That brings us to murmurs.
Between the heart sounds, healthy valves are silent, because normal flow is laminar, or streamlined.
Turbulent flow, by contrast, is heard as a murmur, an important sign of valve lesions.
Those lesions are a narrowing, called stenosis, or reversed flow, called insufficiency or incompetence.
A stenosis murmur sounds while its valve should be open, and an incompetence murmur while it should be shut.
Aortic stenosis gives a systolic murmur, between S one and S two, that peaks at mid-systole.
With mitral incompetence, the murmur lasts throughout systole.
Aortic incompetence brings a murmur in early diastole.
In mitral stenosis, the murmur is low-pitched and mid-diastolic.
Plotted as pressure against volume, the same cycle becomes the pressure-volume loop.
On that loop, aortic valve closure and S two sit at the top-left corner.

## Summary

To sum up, passive valves divide the cycle into diastole and systole.
Diastole covers isovolumetric relaxation, eighty per cent passive filling, and atrial contraction, which adds the last twenty per cent.
Systole covers isovolumetric contraction, which starts with S one and lasts zero point zero five seconds, followed by ejection, which lasts zero point three seconds.
Each ventricle swings between an end-diastolic volume of about one hundred and thirty millilitres and an end-systolic volume of about sixty.
Both sides eject seventy millilitres.
But aortic pressure is one hundred and twenty over eighty millimetres of mercury, against twenty-five over eight in the pulmonary artery.
Semilunar closure gives S two and the dicrotic notch, and it ends systole.
Faster rates shorten diastole most, so at about one hundred and eighty beats per minute, diastole is shorter than systole.
Murmurs are turbulent flow through a stenosed or incompetent valve.
And that's one full turn of the cardiac cycle, from the valves to the sounds they make.
