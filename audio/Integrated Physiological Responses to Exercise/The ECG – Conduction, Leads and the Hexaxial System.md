## The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System

Each heartbeat starts as an electrical signal that travels a fixed route through the heart, pausing between the upper and lower chambers.
The electrocardiogram, or E C G, records this activity from the skin as waves.
A wave points up or down depending on whether the activity travels towards or away from a positive electrode.
Each electrode pair, called a lead, sees the heart from its own angle.
The grid on the paper turns the waves into timings and a heart rate.
In this note, we'll follow the signal along its route, see why each wave points up or down, meet the twelve leads, and finish by reading the paper.

## 1. How excitation spreads

We begin with how excitation spreads from cell to cell.
A heartbeat starts with an action potential, in which the inside of a heart cell flips from negative to positive, triggering contraction.
This action potential spreads by local currents.
Positive charge flows through the gap junctions, the channels linking neighbouring cells, into the resting neighbour, still negative inside, and excites it.
Meanwhile, outside the cells, charge flows back, from the resting cell's positive surface towards the excited cell's now-negative surface.
This reduces the positive charge outside the resting cell, helping it to fire.
The signal starts in the sinoatrial, or S A, node, the heart's pacemaker, and changes speed along its route.
Through the atrial muscle, it travels at zero point five metres per second.
Next, it reaches the atrioventricular, or A V, node, the only bridge from the atria to the ventricles.
At zero point zero five metres per second, the A V node is the slowest part of the route.
From there, the bundle of His and its branches carry the signal down the septum, the wall between the ventricles, at one metre per second.
This conducting system continues into the Purkinje fibres, which pass through the ventricle walls and are the fastest part, at five metres per second.
In the ventricular muscle itself, the signal slows again, to zero point five metres per second.
The slow A V node holds the signal back for about zero point one seconds, so the atria empty before the ventricles contract.
The fast Purkinje fibres, by contrast, reach all ventricular muscle almost at once, activating the ventricles from the apex, or tip, upwards.
As a result, both ventricles contract together, from the apex up.
On the E C G, the P wave comes from atrial excitation.
The Q R S complex, made up of the Q, R and S waves, comes from ventricular excitation, and the T wave from ventricular recovery.

## 2. What an ECG records

Let's turn to what an E C G records.
The local currents that spread excitation also flow through the salty extracellular fluid, which is how they reach the skin.
The E C G is a recording of voltage changes at the skin, caused by heart muscle depolarising and repolarising.
In other words, the muscle is excited, then recovers.
A sensitive voltmeter, wired to metal electrodes on the skin, records these tiny voltages, of about one millivolt, on moving paper or a screen.
Each recording compares a positive electrode with a negative one, and this pairing is called a lead.
The size of the signal depends on how much muscle is active.
The small S A and A V nodes therefore do not show on the E C G.

## 3. The vector rules

Next come the vector rules, which begin from the fact that spreading excitation is a vector, with both a size and a direction.
Its direction relative to the positive electrode decides whether the trace goes up or down.
Recovery, or repolarisation, is the reverse change, which is why its rules are reversed too.
Depolarisation moving towards the positive electrode gives an upward trace, and depolarisation moving away gives a downward one.
For repolarisation, moving away from the electrode gives an upward trace, and moving towards it gives a downward one.
With no electrical activity, the trace is a flat baseline, called the isoelectric line.
A wave moving at ninety degrees to the lead draws a flat trace as well.
To remember all this, start from the rule that depolarisation towards the electrode means an upward trace.
Flip either the wave type or the direction, and the trace flips.
Flip both, and it is up again.

## 4. The origin of the waves in lead II

Let's now put these rules to work in lead two, to find where each wave comes from.
Lead two runs from the right arm, its negative end, to the left leg, its positive end, which lies directly below the heart.
For this lead, moving towards the positive electrode means moving down and to the patient's left.
Over a single beat, the trace builds up step by step.
The beat begins when the S A node fires by itself, and excitation spreads down the atria, towards the electrode, drawing an upward P wave.
The septum is the first part of the ventricles to be excited, and its overall direction is away from the electrode, giving a small downward Q wave.
Most of the ventricular muscle is then excited from the inside outwards, towards the electrode, producing the tall upward R wave.
The last part, near the top, is excited from the inside outwards but away from the electrode, which draws a small downward S wave.
The ventricles recover from the outside inwards, from the apex up, which means recovery moves away from the electrode.
Recovery moving away draws an upward T wave.
The atria recover too, but atrial repolarisation is not seen, because it happens during the much larger Q R S complex.
What each wave triggers is covered in the note on the cardiac cycle.

## 5. The twelve leads: limb leads, the hexaxial system and chest leads

Stepping back from that single lead, we widen the view to the full set.
A standard E C G looks at the heart through twelve leads, each viewing it from a different direction.
Six of them are limb leads, made from electrodes on the arms and legs.
These are Einthoven's three bipolar leads plus three augmented leads, and together they form one picture, the hexaxial reference system.
The other six are chest leads.
Lead two, the one we just followed, is one of Einthoven's leads.
The first set, Einthoven's bipolar leads, uses electrodes on the left arm, the right arm and the left leg.
The right leg carries only an electrical earth.
Pairing these three electrodes gives leads one, two and three, which form Einthoven's triangle in the frontal plane, the body seen from the front.
The second set, the augmented leads, uses the same three electrodes, with no new ones added.
Only the way the machine combines them changes.
Picture the electrodes as the three corners of Einthoven's triangle.
Each bipolar lead runs along one side, from corner to corner.
An augmented lead, instead, runs from the middle of one side to the opposite corner.
To do this, the machine joins two electrodes together as the negative end, which acts like a point halfway between them.
The third electrode becomes the positive end.
Take the A V F lead, for example, where the two arm electrodes are joined, giving a point midway between the shoulders.
From that point, the lead runs straight down to the foot.
These leads look at the heart from their positive limb, which gives each its name.
The R in A V R is for the right arm, the L in A V L for the left arm, and the F in A V F for the foot.
In these names, the letters A V stand for augmented voltage, because this wiring makes the signal bigger.
These leads are called unipolar, since only one electrode does the looking.
Though they are wired differently, the two sets form one system.
All six limb leads lie in the frontal plane, so they are drawn through one centre, as six lines thirty degrees apart.
This is the hexaxial reference system.
Read it like a clock on the patient's chest, as you face them.
Zero degrees is three o'clock, on the patient's left, and each hour adds thirty degrees.
Six o'clock, straight down, is plus ninety degrees.
With the left leg, or left foot, at the triangle's bottom corner, each lead points towards its positive end.
Lead one points from the right arm, its negative end, to the left arm, its positive end, at zero degrees.
Lead two, from the right arm to the left leg, lies at plus sixty degrees.
Lead three, from the left arm to the left leg, lies at plus one hundred and twenty degrees.
Running straight down, A V F sits at plus ninety degrees.
In A V L, the right arm and left leg are joined as the negative end, and the lead points at minus thirty degrees.
In A V R, it is the left arm and left leg that are joined, and the lead points at minus one hundred and fifty degrees.
Every lead has its own typical Q R S shape.
A lead sees only the part of a wave that runs along its line.
A wave therefore looks tallest in the lead pointing the same way.
The mean Q R S axis, the average direction of ventricular excitation, normally points at about plus sixty degrees.
Its normal range is from minus thirty to plus ninety degrees.
This makes the Q R S tallest in lead two.
Looking from almost the opposite side, A V R is the only mainly downward lead.
Likewise, a wave at plus one hundred and twenty degrees is tallest in lead three.
It is smaller in A V F, thirty degrees away, and flat in A V R, ninety degrees away.
In A V L, one hundred and fifty degrees away, it points downward.
The chest leads, V one to V six, are six electrodes across the chest.
V one and V two sit either side of the breastbone, and V three to V six curve round the left side towards the armpit.
Each electrode is the positive end of its own lead.
All six share one negative end, made by joining the three limb electrodes together.
These average out to one point in the middle of the body, roughly at the centre of the back, while the right leg stays as the earth.
A chest lead thus runs from the back, through the heart, to its electrode.
Together, the chest leads show the horizontal plane, a slice across the chest, which the limb leads miss.
Sitting close to the heart, they draw large deflections, meaning big waves.
The thick left ventricle pulls the wave leftwards.
This is why V one and V two, over the right ventricle, show a deep S wave.
V three and V four, over the septum, show an R wave and an S wave.
V five and V six, over the left ventricle, show a tall R wave.

## 6. Reading the paper: calibration, intervals and heart rate

Last of all, here is how to read the paper.
For calibration, a test pulse of one millivolt is drawn ten millimetres tall, which is two large boxes, or ten small squares.
With the paper moving at twenty-five millimetres per second, one second spans five large boxes, or twenty-five small squares.
It follows that a large box is zero point two seconds, and a small square zero point zero four seconds.
The trace itself is made up of waves, segments and intervals.
The waves are P, Q, R, S and T.
Segments are the flat stretches between waves, such as the S T segment, which runs from the end of the S wave to the start of the T wave.
Intervals include both waves and segments.
One of these intervals is the P R interval, from the start of the P wave to the start of the Q R S complex.
It takes in the P wave and the flat line where the signal waits in the A V node.
The P R interval is thus the time from the start of atrial excitation to the start of ventricular excitation.
It reflects A V node conduction, and it normally lasts between zero point one two and zero point two seconds, or three to five small squares.
A longer interval means slowed A V conduction, known as first-degree block, which the note on arrhythmias covers.
The grid also gives the heart rate.
At five large boxes per second, a minute holds three hundred large boxes.
To find the heart rate, divide three hundred by the number of large boxes between two R waves.
Four boxes, for example, give seventy-five beats per minute.

## Summary

To sum up, excitation spreads from cell to cell by local currents.
It slows in the A V node, to zero point zero five metres per second, letting the atria empty first.
Through the Purkinje fibres, it speeds up to five metres per second, which makes both ventricles contract together.
The E C G records about one millivolt at the skin.
Depolarisation moving towards the positive electrode draws an upward wave, and repolarisation follows the reverse rules.
In lead two, this gives upward P, R and T waves, while atrial recovery stays hidden in the Q R S complex.
Each lead looks from its positive end at a fixed angle.
Lead one sits at zero degrees, lead two at plus sixty and lead three at plus one hundred and twenty.
Among the augmented leads, A V F sits at plus ninety degrees, A V L at minus thirty and A V R at minus one hundred and fifty.
A wave is tallest in the lead matching its direction, and six chest leads complete the twelve-lead E C G.
On standard paper, the P R interval is between zero point one two and zero point two seconds.
Heart rate is three hundred divided by the number of large boxes between R waves.
And that's the E C G, from one heart cell firing to reading a heart rate off the paper.
