## The Lung's First Line of Defence – Nose, Cilia, Mucus and Antimicrobial Peptides

The airways clean the air we breathe before it can harm the lung.
In this note, we'll follow a particle on its way in, from the nose to a moving layer of mucus, and on to the molecules that kill microbes.

## 1. Why the lung needs its own defences

The lung needs defences of its own because it is open to the outside air.
We breathe in roughly ten thousand litres of air a day, laden with dust, pollen and microbes.
Physical barriers stop most of these, and molecules dissolved in the airway lining attack the microbes.
Only the survivors meet the lung's immune cells.
All these defences belong to innate immunity, the inborn system that acts within hours.
Adaptive immunity, which targets one particular microbe, takes days.

## 2. The nose: a filter that sorts particles by size

We begin with the nose, the first filter.
Its lining swells or shrinks to suit conditions such as exercise, a change of climate, or sleep.
Air entering the nose soon reaches its narrowest point, the nasal valve, about one and a half centimetres inside the nostril.
Squeezing through this gap, the air can reach eighteen metres per second, the speed of a gale.
Nowhere else in the respiratory tract does air move faster.
That speed matters, because how far a particle gets depends on four things.
These are its size, its density, its time in the airway, and its speed.
Heavy, fast particles cannot follow the air around bends, so they crash into the wet wall and stick.
In practice, the nasal mucosa, the moist lining of the nose, traps particles between zero point five and three micrometres across, about the size of bacteria.
Cilia, tiny beating hairs on the lining, sweep these particles to the back of the nose, where most are swallowed.
Particles smaller than zero point five micrometres follow the air around bends, and pass through too quickly to settle.
The nose therefore filters them poorly, and they can reach the alveoli, the air sacs where oxygen enters the blood.

## 3. Cilia: the escalator that carries mucus out of the airway

Cilia keep the airways clean from the nose deep into the lung.
A single lining cell can carry between two hundred and three hundred of these hair-like projections.
They resemble the whip-like tails, or flagella, of some bacteria, but the two are unrelated.
Each cilium bends thanks to motor proteins called dyneins, which slide its inner rods past one another.
The dyneins run on energy released by splitting adenosine triphosphate, or A T P, the cell's energy currency.
Each beat has a stiff forward stroke that pushes the mucus along.
A limp return stroke then slips back beneath the mucus.
Neighbouring cilia beat slightly out of step, which sends waves rippling along the lining, like wind across a wheat field.
These waves carry the mucus, and everything stuck in it, upwards and out of the airway.
This moving belt is the mucociliary escalator.
Besides moving mucus, the belt keeps particles from touching the cells beneath.
The escalator fails in two inherited diseases.
In cystic fibrosis, the mucus is too thick for the cilia to move.
In primary ciliary dyskinesia, the cilia themselves are faulty.
The metals cadmium, copper and magnesium can interfere with cilia as well, and smoking can do the same.

## 4. Mucus: the sticky blanket that traps and kills microbes

The mucus that rides on the cilia is made by goblet cells, cup-shaped cells in the airway lining.
It is ninety-five to ninety-seven per cent water.
Most of the rest is mucins, sugar-coated proteins that turn the water into a sticky gel.
Mucus also carries salts, fats called lipids, and innate immune proteins that attack microbes directly.
These ingredients give mucus five jobs.
First, it is a physical barrier.
Second, it is a lubricant.
Third, it keeps the cells beneath it moist and working.
Fourth, it destroys microbes.
And fifth, riding on the cilia, it removes them.
Mucus is like flypaper laid on a conveyor belt, and the cilia are the belt.

## 5. Molecules that attack microbes: antimicrobial peptides, lysozyme and lactoferrin

Let's turn to the molecules that attack microbes.
Many of the microbe-killing proteins in mucus are antimicrobial peptides, which are short chains of amino acids.
They have broad activity, meaning each one attacks whole classes of microbes rather than one species.
The airway makes more of them during an infection.
Most kill by breaking either a bacterium's rigid wall or the thin, fatty membrane that holds the cell together.
A hole in the membrane lets the contents leak out, and the bacterium dies.
Two families of antimicrobial peptides kill in this way.
The first family is the defensins.
They are very small, only between eighteen and forty-five amino acids long, and they break up the membrane.
Alpha-defensins are made by neutrophils and natural killer cells, which are white blood cells.
Beta-defensins come from white blood cells in general, called leukocytes, and from the epithelial cells that line the airway.
The second family is the cathelicidins.
Cathelicidins are small too, between twelve and eighty amino acids long.
The human cathelicidin is called L L thirty-seven, and it is thirty-seven amino acids long.
Cathelicidins punch pores, or holes, right through the membrane.
L L thirty-seven also signals to receptors on other cells, helping to call in immune cells.
Both families are either secreted or used inside the phagosome.
The phagosome is the bubble in which a cell digests a microbe it has swallowed.
Peptides can break a membrane in four ways, each described by a model.
The membrane they attack is a double row of fat molecules.
In two of the models, the barrel-stave model and the toroidal pore model, the peptides stand upright and line a hole.
In the carpet model, they lie flat and coat the surface until it dissolves, like grease in washing-up liquid.
In the aggregate model, they clump with fat molecules into knots that span the membrane.
Whichever way it happens, the membrane leaks.
Next comes lysozyme, one of the body's chemical barriers, which attacks the bacterial wall rather than the membrane.
The wall is built from peptidoglycan, a mesh of sugar chains.
Lysozyme is an enzyme that splits the beta one four links between these sugars, a reaction called hydrolysis.
The mesh falls apart, which makes the bacterium swell with water and burst.
Two other molecules starve bacteria instead of breaking them.
Bacteria need iron to make energy and copy their D N A, so without iron they cannot multiply.
Hepcidin is a third kind of antimicrobial peptide.
It is an iron-binding, or iron-chelating, protein made mostly by the liver.
It works strongly against some microbes and not at all against others.
Lactoferrin, a mucus protein often classed with lysozyme as an enzyme, likewise sequesters iron, locking it away from bacteria.

## Summary

To recap, air speeds through the narrow nasal valve at up to eighteen metres per second.
Heavy, fast particles therefore crash into the wet lining of the nose.
That lining traps particles between zero point five and three micrometres across, while smaller ones are filtered poorly and can reach the alveoli.
Each lining cell can carry between two hundred and three hundred cilia, driven by dyneins.
Their waves move mucus upwards and out of the airway, forming the mucociliary escalator.
Mucus from goblet cells is ninety-five to ninety-seven per cent water, thickened by mucins.
It traps, kills and removes microbes.
Most antimicrobial peptides, such as the defensins and the cathelicidin L L thirty-seven, kill by breaking bacterial membranes.
Lysozyme splits the beta one four links of peptidoglycan in the bacterial wall.
Hepcidin and lactoferrin, by contrast, starve bacteria by locking iron away.
And that is how the nose, the cilia, the mucus and its microbe-killing molecules form the lung's first line of defence.
