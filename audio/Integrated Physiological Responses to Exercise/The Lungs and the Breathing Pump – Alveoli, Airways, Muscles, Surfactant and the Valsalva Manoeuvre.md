## The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre

Every cell needs oxygen and must get rid of carbon dioxide.
The lungs are where that trade with the outside air happens.
In this note, we'll see how the lungs are built and how a breath is pumped in and out.
We'll also see what keeps the air sacs open, and why straining against a closed throat can make a weightlifter faint.

## 1. What the lungs are for

We begin with what the lungs are for.
The lungs exist to swap gases between air and blood.
Each cell burns fuel with oxygen and makes carbon dioxide as waste.
The body therefore needs somewhere for oxygen to get in and for carbon dioxide to get out.
The alveoli are the tiny air sacs at the ends of the airways, where this swap happens.
Oxygen moves from the air in the alveoli into the blood.
Carbon dioxide moves the other way, and it is breathed out.
The swap needs a huge contact surface.
The lungs provide seventy to one hundred square metres of it, folded into the chest.
That is about the floor of a small flat.
An adult's lungs weigh about one kilogram and hold four to six litres of air.
Only ten per cent of the lung is solid tissue, and the rest is air and blood.

## 2. The alveoli

Let's look more closely at the alveoli.
The alveoli are the working part of the lung, and there are about six hundred million of them.
Blood reaches them through the pulmonary circulation, the loop from the right side of the heart through the lungs and back.
The alveoli receive the largest blood supply of any organ.
Air and blood meet across an extremely thin wall.
The alveolar wall is one cell thick.
This wall shares a fused membrane with the wall of the capillary, the tiny blood vessel pressed against it.
The whole barrier is only between zero point two and two point five micrometres thick, many times thinner than a human hair.
The thinner the barrier, the faster gases cross it.
Small pores join neighbouring alveoli, and they are fully developed by three to four years of age.
These pores let surfactant spread from sac to sac.
Surfactant is a detergent-like film that makes the alveoli easier to inflate.
The pores let air pass sideways as well.
Thanks to this, a sac whose own airway is blocked is still ventilated by its neighbours.
This is called collateral ventilation, and it helps prevent collapse.
As for the gases themselves, two hundred and fifty millilitres of oxygen leave the alveoli for the blood every minute at rest.
In that same minute, two hundred millilitres of carbon dioxide come the other way.

## 3. The two zones of the airways

Air reaches the alveoli through a branching tree of tubes, and this tree falls into two zones with different jobs.
Air enters at the nose and mouth.
On the way in, it passes the throat, known as the pharynx, and the voice box, known as the larynx.
Next comes the windpipe, or trachea.
After the trachea, air enters the bronchi, which keep dividing into narrower bronchioles.
The first zone is the conducting zone, made up of the trachea, the bronchi and the bronchioles.
These tubes carry air but have no alveoli, which means no gas is exchanged in them.
At the end of a breath in, the air left in these tubes never meets blood, and it is simply breathed out again.
Air that is moved in and out without taking part in exchange is called dead space.
The anatomic dead space is about one hundred and fifty millilitres, a small teacup of air.
Besides carrying air, the conducting zone warms and moistens it.
The same zone filters out particles too, makes the voice, and secretes antibodies into the airway lining.
The antibodies form part of the lung's defences.
The second zone is the transitional and respiratory zone.
Its parts are the respiratory bronchioles, the alveolar ducts and the alveoli.
It is here that gas exchange happens and surfactant is made.
The cells of this zone have other jobs as well.
They activate and inactivate molecules in the passing blood and help regulate clotting.
In addition, they release hormones and are home to immune cells of the inborn, or innate, defences.

## 4. Why air slows down deep in the lung

The branching of the airways changes how the air moves, because it changes how much room the air has.
Each time an airway divides, the two new tubes are narrower than the parent tube.
Added together, though, their cross-section is bigger.
After sixteen divisions, the total cross-sectional area is enormous.
Total cross-sectional area is the combined area of all the tubes, cut across at one level.
Now that the same stream of air has far more room, its forward speed falls sharply.
Slow flow has two consequences.
First, the flow stays laminar, meaning smooth and streamlined rather than swirling.
Meeting little resistance, laminar flow costs the breathing muscles little effort.
Second, in the deepest lung, the air hardly flows at all.
Until air reaches the deepest lung, it moves by bulk flow, or convection.
Bulk flow is a whole stream pushed along a tube by a pressure difference.
Beyond that point, gas moves by diffusion.
Diffusion is the random drift of molecules from where they are plentiful to where they are scarce.
Over the tiny distances inside an alveolus, diffusion is fast enough on its own.
The same rule linking total area to speed makes blood crawl through the capillaries.
Back in the airways, the branching runs through generations numbered from zero to twenty-three.
Through the whole conducting zone, the total cross-sectional area stays small and barely grows.
That is because the tubes there are few, and their areas add up to little.
The terminal bronchioles, at about generation sixteen, are the last tubes of the conducting zone.
From there, the total area rises sharply, shooting past three hundred square centimetres.
This sudden widening is like a river reaching its delta.
Air speed falls exactly where the area rises, and diffusion takes over.

## 5. Breathing in

Now for the pump itself, starting with breathing in.
The breathing muscles draw air in by making the chest bigger, which lowers the pressure inside the lungs.
The main muscle is the diaphragm, a dome of muscle under the lungs that separates the chest from the belly.
When the diaphragm contracts, it flattens and moves down towards the belly, making the chest longer.
At the same time, the outer rib muscles contract.
These muscles, called the external intercostals, lift the ribs up and outwards, making the chest wider.
Breathing in is therefore always active.
The inspiratory muscles contract to draw air in, while the expiratory muscles, which squeeze air out, stay relaxed.
Clinging to the inside of the chest wall, the lungs are pulled open with it.
Expanding the lungs lowers the pressure of the air already inside them.
This follows Boyle's law, which says that when a gas is given more room, its pressure falls.
Squeeze a gas into less room, and its pressure rises.
As the chest expands, the pressure inside the lungs, called the intrapulmonary pressure, drops slightly below atmospheric pressure.
Atmospheric pressure is the pressure of the air outside.
Air always flows from higher pressure to lower, so air flows in and the lungs inflate.
Pulling back the plunger of a syringe draws air in for exactly the same reason.
Breathing in ends when the chest stops expanding.
By then, enough air has entered to bring the pressure inside back up to atmospheric pressure.

## 6. Breathing out

Breathing out reverses the process of breathing in, and it can be passive or active.
At rest and in light exercise, expiration is passive, meaning no muscle has to push.
Breathing in stretched the elastic lung tissue and the chest wall.
The diaphragm and the outer rib muscles are the inspiratory muscles.
When they relax, the tissue springs back, like a stretched balloon let go.
This springing back is called elastic recoil.
The sternum and ribs drop, the diaphragm rises back into its dome, and the chest shrinks.
In vigorous exercise, recoil alone cannot empty the lungs fast enough, so breathing out becomes active.
The expiratory muscles contract.
These are the abdominal muscles and the inner rib muscles, called the internal intercostals.
Together, they pull the ribs down and push the belly contents up against the diaphragm.
This squeezes the chest hard and fast.
Whether breathing out is passive or active, the shrinking chest compresses the alveolar gas.
The pressure of that gas rises above atmospheric pressure, and air flows out.
Expiration ends when the squeeze stops and intrapulmonary pressure has fallen back to atmospheric pressure.

## 7. Surfactant and compliance

Let's turn to what keeps the alveoli open.
Inflating the alveoli is harder than it looks, since they are wet.
Each alveolus is lined with a thin layer of fluid, and water molecules attract one another strongly.
At a wet surface, this attraction creates surface tension, a pull that tries to shrink the surface as much as possible.
Surface tension is the force that makes a water drop bead up.
In a wet bubble like an alveolus, this pull tends to collapse the bubble.
The pull is strongest in small bubbles, since their surface bends more tightly and points more of the pull inwards.
As a result, a small bubble has a higher collapsing pressure than a large one.
This means the smallest alveoli would collapse first.
Surfactant is the lung's answer.
The alveolus is lined by two kinds of cell.
Type one cells form the thin exchange wall, and type two cells make surfactant.
Surfactant, short for surface active agent, is a detergent-like mixture.
The mixture contains proteins, fatty molecules called phospholipids, and calcium.
Like a detergent, surfactant spreads over the water layer and breaks up the attraction between the water molecules at the surface.
That lowers the surface tension.
Lower surface tension makes the lungs easier to stretch, and this ease of stretching is called compliance.
Compliance is how much the lungs and chest expand for a given change in pressure.
A highly compliant lung inflates easily, like a soft balloon rather than a stiff one.
By lowering surface tension, surfactant raises compliance.
With higher compliance, less work is needed to inflate and deflate the alveoli with each breath.
Without surfactant, the smaller alveoli collapse, a state called atelectasis.
Surfactant also helps prevent oedema, the flooding of the air sacs with fluid.
Unchecked surface tension would suck fluid out of the capillaries into the air space.
Compliance is one of two things that set how much air the lungs can move.
The amount of air moved each minute depends on the size of the biggest breath and on how fast the breaths come.
Both of these depend on compliance and on airway resistance.
Airway resistance is how much the tubes resist the flow of air through them.
Age, disease, the shape of the ribcage, the environment and genetics all affect these two properties.

## 8. Straining against a closed throat: the Valsalva manoeuvre

Finally, the breathing muscles can brace the body instead of moving air.
That is what the Valsalva manoeuvre does.
The glottis is the opening between the vocal cords, at the top of the windpipe.
The Valsalva manoeuvre is a forced exhalation against a closed glottis.
The person shuts the glottis and squeezes with the expiratory muscles as if breathing out, yet no air can leave.
The pressures involved are large.
In quiet breathing, the pressure inside the lungs swings by only about three millimetres of mercury either side of atmospheric pressure.
In a Valsalva manoeuvre, the pressure inside the chest can rise as much as one hundred and fifty millimetres of mercury above atmospheric pressure.
The pressure in the abdomen rises even higher.
People strain like this instinctively when lifting something heavy.
During a lift, the expiratory muscles brace the chest and abdomen.
A closed glottis turns the chest into a rigid, pressurised column.
For this reason, the manoeuvre is thought to help a lift by improving leverage.
The cost is the pressure itself.
The very high abdominal pressure can push tissue through a weak point in the abdominal wall, producing a hernia.
Holding the strain too long causes a second problem, this time for the circulation.
The big veins that return blood to the heart pass through the chest, carrying blood at low pressure.
The high chest pressure consequently squashes these veins flat.
Less blood returns to the heart, a fall in what is called venous return.
With less blood coming back, the heart fills less between beats.
A heart that fills less ejects less with each beat, which is Starling's law.
Accordingly, the stroke volume, the amount pumped per beat, falls.
Blood pressure falls with it.
The brain then gets less blood.
The lifter becomes dizzy, sees spots before the eyes, or faints, an event known as syncope.
Pulse pressure is the difference between the highest and lowest pressure in the aorta during one beat.
The aorta is the main artery leaving the heart.
Measured in millimetres of mercury, aortic pulse pressure can be followed beat by beat before, during and after the strain.
These three periods are called pre-strain, strain and post-strain.
Pulse pressure rises and falls with the amount of blood the heart ejects.
Tracking pulse pressure beat by beat is really tracking stroke volume.
The response runs through four phases.
Phase one is a brief rise in pulse pressure at the start of the strain.
This rise comes from the sudden chest pressure, which squeezes blood from the chest into the aorta.
In phase two, pulse pressure falls steeply and stays low.
The reason is that venous return is blocked, and the heart has less to pump.
Phase three is a brief further dip when the strain is released.
At that moment, the chest refills with blood that has not yet reached the heart.
Phase four is the overshoot.
The pent-up blood floods back into the heart.
The heart pumps it out against vessels that had been tightened by reflexes while pressure was low.
The lesson is that a long strain starves the heart of blood to pump.

## Summary

To recap, the lungs pack seventy to one hundred square metres of exchange surface into about six hundred million alveoli.
Air and blood meet there across a membrane only between zero point two and two point five micrometres thick.
The conducting zone, made of the trachea, bronchi and bronchioles, only carries air.
That is why its one hundred and fifty millilitres is anatomic dead space.
The respiratory zone does the exchange.
Branching makes the total cross-sectional area huge.
Given all that extra room, air slows, stays laminar, and finally moves by diffusion in the deepest lung.
Breathing in is active.
The diaphragm flattens and the ribs lift.
By Boyle's law, this makes intrapulmonary pressure fall below atmospheric pressure, and air flows in.
Breathing out is passive at rest, driven by elastic recoil.
In hard exercise it becomes active, with the abdominal muscles and the internal intercostals squeezing the chest.
Surfactant from type two cells lowers surface tension.
That raises compliance, cuts the work of breathing, and prevents atelectasis and oedema.
A long Valsalva manoeuvre collapses the chest veins.
This cuts venous return, stroke volume and blood pressure, until the lifter faints.
And that is how the lungs are built, how their air sacs are kept open, and how the breathing pump moves each breath in and out.
