## The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane

Breathing only helps if oxygen crosses from the air into the blood and carbon dioxide crosses back out.
In this note, we'll look at the simple pressure rule that moves each gas, and see why damaged lungs struggle in exercise.

## 1. What is in the air and how hard it presses

We begin with what is in the air.
Air is a mixture of gases, mostly oxygen and nitrogen.
Oxygen makes up twenty point nine per cent of air, and nitrogen seventy-eight point nine per cent.
The rest is carbon dioxide at zero point zero three per cent, water vapour at zero point zero four per cent, and tiny amounts of other gases.
The moving gas molecules strike every surface, and their impacts add up to atmospheric pressure.
At sea level, this pressure holds up a column of mercury seven hundred and sixty millimetres high in a barometer.
That is why sea-level pressure is written as seven hundred and sixty millimetres of mercury.
Pressure is also measured in pascals.
Hospitals often give gas pressures in kilopascals.
One kilopascal is about seven point five millimetres of mercury.

## 2. Each gas pushes on its own: partial pressure

Next, let's look at partial pressure.
Each gas in a mixture pushes on its own, whatever other gases are around it.
The part of the total pressure that comes from one gas is called its partial pressure.
Adding up the partial pressures of all the gases gives the total pressure.
This is Dalton's law.
The share of the pressure from each gas matches its share of the mixture.
Oxygen makes up about a fifth of the air, so it supplies about a fifth of the air's pressure.
Put another way, the partial pressure of a gas equals its fraction of the mixture times the total pressure.
Oxygen is twenty point nine per cent of air, which as a fraction is zero point two zero nine.
At sea level, oxygen's partial pressure is therefore zero point two zero nine times seven hundred and sixty millimetres of mercury.
That comes to one hundred and fifty-nine millimetres of mercury.
Partial pressures are written with a P before the gas.
The oxygen pressure is written P O two, and the carbon dioxide pressure is written P C O two.
The same calculation works for the three main gases in dry air at sea level.
Oxygen, at twenty point nine per cent, has a partial pressure of one hundred and fifty-nine millimetres of mercury.
Each litre of air contains two hundred and nine point three millilitres of oxygen.
Carbon dioxide, at zero point zero three per cent, has a partial pressure of only zero point two millimetres of mercury.
A litre of air holds just zero point four millilitres of carbon dioxide.
Nitrogen, at seventy-eight point nine per cent, has a partial pressure of six hundred millimetres of mercury.
Every litre of air contains seven hundred and ninety point three millilitres of nitrogen.
Partial pressure drives diffusion, the spreading of molecules as they move about at random.
A gas always diffuses from higher to lower partial pressure, just as heat flows from hot to cold.
The rule holds whatever other gases are present.
The difference between the two pressures is called the gradient, and a bigger gradient moves gas faster.
The flow stops when the pressures are equal, a balance called equilibrium.
Importantly, diffusion is driven by partial pressure, not percentage.
Pack twice as much air into the same space, and it is still twenty point nine per cent oxygen.
Yet the oxygen pressure doubles, from one hundred and fifty-nine to three hundred and eighteen millimetres of mercury.
Gases dissolved in blood also have a partial pressure.
This allows a pressure in the air to be compared directly with a pressure in the blood.
In the lungs, oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the same thin wall at once, in opposite directions.
The wall is called the alveolar-capillary membrane.
The membrane separates the air in each air sac, called an alveolus, from the blood in the fine vessels around it, called capillaries.

## 3. The pressure staircase from air to muscle and back

We can now track each gas on its journey through the body.
Oxygen's journey from the air to a muscle is a staircase of falling pressures.
Carbon dioxide walks a similar staircase down, from muscle to air.
Breathed-in air, called inspired air, has an oxygen pressure of one hundred and fifty-nine millimetres of mercury.
Inspired air has almost no carbon dioxide, with a pressure between about zero point two and zero point three millimetres of mercury.
In the windpipe, or trachea, the air is warmed and saturated with water vapour.
At body temperature, this water vapour presses with forty-seven millimetres of mercury.
That leaves seven hundred and thirteen millimetres of mercury for the other gases.
As a result, oxygen's pressure falls to one hundred and forty-nine millimetres of mercury, which is its twenty point nine per cent share of what is left.
In the alveoli, the oxygen pressure settles at about one hundred millimetres of mercury, and the carbon dioxide pressure at about forty.
The reason for these values is that each breath only partly refreshes the stale air in the lungs, while the blood keeps removing oxygen and adding carbon dioxide.
Exchange with the blood is fast, reaching equilibrium in about zero point two five seconds.
Blood therefore leaves the lungs with roughly the same pressures as the alveolar air.
This oxygen-rich blood is called arterial blood.
In arterial blood, a healthy oxygen pressure lies between seventy-five and one hundred millimetres of mercury.
Expressed in kilopascals, that is between ten point five and thirteen point five.
At a muscle, the exchange runs the other way, because muscle cells constantly use oxygen and make carbon dioxide.
Even at rest, the oxygen pressure around muscle cells is about forty millimetres of mercury, and the carbon dioxide pressure about forty-six.
Arriving arterial blood consequently gives up oxygen and takes up carbon dioxide.
The blood returns to the lungs in the veins as venous blood, carrying the muscle's pressures.
In exercise, the muscle uses oxygen faster, which steepens the gradients for both oxygen and carbon dioxide.
Back in the lungs, venous blood with an oxygen pressure of forty millimetres of mercury meets alveolar air at one hundred.
That is a gradient of sixty millimetres of mercury.
For carbon dioxide, the gradient is only six millimetres of mercury, from forty-six down to forty.
That small gradient is enough, since carbon dioxide dissolves very readily in body fluids.
This lets carbon dioxide cross between about twenty and twenty-five times more easily than oxygen.
Nitrogen is neither used nor made by the body, and for that reason it is not exchanged.
Nitrogen matters only in diving, where it dissolves under high pressure and can bubble out on surfacing.
Now let's follow the whole loop at rest.
Blood leaves the lungs as arterial blood, travels to the skeletal muscle, and returns to the lungs as venous blood.
Starting from inspired air, oxygen steps down from one hundred and fifty-nine millimetres of mercury to one hundred and forty-nine in the trachea.
It then drops to one hundred in the alveolus, and to forty at the muscle.
Each step is a gradient that moves the oxygen on.
Along the same route, carbon dioxide climbs from zero point three millimetres of mercury in inspired air, to forty in the alveolus, and to forty-six at the muscle.
This climb is due to carbon dioxide travelling the other way, from muscle to air.

## 4. When the exchange is impaired: thick membranes, lost surface and too little time

Let's turn to what happens when gas exchange in the lungs is impaired.
Given that diffusion across the alveolar membrane takes time, anything that slows it can stop the blood from loading fully with oxygen.
Two changes slow diffusion down.
One is a thicker membrane, and the other is a smaller alveolar surface area.
A membrane can be thickened by scarring, by fluid, or by inhaled pollutants such as smoke.
A thickened membrane gives each molecule a longer path.
The alveolar surface shrinks when emphysema destroys alveoli, or when pneumonia fills them with fluid.
A smaller surface leaves fewer places to cross.
Either change lengthens the time the blood needs to reach equilibrium.
At rest, this rarely matters, since blood spends about zero point seven five seconds in a lung capillary but needs only about zero point two five seconds.
In exercise, the heart pumps several times more blood through the lungs.
As a result, each red blood cell may stay in the capillary for only about zero point two five seconds.
A healthy lung still loads the blood just in time.
A damaged lung cannot load the blood in time, which means the blood leaves short of oxygen.
This is why people with lung disease may cope at rest, yet struggle and perform poorly in exercise.
To see how the timing works, think of a single capillary wrapping around an alveolus, with blood flowing from the start of the capillary to its end.
As the blood moves along, its oxygen partial pressure climbs.
In a healthy lung, the blood's oxygen pressure reaches the alveolar level, the oxygen pressure in the alveolus itself, by about zero point two five seconds.
Blood leaves the capillary after about zero point two five seconds in exercise, and after about zero point seven five seconds at rest.
A thickened membrane would slow the climb, so blood leaving during exercise would fall short of the alveolar level.
It is like a glass under a slow tap.
The glass fills if you stroll past, but not if you run.
In some highly trained athletes, even healthy lungs can reach this time limit during very hard exercise.
The same problem appears at altitude, where thinner air lowers the alveolar oxygen pressure and shrinks the gradient that loads the blood.
Finally, once in the blood, little oxygen can stay dissolved.
For this reason, most oxygen needs a carrier.
That carrier is haemoglobin, a protein in red blood cells.

## Summary

To recap, air is twenty point nine per cent oxygen, at a total pressure of seven hundred and sixty millimetres of mercury.
It follows from Dalton's law that oxygen's partial pressure is one hundred and fifty-nine millimetres of mercury.
Every gas diffuses from higher to lower partial pressure, whatever other gases are present.
Oxygen steps down from one hundred and forty-nine millimetres of mercury in the trachea, to one hundred in the alveoli and arterial blood.
It then falls to forty in resting muscle and venous blood.
Carbon dioxide needs a gradient of only six millimetres of mercury, from forty-six to forty.
Such a small gradient works because carbon dioxide crosses between twenty and twenty-five times more easily than oxygen.
Blood reaches equilibrium in about zero point two five seconds.
In arterial blood, a healthy oxygen pressure is seventy-five to one hundred millimetres of mercury.
In kilopascals, that is ten point five to thirteen point five.
A thicker membrane or a smaller alveolar surface slows the loading of oxygen into the blood.
That matters in exercise, when blood stays in the capillary for about zero point two five seconds instead of zero point seven five.
And that is how differences in partial pressure move oxygen and carbon dioxide across the alveolar membrane, and why damaged lungs struggle in exercise.
