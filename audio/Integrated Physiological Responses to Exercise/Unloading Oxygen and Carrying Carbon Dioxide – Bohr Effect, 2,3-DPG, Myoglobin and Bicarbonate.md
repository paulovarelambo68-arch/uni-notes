## Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate

Working muscle needs extra oxygen, and it must clear extra carbon dioxide.
In this note, we'll see how working muscle makes the blood release more oxygen, keeps a small oxygen store of its own, and sends carbon dioxide back to the lungs.

## 1. Muscle tells haemoglobin to let go: the Bohr effect

We begin with how working muscle gets haemoglobin to let go of its oxygen.
Working muscle changes its blood in three ways.
Haemoglobin is the oxygen carrier in red blood cells, and each change makes it release more oxygen.
First, the muscle is hotter, because breaking down fuel releases heat.
Second, that same breakdown produces more carbon dioxide.
Third, the muscle is more acidic, meaning it has more free hydrogen ions and hence a lower p H.
These hydrogen ions come partly from that extra carbon dioxide, which forms an acid in water, and partly from the lactate that builds up in hard exercise.
Each of these changes shifts the dissociation curve down and to the right.
The dissociation curve describes the amount of oxygen that haemoglobin holds at each oxygen pressure, or P O two.
After a right shift, haemoglobin holds less oxygen at any given pressure, so more is released.
In other words, haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen has fallen.
Affinity means how tightly haemoglobin holds on to oxygen.
Picture haemoglobin as a magnet that grips oxygen less tightly when it is warm and in acid.
Hydrogen ions and carbon dioxide loosen this grip by attaching to haemoglobin and changing its shape.
Heat loosens the grip too.
The released oxygen diffuses, or spreads, into the muscle fibres, where oxygen pressure is lower.
This loosening of haemoglobin's grip is called the Bohr effect.
The Bohr effect predominates in intense exercise, during which heat, carbon dioxide and lactate all climb.
Let's look more closely at carbon dioxide, p H and temperature.
At any given oxygen pressure, each of these three factors alters haemoglobin's saturation, meaning how full it is with oxygen.
Oxygen pressure is measured in millimetres of mercury.
Compared with normal blood, a high carbon dioxide pressure, more acid and more heat each cause a right shift.
Haemoglobin's affinity is decreased in blood with a p H of seven point two, and in blood at forty-three degrees Celsius.
In these conditions, haemoglobin holds the least oxygen at any one oxygen pressure, because its grip is weakest.
On the other hand, affinity is increased in blood with a p H of seven point six, and in blood at twenty degrees Celsius.
This opposite change is a left shift, in which haemoglobin holds on more tightly.
A left shift happens in the cold.
It also happens in the alkaline, or less acidic, blood of the first days at altitude.

## 2. The red cell's own release signal: 2,3-DPG

Let's turn to a release signal that the red cell makes for itself.
This signal is a small molecule called two three diphosphoglycerate, or two three D P G.
Another name for the same molecule is two three bisphosphoglycerate, or two three B P G.
Two three D P G binds to deoxyhaemoglobin, which is haemoglobin that has given up its oxygen.
It holds deoxyhaemoglobin in that unloaded shape, and oxygen is therefore released more easily.
This is another right shift of the dissociation curve, and it weakens the haemoglobin magnet further.
Two three D P G is a by-product of the way red cells make energy.
Since red cells have no mitochondria, they rely entirely on anaerobic glycolysis, the breakdown of glucose without oxygen.
Along this pathway, the enzyme that makes two three D P G is normally held back by oxyhaemoglobin, which is haemoglobin carrying oxygen.
It follows that a fall in oxyhaemoglobin raises the production of two three D P G.
This happens at high altitude and in anaemia, a shortage of haemoglobin.
The extra two three D P G makes the blood unload oxygen more readily, which offsets the poorer oxygen supply.
In pregnancy, a rise in two three D P G helps oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the placenta.

## 3. The muscle's own oxygen store: myoglobin

Muscle fibres keep a small oxygen store of their own, in a protein called myoglobin.
Myoglobin is a ball-shaped, or globular, protein that contains iron and is found in skeletal and heart muscle.
Like haemoglobin, myoglobin binds oxygen reversibly, picking it up and releasing it again.
Each myoglobin molecule, though, has only one iron atom, which means it holds just one oxygen molecule.
In haemoglobin, each oxygen that binds makes the next one bind more easily.
This is called cooperativity, and it bends haemoglobin's dissociation curve into an S shape.
Myoglobin's single seat allows no such teamwork.
As a result, myoglobin's dissociation curve rises steeply from the lowest pressures before flattening out, a shape called a hyperbola.
In short, the S shape suits a deliverer, and the hyperbola suits a keeper.
Myoglobin also has a higher affinity for oxygen than haemoglobin, and for that reason its dissociation curve lies further to the left.
At pressures where haemoglobin has already let go, myoglobin keeps its oxygen.
It stays almost fully loaded at rest and in moderate exercise.
In hard exercise, the oxygen pressure inside the fibre falls, and myoglobin hands its oxygen to the mitochondria.
It releases most of its oxygen only once the oxygen pressure inside the fibre drops below five millimetres of mercury.
Think of myoglobin as a reserve tank that opens only when the main supply runs low.
Unlike haemoglobin, myoglobin is not subject to the Bohr effect.
Heat and acid therefore cannot make myoglobin, the stronger magnet, loosen its grip early.
Slow-twitch fibres, which contract slowly and resist fatigue, contain more myoglobin.
Myoglobin is a red pigment, which is why slow-twitch fibres look redder.

## 4. The return journey: carrying carbon dioxide

For the return journey, working muscle hands carbon dioxide to the blood to carry back to the lungs.
This carbon dioxide travels in three forms.
About seven per cent is dissolved in the plasma, the liquid part of the blood.
Only these free, dissolved molecules exert gas pressure, so they set the blood's carbon dioxide pressure, or P C O two.
About twenty-three per cent rides loosely on haemoglobin's protein chains, in a form called carbamino-haemoglobin.
The rest, about seventy per cent, travels as bicarbonate, which is made inside the red cell.
Bicarbonate forms in two steps.
First, carbon dioxide joins water to make carbonic acid.
Next, the carbonic acid splits into bicarbonate and a hydrogen ion.
Each step can run either way.
On its own, the first step is slow, but the enzyme carbonic anhydrase in the red cell speeds it up thousands of times.
The reason bicarbonate is the main route is that it dissolves far more easily in water than carbon dioxide gas does.
In the lungs, the reaction runs backwards.
The blood's P C O two falls as carbon dioxide diffuses out into the alveoli, the lungs' air sacs.
Bicarbonate and hydrogen ions then rejoin as carbonic acid.
The carbonic acid splits back into water and carbon dioxide, and the carbon dioxide is breathed out.
The hydrogen ion from bicarbonate formation explains why carbon dioxide makes the blood more acidic.
That acidity helps weaken the haemoglobin magnet in working muscle.
During a rise in carbon dioxide, this same hydrogen ion is what chemoreceptors detect.
Chemoreceptors are chemical sensors, and they tell the brain's breathing centres to increase breathing.
Haemoglobin does a buffering job, mopping up many of the hydrogen ions from bicarbonate formation.

## Summary

To recap, heat, carbon dioxide and acid in working muscle lower haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen.
These three factors shift haemoglobin's dissociation curve to the right, releasing more oxygen.
This is the Bohr effect, and it is strongest in intense exercise.
Red cells, which lack mitochondria, make two three D P G by anaerobic glycolysis.
Two three D P G binds deoxyhaemoglobin, the form that has given up its oxygen, and causes a right shift as well.
Oxyhaemoglobin, the form carrying oxygen, holds back the enzyme that makes two three D P G.
Consequently, the level of two three D P G rises when oxyhaemoglobin falls, as it does at altitude and in anaemia.
The muscle's own oxygen store, myoglobin, is richest in slow-twitch fibres.
Myoglobin holds one oxygen per molecule, and for this reason its dissociation curve is a hyperbola rather than an S shape.
It has a higher affinity than haemoglobin, shows no Bohr effect, and gives up most of its oxygen only below five millimetres of mercury.
About seven per cent of carbon dioxide travels dissolved, and twenty-three per cent travels on haemoglobin.
The rest, about seventy per cent, travels as bicarbonate.
Bicarbonate is made by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, and the process is reversed in the lungs.
And that's how working muscle draws extra oxygen from the blood, keeps a reserve of its own, and sends its carbon dioxide back to the lungs.
