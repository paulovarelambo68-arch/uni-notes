## Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles

Blood moves only when a pressure difference pushes it.
That pressure is spent overcoming the friction, or resistance, of the vessels the blood flows through.
In this note, we'll first locate that resistance, which turns out to sit mainly in the arterioles, and see why their radius matters so much.
After that, we'll follow the four kinds of signal that tighten or relax the smooth muscle in the arteriolar wall.
They are local, neural, hormonal and paracrine, and between them they set resistance and blood flow.

## 1. The pressure profile

We begin with the pressure profile.
This note is about one term of the equation in which mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance.
That term is the total peripheral resistance.
Since pressure is used up in overcoming resistance, a map of pressure along the circulation shows where the resistance lies.
In the left ventricle, pressure falls close to zero in diastole and rises to about one hundred and twenty millimetres of mercury in systole.
In the large arteries, pressure is pulsatile, rising in systole and falling in diastole.
It never falls far, though, as the stretched elastic arteries recoil between beats.
This pulsatility is lost in the arterioles, which are the major site of pressure fall, from about seventy-five to about thirty-five millimetres of mercury.
In the capillaries, pressure falls further, from thirty-five to fifteen millimetres of mercury.
That leaves only a low pressure gradient to drive blood back to the heart through the veins, where pressure approaches zero at the right atrium.
Venous pressure is therefore lower than capillary pressure, not higher.
Seen as a whole, the pressure profile shows the pulse of the large arteries fading across the arterioles, exactly where the mean pressure drops most steeply.
The central point is that the gradient from the aorta to the right atrium drives blood through the whole systemic circulation.
The total cross-sectional area changes along the way.
The aorta's four square centimetres branch into over a thousand square centimetres of capillaries, far more than the arterioles' area.
This is why blood moves slowest in the capillaries.

## 2. The site of resistance

Why, then, does the biggest pressure drop mark the biggest resistance?
Blood behaves like electric current, which flows down a voltage gradient according to Ohm's law.
That law states that voltage equals current times resistance.
In the circulation, the pressure difference plays the part of voltage.
Flow is directly proportional to the pressure difference and inversely proportional to resistance.
Hence the pressure difference equals flow times resistance, or, put the other way round, resistance is proportional to the pressure difference divided by flow.
Flow in an individual vessel varies enormously, given that the circulation divides into parallel organ beds, and each level into countless parallel vessels.
The levels themselves, however, are in series, running from arteries to arterioles, capillaries and veins.
All the arterioles together therefore carry the same total flow as all the capillaries together, namely the whole cardiac output.
With flow the same at every level, the pressure drop across a level is directly proportional to that level's combined resistance.
It follows that the level costing the most pressure, the arterioles, must be the major site of resistance.
The same reasoning holds within any single organ, whose own flow passes through each of its levels in turn.

## 3. Radius to the fourth power

What gives arterioles their resistance, and lets them vary it, is their radius.
Compare two tubes draining one reservoir.
A narrow tube with a radius of one unit passes one unit of flow, which is one to the fourth power.
A wider tube with a radius of two units passes sixteen units, which is two to the fourth power.
A radius of three would pass eighty-one units, three times three times three times three, which is three to the fourth power.
Flow, in short, rises with the fourth power of the radius, and the wider tube fills a beaker far faster.
Because resistance is the inverse of flow at a given pressure, the narrow tube's resistance is sixteen times the wide tube's.
In other words, resistance is proportional to one over the radius to the fourth power.
This rule is the radius term of Poiseuille's law.
The law's other terms, vessel length and blood viscosity, hardly change in the body.
The rule cuts both ways, so halving a radius raises resistance sixteenfold.
To sum up so far, the more resistance a stretch of the circulation offers, the more pressure is lost in crossing it.
How much a stretch resists depends on how narrow its vessels are, and on how many of them the blood can spread across.
The wide arteries resist little, and pressure falls only slightly along them.
The arterioles resist most and take the biggest drop, from about seventy-five to about thirty-five millimetres of mercury.
The capillaries are narrower still, but blood is spread across billions of them at once.
Together, they resist less than the arterioles, and pressure falls less across them, from thirty-five to fifteen millimetres of mercury.
The wide veins resist least, and they use up the small remainder on the way back to the heart.
The arterioles are called the resistance vessels for two reasons.
Most of the resistance sits in them, and their thick muscular wall can change their radius.
That makes them the place where the body adjusts resistance, and the rest of this note is about what controls that muscle.

## 4. Autoregulation

The first control is built into the organs themselves.
Some organs, notably the brain, kidney and heart, autoregulate their blood flow.
When the metabolism within such an organ is constant, autoregulation keeps its blood flow constant in the face of changing arterial pressure.
Across mean arterial pressures from seventy to one hundred and seventy millimetres of mercury, flow holds steady.
A rise in mean arterial pressure constricts the arterioles, and a fall dilates them.
Through the fourth-power rule, this offsets the change in driving pressure.
Beyond that range, the adjustment runs out.
Above one hundred and seventy millimetres of mercury, flow increases, and below seventy it falls.
It drops to zero at twenty to thirty millimetres of mercury, the critical closing pressure.
This is believed to be the minimum pressure needed to keep small blood vessels open.
Within the autoregulated range, flow no longer follows pressure.
Outside it, flow simply rises and falls with pressure, down to zero at the critical closing pressure.
The adjustment behind this steady flow is the myogenic response.
The name reflects its origin in the smooth muscle cells themselves, with no nerve or hormone involved.
What the muscle senses is not pressure but its own wall tension, the stretching force in the vessel wall.
Wall tension rises with the pressure inside, following Laplace's law, which states that tension equals pressure times radius.
Stretch makes the arteriolar smooth muscle contract, and a fall in tension makes it relax.

## 5. Arteriolar tone and the four factors that set it

Autoregulation serves the organ, but the body as a whole needs its resistance regulated as well.
Regulating total peripheral resistance is very important for maintaining arterial blood pressure.
The resistance vessels, the arterioles, lie within each tissue.
Since the tissues' vascular beds are in parallel, each organ's arterioles set its share of the cardiac output.
Were they all to dilate at once, blood pressure would drop to a dangerously low level.
Resistance is set by contraction or relaxation of the smooth muscle in the arteriolar wall, which changes the calibre of the arteriole.
Contraction here is called vasoconstriction, and relaxation is called vasodilation.
The tone of that muscle, meaning its state of tension, is influenced by four factors.
These are local factors, neural activity, hormones and paracrines.
Every one of these inputs acts on the same thing, the vessel's diameter.
The arteriole's tone is thus the balance of dilators against constrictors.
Two groups of local factors serve the tissue itself.
In the first, metabolites dilate, because they build up when blood supply falls short of demand.
Oxygen belongs to the same group but constricts, as plenty of it means supply is ample.
In the second, a rise in blood pressure constricts the arteriole through the myogenic response.
Nerves and hormones, by contrast, serve the whole body.
Sympathetic nerves can both constrict and dilate, thanks to their constant firing at rest in many beds.
More firing constricts, and less firing dilates.
Their transmitter is noradrenaline.
Adrenaline works both ways too, constricting through alpha receptors and dilating through beta receptors.
Among the paracrines are the endothelium-derived relaxing and constricting factors.
These are nitric oxide and endothelin, both made by the vessel's own lining.

## 6. Neural control

Nervous control acts extremely rapidly, and it can govern large parts of the circulation simultaneously.
The parasympathetic system contributes by regulating heart rate.
The sympathetic nervous system, however, plays the major role in controlling total peripheral resistance.
It is also the pathway through which the baroreflex adjusts resistance.
All blood vessels except capillaries and small arterioles are innervated by the sympathetic nervous system.
Its transmitter, noradrenaline, binds alpha adrenergic receptors on vascular smooth muscle and makes it contract.
Innervation of the arterioles lets sympathetic stimulation raise their resistance, reducing blood flow through an organ.
Innervation of the veins lets it change the volume of blood they hold.
A change in venous volume, in turn, changes venous return and cardiac output, which is the capacitance role of the veins.
The innervation is dense in the arterioles of the skin, kidney and gut, but poor in those of the brain and heart.
A sympathetic surge accordingly redirects blood from the skin, kidney and gut to the brain and heart.
In some organs, the sympathetic nerves fire constantly, giving a resting vasomotor tone.
Extra sympathetic discharge causes vasoconstriction in these organs, and reduced discharge causes vasodilation.
Nerves can thus dilate a bed simply by firing less.

## 7. Local metabolic control

Nerves serve the whole body, but metabolites serve the tissue, matching its flow to its need.
When an organ's metabolic activity rises, the oxygen in its interstitial fluid falls and metabolites accumulate.
Its arterioles dilate, and blood flow to it increases.
This response is called active hyperaemia, or metabolic vasodilation.
Hyperaemia simply means increased blood flow.
Vasodilator metabolites are produced locally whenever the supply of oxygen falls below oxygen consumption.
Together with the low partial pressure of oxygen itself, they reduce local vascular resistance and increase blood flow to meet the tissue's needs.
No single metabolite has been identified.
The candidates are carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions, lactate and adenosine.
A T P and potassium ions have been named as well.
The response limits itself.
The increased flow washes the metabolites out, and with them gone, resistance rises and flow falls back towards normal.

## 8. Hormonal control

Hormones reach every vascular bed through the blood.
The adrenal medulla, the core of the adrenal gland, releases adrenaline, or epinephrine, along with a small amount of noradrenaline.
Noradrenaline always causes vasoconstriction.
The action of adrenaline, on the other hand, depends on the relative distribution of alpha and beta adrenergic receptors in an organ's vessels.
Alpha receptors contract vascular smooth muscle, whereas beta two receptors relax it.
Adrenaline causes vasodilation in organs with a high density of beta receptors, namely the vasculature of the heart and of skeletal muscle.
Skeletal muscle shows both effects at once.
Sympathetic nerves constrict its arterioles through alpha receptors, while circulating adrenaline dilates them through beta two receptors.
Two other hormones constrict vessels.
One is angiotensin two, a powerful vasoconstrictor, and the other is vasopressin, otherwise known as antidiuretic hormone.

## 9. Paracrine control and the endothelium

The last factor acts over the shortest distance.
Paracrines are released by one type of cell and act on the neighbouring cells.
In the vessel wall, their chief source is the endothelium, the single layer of cells lining the lumen.
Lying directly against the smooth muscle, the endothelium is close enough for what it releases to reach the muscle by diffusion.
Mast cells, which are immune cells of connective tissue, contribute too.
Platelets, the cell fragments that start clotting, are another source.
Prostaglandins, a family of lipid messengers, come in both dilator and constrictor forms.
Consider five paracrines, of which three are vasodilators.
Histamine comes from mast cells, nitric oxide from endothelial cells, and prostacyclin, a prostaglandin, from endothelial cells and platelets.
The other two are vasoconstrictors.
Endothelin comes from endothelial cells, and thromboxane, another prostaglandin, from the endothelium and platelets.
Of the endothelium's products, one of the most important is nitric oxide.
Endothelial cells release nitric oxide, which diffuses to the underlying vascular smooth muscle cells.
There it activates the enzyme guanylyl cyclase, raising the concentration of cyclic G M P, a second messenger.
As a result, the smooth muscle cell relaxes and the vessel dilates.
The endothelium of the arterioles releases nitric oxide continuously and in significant amounts.
This contributes to arteriolar vasodilation in the basal state, meaning that resting arterioles are already held partly open.
Viagra inhibits the breakdown of cyclic G M P by blocking the enzyme phosphodiesterase five, and in this way it maintains vasodilation.
Picture the wall of an innervated arteriole in layers.
Endothelial cells line the lumen, smooth muscle cells lie just outside them, and sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibres sit in the outer layer.
Oxygen also works through nitric oxide.
Hyperoxaemia, a high oxygen concentration, causes vasoconstriction by reducing nitric oxide levels.
The likely reason is that excess oxygen generates reactive oxygen species, which destroy nitric oxide.
That is how oxygen acts as a local constrictor.

## Summary

To recap, pressure is spent overcoming resistance.
In the arterioles, pressure falls most steeply, from about seventy-five to about thirty-five millimetres of mercury, and the pulse is lost.
Resistance is proportional to the pressure drop divided by flow, and flow is the same at every level, so the arterioles are the resistance vessels.
Their resistance depends on the radius to the fourth power, meaning that halving the radius raises it sixteenfold.
The brain, kidney and heart autoregulate their flow across mean arterial pressures from seventy to one hundred and seventy millimetres of mercury.
They do so through the myogenic response, and flow stops at the critical closing pressure of twenty to thirty millimetres of mercury.
Metabolites such as adenosine, until they are washed out, dilate the arterioles.
Sympathetic noradrenaline constricts them, mainly in the skin, kidney and gut.
Angiotensin two, antidiuretic hormone and circulating noradrenaline all constrict, while adrenaline dilates where beta receptors dominate.
Finally, endothelial nitric oxide relaxes smooth muscle through cyclic G M P, and its loss in hyperoxaemia causes constriction.
And that is how the arterioles set the body's resistance, and what tells them to do it.
