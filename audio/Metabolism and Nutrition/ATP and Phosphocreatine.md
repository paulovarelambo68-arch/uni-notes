## ATP and Phosphocreatine

Every job a cell does, from moving a muscle to building a protein, is paid for with one small molecule.
In this note, we'll meet that molecule, see how little of it the body keeps, and look at the two fastest ways the cell refills it.

## 1. Why the cell needs a currency

Food holds its energy in three macronutrients, carbohydrate, fat and protein.
This is potential energy, stored in the chemical bonds of each nutrient.
It cannot be handed directly to the work a cell does.
A muscle fibre cannot contract by burning a piece of fat on the spot.
Instead, the energy released when these nutrients are broken down is funnelled into one molecule, adenosine triphosphate, or A T P.
The potential energy held in A T P then powers every process in the cell that needs energy.
The cell therefore has two great energy tasks.
The first is to extract the potential energy of food and conserve it in the bonds of A T P.
The second is to extract the chemical energy of A T P and transfer it to biologic work.
That work comes in mechanical, chemical and transport forms.

## 2. What ATP is

A T P is built from adenosine, which is the base adenine joined to the sugar ribose.
The adenosine is linked to a chain of three phosphate groups.
A phosphate group is a phosphorus atom surrounded by oxygen atoms.
The two bonds that join the phosphates together are called high energy bonds.
That name comes from the large amount of free energy released when one of them is split.
Free energy is the part of a molecule's energy that can do work.
A T P pays for six kinds of work.
These are nerve transmission, muscle action, circulation, tissue synthesis, digestion and glandular secretion.
One molecule serves every purpose, from a heartbeat to building protein from amino acids.

## 3. Spending ATP

The cell releases the energy in A T P by hydrolysis, which means splitting a bond by adding water.
The enzyme that does this is adenosine triphosphatase, or A T P-ase.
It joins A T P with water and splits off the outermost phosphate.
What remains is adenosine diphosphate, or A D P, which has two phosphates.
A free inorganic phosphate, called P i, is also split off.
The whole reaction is A T P plus water, giving A D P plus P i, plus seven point three kilocalories per mole.
A mole is simply a fixed, very large number of molecules.
A mole of A T P weighs about half a kilogram.
That means A T P carries very little energy for its weight, far less than glucose or fat.
This is why A T P works as a carrier that is used again and again, not as a store.
The reaction is exergonic, meaning that it releases energy.
The size of this release is measured as the free energy change, called delta G.
Delta G is the free energy of the products minus the free energy of the starting molecules.
A D P and P i hold less free energy than A T P and water, which makes the change negative.
Delta G for this reaction is minus seven point three kilocalories per mole.
A minus sign tells you that energy is given out.
A plus sign would mean that energy must be put in, as when the cell builds new molecules.
This free energy powers all forms of biologic work, which is why A T P is called the cell's energy currency.
The reaction needs no oxygen and happens at once.
As a result, energy is there the instant a muscle is told to contract.

## 4. Catabolism and anabolism meet at ATP

A T P links the two halves of metabolism.
The first half is catabolism, in which carbohydrates, fats and proteins are broken down with oxygen to water and carbon dioxide.
Catabolism is exergonic, and its energy is used to join A D P and P i back into A T P.
The second half is anabolism, the building of large synthesised end products from small building block precursors.
Anabolism is endergonic, meaning that it needs an input of energy.
It is paid for by splitting A T P into A D P and P i.
A T P and A D P cycle endlessly between the two halves.
A T P behaves like a rechargeable battery, charged by catabolism and drained by anabolism and every other kind of work.
On the catabolic side, energy from food turns A D P and P i back into A T P, recharging the battery.
On the anabolic side, A T P goes back to A D P and P i, because that energy is spent on building.

## 5. A limited currency

Despite its importance, A T P is kept in very small amounts, so it must be continually resynthesised.
The whole body stores only eighty to one hundred grams of A T P at rest.
That is enough to power two to three seconds of maximal exercise.
The store is small because A T P is a heavy molecule, as we saw earlier, which is why the cell recycles the same molecules over and over instead.
Across one day, a person turns over an amount of A T P comparable to a large fraction of their body weight.
Even so, the A T P level of skeletal muscle falls only under extreme exercise conditions, because resynthesis normally keeps pace with use.
The control is built in.
As A T P falls and A D P rises, that change is itself the signal that speeds up the reactions that remake A T P.
A T P must be remade at exactly the rate it is spent, and the rest of energy metabolism exists to do that.

## 6. Phosphocreatine, the energy reservoir

The fastest top up comes from phosphocreatine, or P C r, also called creatine phosphate.
It is a molecule of creatine that carries a high energy phosphate.
The enzyme creatine kinase, also called creatine phosphokinase, passes that phosphate straight to A D P.
The reaction is P C r plus A D P giving creatine plus A T P.
This reaction is anaerobic, meaning that it needs no oxygen.
It is also a single step, which makes it the fastest way a cell can remake A T P.
Cells store about four to six times more P C r than A T P.
P C r reaches its maximum energy yield in about ten seconds.
Together, A T P and P C r are the anaerobic sources of phosphate bound energy.
They power all out efforts lasting a few seconds, such as a sprint start, a jump, a heavy lift or a throw.
The reaction is reversible, so at rest A T P from the slower pathways recharges the P C r store.
The key idea is that P C r never powers work itself.
Its energy goes into putting A D P and P i back together as A T P, allowing the A T P splitting reaction to keep running.
All of this happens in a single step, in which creatine kinase hands the phosphate straight from P C r to A D P.
The small amount of energy left over is lost as heat.

## 7. Adenylate kinase, the last resort

Sometimes A T P is used faster than it is remade, and A D P builds up.
When that happens, a second enzyme steps in.
Adenylate kinase takes the phosphate from one A D P and puts it on another.
The reaction is two A D P giving A T P plus A M P, and it can run in both directions.
A M P is adenosine monophosphate, adenosine with a single phosphate.
The enzyme needs magnesium, M g two plus, to work.
This rescues one A T P from two A D P.
It is less efficient, though, because the A M P is metabolised and not recycled.
The nitrogen in A M P is removed as ammonia.
As a result, the muscle slowly loses part of its stock of adenine molecules.
Rising A M P also acts as an alarm that energy is low, which switches on the pathways that remake A T P.
This reaction runs hard in McArdle disease, where a missing enzyme starves muscle of fuel and A D P piles up.

## 8. Where the lasting supply comes from

Phosphocreatine counts as one of the six fuel sources a muscle can draw on.
It works by phosphorylating A D P, which means adding a phosphate to it, under the control of creatine kinase and adenylate kinase.
Yet these stores last only seconds.
For anything longer, A T P must be remade from food.
This happens without oxygen in the fluid of the cell, and with oxygen in the mitochondria, the cell's power stations.

## Summary

Let's pull it all together.
Food holds potential energy, but a cell can spend it only after it has been moved into A T P.
A T P is adenosine joined to three phosphates by high energy bonds.
A T P-ase splits A T P with water into A D P and P i, releasing seven point three kilocalories per mole.
That energy powers all biologic work without needing oxygen.
Catabolism of carbohydrate, fat and protein is exergonic and recharges A D P into A T P.
Anabolism is endergonic and drains A T P again.
In this way, A T P cycles like a rechargeable battery.
The body holds only eighty to one hundred grams of A T P, enough for two to three seconds of maximal exercise.
A T P must therefore be remade as fast as it is spent, and rising A D P is the signal to do so.
The fastest refill is phosphocreatine, whose phosphate is passed to A D P by creatine kinase in one anaerobic step.
Muscle holds four to six times more P C r than A T P, and P C r gives its maximum yield in about ten seconds.
That makes A T P and P C r the fuel for sprints, jumps, lifts and throws.
As a last resort, adenylate kinase turns two A D P into A T P and A M P.
This is less efficient because A M P is broken down and not recycled.
Beyond these few seconds, A T P must be remade from food.
That happens without oxygen in the fluid of the cell, and with oxygen in the mitochondria.
