## Energy Pathways Overview

Food cannot power a muscle directly.
Its energy has to be released step by step and captured as A T P, the molecule that drives every contraction.
What follows is a map of that journey.
We'll see where in the cell each step happens, which steps need oxygen, and which fuels a working muscle can call on.

## 1. Three stages from food to ATP

The energy in food reaches A T P in three stages, and each stage takes the fuel one step further from the plate.
Stage one is the digestion, absorption and assimilation of the macronutrients.
These are the nutrients we eat in large amounts, namely carbohydrate, fat and protein.
Assimilation means taking a nutrient into the body's own use.
In this stage, each large food molecule is cut into smaller subunits.
Each food gives its own subunit.
Proteins become amino acids.
Carbohydrates become simple sugars, mainly glucose.
Fats become glycerol and fatty acids.
Every one of these cuts is made by hydrolysis, a reaction in which water breaks a chemical bond.
All of this happens inside the gut.
The products are absorbed into the blood together with water, minerals and vitamins, mostly in the small intestine.
That blood passes through the liver before it reaches the rest of the body.
To do this work, the gut also secretes enzymes and bile salts, and its muscular contractions, called motility, move the contents along.
Even so, no usable energy is captured in stage one.
Stage two happens inside the cell.
Part of it runs in the cytosol, the fluid of the cell, and part in the mitochondrion, a small compartment of the cell.
Here, amino acids, glucose, and the glycerol and fatty acid units are broken down into one common product, acetyl coenzyme A, or acetyl co A.
Acetyl co A is a two carbon fragment carried by a helper molecule called coenzyme A.
Only a little A T P is made in this stage, and it comes from glucose.
In stage three, acetyl co A passes through two linked routes, the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.
The Krebs cycle is also called the citric acid cycle or the T C A cycle.
Together, these two routes break acetyl co A down completely to carbon dioxide and water, and this is where most of the A T P is made.
The cell gets rid of three waste products, which are carbon dioxide, water and ammonia.
The ammonia comes from the nitrogen of amino acids.
So whatever the fuel, every route meets at acetyl co A and then shares one final path.

## 2. Two compartments of the cell

The cell makes A T P in two places, and the place decides whether oxygen is needed.
The first is the cytosol.
The reactions in the cytosol are anaerobic, meaning that they do not use oxygen.
Two kinds of anaerobic reaction make A T P there.
One is the splitting of phosphocreatine, a high energy store that refills A T P within moments.
The other is glycolysis, which takes in glucose, glycogen, glycerol and some deaminated amino acids.
A deaminated amino acid is one whose nitrogen group has been removed.
The second place is the mitochondrion, the singular form of mitochondria.
It is an organelle wrapped in two membranes, and it is often called the power station of the cell.
Its reactions are aerobic, meaning that they depend on oxygen.
They are the Krebs cycle and the respiratory chain.
The respiratory chain is another name for the electron transport chain, which passes electrons from hydrogen on to oxygen.
The fuels for these mitochondrial reactions are fatty acids, pyruvate made from glucose, and some deaminated amino acids.
Both compartments end in the same product, A T P that powers biologic work such as contraction.

## 3. The three pathways at a glance

Three linked pathways do the work of stages two and three, and each has its own place, its own inputs and its own products.
Two carrier molecules, N A D H and F A D H two, link the pathways.
These carriers pick up hydrogen in one pathway and deliver it to another.
The first pathway is glycolysis, which runs in the cytosol.
Its input is carbohydrate, either glucose from the blood or glucose from glycogen.
Glycerol from fat can also join partway through.
Glycolysis does not need oxygen.
It produces a little A T P, hydrogen loaded on N A D H, and pyruvate.
That pyruvate either becomes lactate or enters the mitochondrion.
The second pathway is the Krebs cycle, which runs in the matrix, the inner space of the mitochondrion.
It is fed by acetyl co A from carbohydrate, fat and protein.
The Krebs cycle runs only when oxygen is available, but it does not use oxygen itself.
The cycle releases carbon dioxide, a little A T P, and much hydrogen loaded on N A D H and F A D H two.
The third pathway is oxidative phosphorylation, which runs on the inner mitochondrial membrane.
It takes in the hydrogen carried by N A D H and F A D H two, along with oxygen.
This pathway does need oxygen.
In return, it makes most of the A T P, and water.
The Krebs cycle never touches oxygen, yet it stops without it.
This is because it needs a steady return of empty carriers, N A D plus and F A D.
Only oxidative phosphorylation, which does use oxygen, can empty the loaded carriers.
All three pathways are driven by oxidation and reduction reactions, known as redox reactions.
These are the paired loss and gain of electrons that underlie all energy metabolism.

## 4. Six fuel sources of a muscle

A working muscle can draw on six sources of fuel, and three terms are needed to understand the list.
A triacylglycerol is a fat molecule, also called a triglyceride, made of one glycerol and three fatty acids.
Glycogen is the storage form of glucose, a large branched chain of glucose units.
A carbon skeleton is what is left of an amino acid once its nitrogen has been removed.
The first source is the triacylglycerol and glycogen stored within the muscle cells themselves, the fuel closest to hand.
The second is blood glucose, which the muscle takes up from the circulation.
The third is free fatty acids, which are fatty acids released from fat stores and carried in the blood, and these are delivered to the muscle.
The fourth is the carbon skeletons of amino acids, which come from the muscle's own protein and from the liver.
The fifth source is glycolysis, the first phase of glucose breakdown, whose anaerobic reactions make A T P without oxygen.
The sixth is phosphocreatine, which phosphorylates A D P.
This means it hands a phosphate to A D P to remake A T P, under the control of the enzymes creatine kinase and adenylate kinase.
Which of these sources the muscle leans on depends mostly on how hard and how long it works.

## 5. Which source when

Each source differs in how fast it delivers A T P and how long it lasts, so each suits a different kind of effort.
The stores of A T P and phosphocreatine dominate all out efforts of up to about ten seconds.
A one hundred metre sprint, a jump or a heavy lift are efforts of this kind.
Glycolysis ending in lactate dominates hard efforts of roughly thirty seconds to two minutes, such as a four hundred metre run or a one hundred metre swim.
It does so because pyruvate is made faster than the mitochondria can take it.
Aerobic metabolism dominates anything longer than a few minutes, such as distance running or cycling.
Within aerobic metabolism, fat is prominent at low intensity and long duration, and carbohydrate takes over as intensity rises.
These times are approximate.
The systems never switch on one after another.
Instead, all of them run together, and only their shares shift.

## Summary

Food energy reaches A T P in three stages.
First comes digestion into subunits, namely amino acids, glucose, glycerol and fatty acids.
No energy is captured at this stage.
Second comes breakdown inside the cell to acetyl co A.
Third comes the complete breakdown of acetyl co A to carbon dioxide and water, which yields most of the A T P.
The cytosol runs the anaerobic reactions, which are glycolysis and the splitting of phosphocreatine.
The mitochondrion runs the aerobic Krebs cycle and electron transport chain.
Glycolysis needs no oxygen, and the Krebs cycle needs oxygen indirectly.
Oxidative phosphorylation uses oxygen and makes most of the A T P.
A muscle has six fuel sources.
They are its own triacylglycerol and glycogen, blood glucose, free fatty acids, amino acid carbon skeletons, anaerobic glycolysis, and phosphocreatine.
A T P and phosphocreatine dominate efforts of up to about ten seconds.
Glycolysis dominates efforts of about thirty seconds to two minutes, and aerobic metabolism anything longer than a few minutes.
All the systems are always running together.
