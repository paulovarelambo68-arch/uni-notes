## Energy and Thermodynamics

Every movement, heartbeat and thought needs energy.
In the human body, all of it comes from food.
In this note, we'll look at what energy is, the two rules it always obeys, and the kinds of work it lets the body do.

## 1. Energy and work

Let's start with what energy actually is.
Energy is the capacity to do work.
It cannot be seen directly.
It shows itself only when something changes, such as a muscle shortening.
The more biologic work the body does, the more energy it transfers.
Work is expressed in mechanical units, but nutrition measures energy in kilocalories.
One kilocalorie is the heat needed to warm one kilogram of water by one degree Celsius.
The study of how energy flows and is exchanged within a living system is called bioenergetics.

## 2. The first law

The first law of thermodynamics states that energy cannot be created or destroyed.
It can only change from one form into another, without being used up.
This is the principle of conservation of energy.
So the body does not produce or consume energy.
It transforms it, for example turning the chemical energy of food into movement and heat.
This law explains energy balance.
Energy eaten equals energy given out as work and heat, plus energy stored.
The body's stores, mainly fat, therefore change only when intake and output differ.

## 3. Potential and kinetic energy

Energy is either stored or moving.
The total energy of a system is its potential energy plus its kinetic energy.
Potential energy is stored energy that comes from structure or position.
Examples are water held at the top of a dam, the chemical bonds of a glucose molecule, and a drawn bow.
Kinetic energy is the energy of motion.
Falling water is one example, as are a moving limb and heat, which is the motion of molecules.
The image of water held behind a dam also describes how mitochondria, the cell's power stations, store energy.

## 4. The second law

The second law describes which way energy moves.
Each time potential energy changes form, it loses some of its capacity to do work.
Stored energy turns into energy of motion, and at every step a little of it ends up as heat.
Heat is the form that is hardest to use.
In the end, all of it becomes heat.
None of the energy disappears, since whatever one form loses, another gains.
What is lost is its usefulness.
Picture water that has run downhill.
It is still there, but it can no longer turn a wheel.
The growing disorder this brings is called entropy.
Its effects are familiar.
A battery runs down even when it is not used.
No energy conversion is one hundred per cent efficient.
Some energy escapes as heat at every step.
In the same way, the energy in food ends as body heat once it has done its work.

## 5. Reactions that release or absorb energy

Chemical reactions can be sorted by what happens to their energy.
The key quantity is free energy, the part of a system's energy that is available to do work.
Some reactions release energy to their surroundings, and these are called exergonic.
Giving energy away makes their free energy fall, which is why they are said to run downhill.
Other reactions do the opposite, absorbing energy and storing it, and these are called endergonic.
Their free energy rises, so they run uphill.
Every piece of biologic work, such as building a molecule, needs an uphill reaction of this kind.
The body uses the two kinds as a pair.
The energy released by a downhill reaction is used at once to drive an uphill one.
This is called a coupled reaction.
Coupling is how the energy of food is loaded into A T P, the molecule that cells spend to do work.
The main example of uphill work is biosynthesis, the building of new molecules.
The energy bound in one substance is passed directly to others to raise their potential energy.
This transfer joins atoms of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen into new molecules and tissues.
These include bone, and the fatty membrane around each cell, which is made of lipid.

## 6. Photosynthesis and respiration

Energy exists in six forms.
They are chemical, mechanical, heat, light, electrical and nuclear.
Each can be converted into the others, and life depends on two such conversions.
The sun releases energy by nuclear fusion, and that energy reaches the earth as light.
In photosynthesis, plants capture that light with the green pigment chlorophyll.
They use its energy to build carbon dioxide and water into glucose, releasing oxygen.
Photosynthesis is endergonic, because the sun's energy ends up stored as potential energy in carbohydrates, lipids and proteins.
Overall, six molecules of carbon dioxide and six of water, plus light energy, give glucose and six molecules of oxygen.
Respiration here means cellular respiration, the process inside cells that releases energy from food, rather than breathing.
It is the reverse of photosynthesis, and it is exergonic.
Cells turn glucose and oxygen back into carbon dioxide and water.
The stored energy is released and coupled to making A T P.
Overall, glucose and six molecules of oxygen give six molecules of carbon dioxide, six of water, and energy.
Animals, then, live on solar energy that plants stored first.
That stored energy is like a rechargeable battery, which the plant charges and the body runs down.

## 7. Three forms of biologic work

The energy released by respiration pays for three forms of biologic work, all through A T P.
The first is chemical work, which is biosynthesis.
This includes building glucose into glycogen, its storage form, and building glycerol and fatty acids into triacylglycerol, also called triglyceride.
Building amino acids into protein is another.
The second is mechanical work, which is muscle contraction.
Think of the arm muscles bending the elbow, or the heart pumping blood.
The third is transport work, which moves substances among cells and across their membranes.
One example is the pump that moves sodium out of a cell and potassium in.
Both travel against their concentration gradients, from where they are scarce to where they are plentiful.
In every case, the energy comes from food and passes through A T P.
Much of it becomes heat straight away, because no step is fully efficient.
The part that does work becomes heat later, once the movement, molecule or gradient it produced is undone.

## Summary

Let's pull it all together.
Energy is the capacity to do work, and nutrition measures it in kilocalories.
The first law says energy is never created or destroyed, only transformed.
The second law says every transfer lowers its capacity for work, until it all ends as heat.
Potential energy is stored by structure or position, and kinetic energy is the energy of motion.
Exergonic reactions release free energy and endergonic reactions absorb it.
Coupled reactions therefore use exergonic reactions to drive endergonic ones.
Photosynthesis stores the sun's energy in glucose, and respiration releases it to make A T P.
Finally, A T P pays for chemical, mechanical and transport work.
