## Enzymes

Every pathway that releases energy in the body is a chain of chemical reactions.
On their own, most of these reactions would run far too slowly to keep a cell alive.
Enzymes are the proteins that speed them up.
Controlling them is how the cell sets the pace of each pathway.

## 1. What an enzyme does

We begin with the basics.
An enzyme is a protein catalyst.
A catalyst speeds up a chemical reaction without being consumed or changed by it.
This means one enzyme molecule can be used again and again.
The molecule an enzyme acts on is its substrate, and the molecule it makes is the product.
Enzymes work by lowering the activation energy.
That is the energy barrier molecules must get over before they can react.
Think of an enzyme as a tunnel through a hill instead of a climb over it.
Less energy is needed to get through, so the journey is quicker.
Yet it still starts and ends in the same valleys.
In the same way, an enzyme changes how fast a reaction goes.
It never changes whether the reaction can go, or how much energy it releases.
That energy is set by the molecules at the start and the end.
Enzyme names usually end in ase, as in dehydrogenase, and often say what the enzyme does.
For example, lactate dehydrogenase removes hydrogen from lactate.

## 2. Lock and key

To act on its substrate, an enzyme must first hold it.
Each enzyme has an active site, a pocket whose shape fits its substrate.
If the two meet in a perfect fit, they join into an enzyme-substrate complex.
Forming this complex switches the enzyme on.
Only the right shape fits.
This lock and key mechanism ensures that each enzyme acts only on its specific substrate, to do one particular job.
The property is called specificity.
Take the enzyme maltase as it digests maltose, a sugar made of two glucose units.
First, maltose fits the active site of maltase and forms the enzyme-substrate complex.
Second, the complex splits with the help of a molecule of water, and releases the product, which is two glucose molecules.
Third, maltase comes out unchanged and is free to take another maltose.
The same enzyme goes round this cycle again and again.

## 3. Coenzymes

Many enzymes also need a small helper to work.
Coenzymes are organic substances that are not proteins, and they help an enzyme act.
A coenzyme binds to the enzyme alongside the substrate.
During the reaction, it takes a small chemical group off the substrate.
Lactate dehydrogenase, for instance, can remove hydrogen from lactate only because a coenzyme, N A D plus, is there to accept it.
The coenzyme then leaves carrying that group, and hands it on in a later reaction.
In short, the job of a coenzyme is to accept and carry a small chemical group, which lets its enzyme complete the reaction.
Coenzymes are reused after each reaction, which is why the body needs only small amounts.
Many are built from B vitamins.
Three coenzymes appear in almost every pathway of energy metabolism.
N A D plus is made from the vitamin niacin, and it carries hydrogen.
F A D is made from the vitamin riboflavin, and it also carries hydrogen.
Coenzyme A is made from the vitamin pantothenic acid.
It carries a two carbon unit called an acetyl group.

## 4. Six classes of enzyme

Enzymes are sorted into six classes, each named after the kind of reaction it speeds up.
One term to know here is A T P, the cell's energy carrier.
Let's take the classes one at a time.
Oxidoreductases move hydrogen or electrons from one molecule to another.
Lactate dehydrogenase is one of them.
Transferases move a chemical group from one molecule to another.
An example is hexokinase, which moves a phosphate from A T P onto glucose.
Hydrolases split a bond by adding water.
Lipase, which splits fat, is a hydrolase.
Lyases add a small molecule to a double bond, or remove one to leave a double bond.
They do this without using water to split and without moving electrons.
Carbonic anhydrase is a lyase, joining water to carbon dioxide and splitting them apart again.
Isomerases rearrange the atoms inside one molecule, as phosphoglycerate mutase does.
Ligases join two molecules using energy from A T P.
Pyruvate carboxylase, which joins carbon dioxide to pyruvate, is a ligase.
Several of these enzymes return in later pathways.
Hexokinase, for one, carries out the first step in the breakdown of glucose.

## 5. Speed, temperature and pH

Enzymes do not all work at the same speed.
Their speed is measured by the turnover number.
This is simply how many substrate molecules one enzyme molecule turns into product in a given time.
It is much like counting how many customers one cashier serves per minute.
Chemists count molecules in moles.
A mole is about six times ten to the power of twenty-three molecules, or roughly six hundred thousand billion billion.
Written formally, the turnover number is moles of substrate converted per mole of enzyme per unit time.
The turnover number is not fixed, since temperature and p H alter enzyme activity.
As temperature rises, molecules collide more often, so the rate rises up to an optimum temperature.
Above that optimum, the rate falls.
The reason is that heat distorts the shape of the protein and its active site, and as a result the substrate no longer fits.
This loss of shape is called denaturation.
The rate therefore climbs like a hill to a peak at the optimum temperature, with faster collisions on the way up and denaturation on the way down.
Acidity acts in a similar way.
Each enzyme has an optimum p H at which it works best.
Away from it, the electrical charges that hold the active site in shape change, and the rate falls.
This matters in hard exercise.
Working muscle becomes warmer and more acid, which changes the speed of its enzymes.
Two digestive enzymes are good examples of an optimum p H.
Pepsin, a digestive enzyme of the stomach, peaks in strong acid at about p H two.
Trypsin, a digestive enzyme of the small intestine, peaks in slightly alkaline conditions at about p H eight.
Each enzyme is tuned to the place in the body where it works.

## 6. Inhibition

Other molecules can also slow an enzyme down.
These inhibitors reduce enzyme activity, and therefore slow the reaction.
Their two kinds differ in where they bind.
A competitive inhibitor resembles the substrate and binds to the active site, which keeps the real substrate out.
A noncompetitive inhibitor does not resemble the substrate and does not bind to the active site.
Instead, it binds at another site and changes the enzyme's structure, reducing its ability to catalyse the reaction.
A competitive inhibitor is a wrong key jammed in the lock, while a noncompetitive inhibitor bends the lock itself.
The difference matters when more substrate is added.
Extra substrate wins more of the contests for the active site.
As a result, more substrate can outcompete a competitive inhibitor.
More substrate cannot help against a noncompetitive inhibitor, because the lock stays bent however many keys arrive.
The cell uses inhibition to control its pathways.
In a chain of reactions, the slowest step sets the pace of the whole chain, like the narrowest point of a road.
The enzyme of that step is the rate limiting enzyme.
The cell speeds the pathway up or slows it down by activating or inhibiting this enzyme.
Often the inhibitor is the pathway's own end product, the molecule made at its last step.
When the cell has more of this product than it needs, the product builds up and binds to the rate limiting enzyme.
That slows the whole pathway.
When the cell uses the product up, the brake comes off and the pathway speeds up again.
This is end product inhibition.
It works like a thermostat that turns the heating off once the room is warm.
You can see it at work in glycolysis, with the enzyme phosphofructokinase.

## Summary

Let's pull it all together.
An enzyme is a protein catalyst that speeds a reaction by lowering its activation energy.
It is not used up, and it does not change the energy the reaction releases.
Its active site fits one substrate like a lock and key.
The two form an enzyme-substrate complex, which splits to release the product and free the enzyme.
Coenzymes are helpers built from B vitamins.
N A D plus, from niacin, and F A D, from riboflavin, carry hydrogen.
Coenzyme A, from pantothenic acid, carries an acetyl group.
The six classes are oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases and ligases.
Speed is measured by the turnover number, which peaks at an optimum temperature and optimum p H.
It falls when the enzyme denatures.
Competitive inhibitors block the active site and can be outcompeted by more substrate.
Noncompetitive inhibitors bind elsewhere and change the enzyme's shape.
Finally, the cell paces each pathway through its rate limiting enzyme, often by end product inhibition.
