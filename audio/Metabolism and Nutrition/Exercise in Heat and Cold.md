## Exercise in Heat and Cold

Exercise in the heat asks one supply of blood to feed the muscles and to carry heat out to the skin.
In this note, we'll follow what happens when that blood runs short, all the way up to the illnesses heat can cause.
After that, we turn to the cold, where the body protects its core at the expense of the muscles.

## 1. Skin and muscle compete for blood

We start with the central conflict.
In the heat, the skin vessels widen, or dilate, to carry heat from the core to the surface.
The skin becomes engorged with a large volume of blood, which pools in the skin instead of flowing straight back.
Less blood returns to the heart, which means venous return falls.
The central blood volume, held in the large veins and the heart, falls with it.
A heart that fills less ejects less with each beat, so stroke volume falls.
That is why the major cardiovascular burden of heat stress results from impaired venous return.
The body compensates in two ways.
It constricts the vessels of the kidneys, gut and liver, known as the renal and splanchnic beds.
This pushes their blood back toward the heart and diverts cardiac output to the skin and the working muscles.
It also raises heart rate.
Cardiac output, or C O, is stroke volume times heart rate.
Stroke volume is shortened to S V, and heart rate to H R.
Put simply, C O equals S V times H R.
A faster heart can therefore make up for a smaller stroke volume, but only up to a point.
Consider how cardiac output is shared out as oxygen uptake, a measure of effort, rises.
Both are measured in litres per minute.
Cardiac output goes to the muscles, to the visceral organs and elsewhere, and to the skin.
In temperate conditions, the skin's share stays narrow, and output reaches about twenty-five litres per minute at an oxygen uptake of four litres per minute.
In hot conditions, the skin's share widens with effort, taking litres that the muscles do not get.
Exercise in the heat ends at an oxygen uptake of three litres per minute.
Measurements at twenty-six and forty-three degrees Celsius, compared at the same oxygen uptake of about two point four litres per minute, show the trade.
In the heat, rectal temperature was thirty-nine point two degrees Celsius, against thirty-eight point zero.
Stroke volume was eighty-eight millilitres per beat, against one hundred and ten.
Heart rate was one hundred and ninety-seven beats per minute, against one hundred and fifty-seven.
As a result, cardiac output stayed close, at sixteen point nine litres per minute against seventeen point seven.
Now follow each measure as oxygen uptake rises.
At forty-three degrees, rectal temperature climbs steeply.
Stroke volume sits about twenty millilitres per beat lower, and heart rate runs twenty to forty beats per minute higher.
Cardiac output matches the cooler condition at first, then falls behind and stops early.
That happens once the faster heart can no longer cover the smaller stroke volume.

## 2. Hormones that save water and salt

Sweating costs water and sodium.
Exercise with a loss of body water triggers two hormones.
Aldosterone comes from the cortex of the adrenal gland, which sits on top of the kidney, and it makes the kidneys retain sodium.
Antidiuretic hormone, or A D H, also called vasopressin, comes from the posterior pituitary gland at the base of the brain.
It makes the kidneys retain water.

## 3. Heat illnesses in order of severity

All four heat illnesses result from strenuous exercise combined with environmental heat.
Together they form a scale of increasing severity.
Heat cramps are the mildest, and they are not defined by a temperature.
They are severe, painful cramping of large muscles, triggered by sodium loss and dehydration.
Heavy sweaters get them most often, and a liberal intake of sodium and water prevents them.
Their warning signs are painful large muscle cramps, profuse sweating and excessive thirst.
Heat exhaustion is mild to moderate, with a core temperature from thirty-eight point five up to about forty degrees Celsius.
It is an inability to sustain cardiac output, often with hot skin and dehydration.
Its warning signs are fatigue, headache, chills and goose bumps, and nausea.
Heat injury is moderate to severe, with a core temperature above forty degrees Celsius.
It is defined by injury to organs or tissues, and no separate warning signs are given for it.
Heat stroke is severe, with a high core temperature, above forty degrees Celsius.
It is profound dysfunction of the central nervous system, with organ or tissue injury, and it can leave profound neuropsychiatric impairment.
Liver damage and rhabdomyolysis, the breakdown of damaged muscle fibres that release their contents into the blood, can complicate it.
Its warning signs are cessation of sweating, confusion and loss of consciousness.
The change from profuse sweating to no sweating, together with confusion, marks the emergency.
Taken in order of severity, the signs tell a story.
At first, the body is fighting the heat, sweating hard and thirsty.
Finally, it is losing, with cooling failed and the brain affected.

## 4. From heat strain to heat stroke

Heat strain damages organs by two routes that feed each other.
In the first route, blood goes to the skin and muscles while the gut vessels constrict.
The gut wall becomes ischaemic, meaning short of blood.
Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, or R O S and R N S, are unstable molecules that attack cells, and they make the wall leaky.
Bacterial toxins cross into the blood, a state called endotoxaemia.
They set off an exaggerated acute phase response, an inflammatory reaction of the whole body.
Through nitric oxide, or N O, this response worsens the ischaemia.
This loop is a vicious circle, like a fire that heats its own fuel.
In the second route, heat shock and ischaemia strike cells directly, in the brain, vessel lining, intestine, liver, kidneys, heart and muscles.
Some cells are injured, with clotting disorders and inflammation.
Others die, by planned self-destruction or by bursting, known as apoptosis and necrosis.
Still others become tolerant by making heat shock proteins, or H S P, which protect other proteins from heat damage.
Every path except tolerance leads to exertional heat injury and heat stroke, the end point of both routes.
These damage the brain and organs through fever, shock, haemorrhage, stroke, rhabdomyolysis and D I C.
D I C stands for disseminated intravascular coagulation, widespread clotting inside small vessels.

## 5. Acclimation to heat

Repeated exercise in the heat over several days brings three groups of changes.
The first is a rise in plasma volume.
Albumin, the main protein in plasma, normally leaks slowly out of the vessels into the fluid around the cells.
Exercise in the heat speeds up the lymph, which carries this albumin back into the blood, and the liver also makes more of it.
With more albumin in the plasma, its oncotic pressure rises, and this pull holds more water inside the vessels.
The rise is temporary, back to normal after about ten days, but it buys time for the other adaptations.
The second change is that the larger plasma volume reduces the cardiovascular strain of exercising in the heat.
The extra plasma means more blood returns to the heart, so it fills better and pumps more with each beat.
With a bigger stroke volume, the heart needs fewer beats for the same work, which lowers heart rate.
It can still pump more blood each minute, which raises cardiac output.
That extra output can supply the skin without taking blood from the muscles.
More heat is carried to the surface and lost, and core temperature stays lower.
The third change is in sweating.
Sweating starts earlier, spreads over more of the body and is more dilute.
This improves evaporative heat loss and prevents a dangerous loss of sodium.
The set point, the temperature the brain defends, falls as well.

## 6. Defending heat in the cold

Cold stress is any environmental condition that causes a loss of body heat.
A fall in core or skin temperature triggers the body's defences.
The P O A H, the preoptic anterior hypothalamus, is the brain's thermostat.
It brings on peripheral vasoconstriction, nonshivering thermogenesis and shivering.
The cerebral cortex brings on behavioural defences.
Dangerous conditions are hard to define, because heat loss depends on the body as well as the weather.
More subcutaneous fat, the fat under the skin, means more insulation.
More inactive muscle in the limbs adds insulation as well.
A smaller ratio of body surface area to mass means less heat loss.
That is why a child, with a large surface for its mass, cools faster than an adult.
Women have more subcutaneous fat, which is an advantage, but less active muscle to make heat, which is a disadvantage.

## 7. Muscle and fuel in the cold

Cold also changes how muscle works and which fuel it burns.
A cooled muscle recruits its fibres differently, and as a result its contractile force falls.
Its shortening velocity and power fall too.
The superficial muscles are affected, while the deep ones are spared.
As fatigue rises, metabolic heat production falls.
In long endurance exercise, running down energy reserves therefore opens the way to hypothermia, a dangerous fall in core temperature.
The catecholamines are epinephrine, also called adrenaline, and norepinephrine.
Normally, a rise in catecholamines raises the use of free fatty acids.
In the cold, catecholamine secretion rises, but free fatty acid use does not.
The reason is that vasoconstriction in subcutaneous fat reduces free fatty acid mobilisation.
Less blood flows through the fat, which means fewer fatty acids are released and carried away.
Blood glucose is well maintained, but muscle glycogen is used faster.
This matters because hypoglycaemia, meaning low blood glucose, suppresses shivering.
Running out of carbohydrate removes a key defence against the cold.

## 8. Three patterns of cold adaptation

Repeated cold stress leads to one of three patterns, and two questions decide which.
The first question is whether the cold actually causes a loss of body heat.
If it does not, the result is habituation, in which shivering and skin vasoconstriction become blunted.
The second question is whether extra metabolic heat is enough to defend body temperature once heat is lost.
If it is enough, the result is metabolic acclimatisation.
Shivering thermogenesis is enhanced, and possibly nonshivering thermogenesis too.
If heat is lost and extra heat production is not enough, the result is insulative acclimatisation.
Skin vasoconstriction is enhanced, and improved muscle blood flow redistributes body heat toward the subcutaneous shell.

## Summary

Let's pull it all together.
In the heat, blood pools in the dilated skin, and venous return and stroke volume fall.
The body compensates by constricting the renal and splanchnic beds and raising heart rate.
Heart rate reaches about one hundred and ninety-seven beats per minute at forty-three degrees Celsius, against one hundred and fifty-seven at twenty-six degrees, until cardiac output falls behind.
Aldosterone retains sodium, and A D H retains water.
Heat illnesses run from heat cramps through heat exhaustion, at thirty-eight point five to about forty degrees Celsius.
Next come heat injury and then heat stroke, above forty degrees.
Heat stroke is marked by no sweating and confusion.
It follows two linked routes, gut ischaemia with endotoxaemia and direct heat shock to cells.
Heat acclimation raises plasma volume, lowers heart rate, and makes sweat earlier and more dilute.
In the cold, muscle loses force, free fatty acid mobilisation falls and glycogen is used faster.
Repeated cold leads to habituation, metabolic acclimatisation or insulative acclimatisation.
