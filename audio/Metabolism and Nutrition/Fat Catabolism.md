## Fat Catabolism

Fat is the body's largest store of energy, far larger than its store of sugar.
In this note, we'll follow fat from storage to the working muscle.
We'll see how fat is taken apart and fed into the same final route that burns glucose, and why it is a slower fuel.

## 1. The largest fuel store

The body keeps most of its energy reserve as fat.
It stores this fat as triacylglycerol, also called triglyceride.
This is a molecule made of three long chains called fatty acids, attached to a small backbone.
Stored fat is the body's most plentiful source of potential energy, meaning energy held in chemical bonds until it is released.
In a young adult male, the fat cells, or adipocytes, hold sixty thousand to one hundred thousand kilocalories.
The triacylglycerol inside the muscles adds about three thousand kilocalories.
The body's whole store of carbohydrate, by contrast, holds only about two thousand kilocalories.
Fat is the better store, packing more energy into less weight.
A gram of fat holds nine kilocalories, against four kilocalories for a gram of carbohydrate.
Gram for gram, fat also yields about two and a half times as much A T P.
A reserve of carbohydrate would make the body much heavier.
How much energy comes from fat varies widely.
Fat supplies thirty to eighty per cent of the energy for biologic work.
The share depends on nutritional status, that is, what the person has eaten and how recently.
It also depends on training level, and on the intensity and duration of the activity.
Fat becomes the primary fuel for exercise and recovery when intense, long exercise has depleted both blood glucose and muscle glycogen.

## 2. Three sources of fat

A working muscle can draw on fat from three places, one inside the muscle and two in the blood.
The first is triacylglycerol stored within the muscle fibre.
It lies as droplets close to the mitochondria, where fuel is burned with oxygen.
The second is triacylglycerol in the blood, inside lipoprotein complexes.
These are parcels of fat wrapped in protein, so that they can travel in watery blood.
The third is free fatty acids in the blood, cut loose from the triacylglycerol of adipose tissue, the body's fat tissue.
Whatever its source, the triacylglycerol must be split into its parts before the muscle can use it.

## 3. Lipolysis and transport

To follow how fat is split, it helps first to know its parts.
The backbone of a triacylglycerol is glycerol, a small molecule of three carbons.
Each fatty acid is a long zigzag chain of carbon atoms carrying hydrogen, and that hydrogen holds most of the energy.
One fatty acid is attached to each carbon of glycerol.
Picture the three fatty acids hanging from the glycerol like the teeth of a comb.
Triacylglycerol, triglyceride, T A G and T G are all names for this one molecule.
Splitting this molecule is called lipolysis.
It is a hydrolysis, a reaction that breaks a bond by adding water, and the enzyme is a lipase.
Each of the three bonds uses one water molecule.
Overall, triacylglycerol plus three water molecules gives glycerol plus three fatty acids.
Each water molecule splits, giving one hydrogen to glycerol and the remaining oxygen and hydrogen, the O H, to the end of the fatty acid.
In fat cells the enzyme is hormone sensitive lipase, named for the fact that hormones switch it on.
It is activated by epinephrine, which is adrenaline, along with norepinephrine, glucagon and growth hormone.
As a result, the hormones released during exercise speed up fat breakdown and the delivery of fatty acids to active muscle.
The freed fatty acids leave the fat cell and enter the blood plasma.
They do not dissolve in water, so they travel bound to the plasma protein albumin.
The fat stored inside muscle fibres is split in the same way.
Its fatty acids, however, are released inside the muscle itself and need no journey through the blood.
Now let's follow the whole path from storage to A T P.
Adipose tissue holds the triacylglycerols, and hormone sensitive lipase splits them into fatty acids and glycerol.
Albumin ferries the fatty acids through the blood plasma into the muscle, where they arrive as free fatty acids, or F F A.
The glycerol takes a separate path, linked to glucose because it can be fed into the same pathway that breaks down glucose, or used to make new glucose.
In the mitochondrion, the fatty acids become acetyl co A.
This passes through the citric acid cycle and electron transport to give A T P.
Oxygen arrives from the red blood cells to finish the process.

## 4. The route of glycerol

After lipolysis, glycerol and the fatty acids go separate ways, and glycerol has the shorter journey.
It gains a phosphate and becomes three-phospho-glyceraldehyde.
This molecule is found halfway down glycolysis, the pathway that splits glucose into pyruvate.
From there it follows the rest of glycolysis to pyruvate.
On the way, glycerol makes A T P by substrate level phosphorylation.
In this process, a phosphate passes directly from a fuel molecule to A D P.
Glycerol also passes hydrogen to N A D plus, the carrier that takes hydrogen to the electron transport chain.
The pyruvate is then oxidised in the Krebs cycle.
The complete breakdown of one glycerol molecule yields nineteen A T P.
Alternatively, the liver can use glycerol to make new glucose.

## 5. Beta oxidation of fatty acids

The fatty acids hold most of the energy in fat, and their route to A T P has more steps.
The whole sequence of fat breakdown runs in seven steps.
First, triacylglycerol is broken down to free fatty acids and glycerol.
Second, the free fatty acids are transported in the blood.
Third, the muscle takes up free fatty acids from the blood.
Fourth, the fatty acids are prepared for breakdown.
Fifth, the activated fatty acid enters the muscle mitochondria.
Sixth, beta oxidation breaks the fatty acid down to acetyl co A, generating N A D H and F A D H two.
Seventh, the Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain oxidise the products together.
The first three steps are lipolysis and transport, and the last four happen inside the muscle fibre.
Preparation means activation, in which a carrier molecule called coenzyme A is attached to the fatty acid.
This tags it for breakdown at a small cost, the equivalent of two A T P.
The activated fatty acid cannot cross the inner membrane of the mitochondrion alone.
A carrier called carnitine moves it into the matrix.
In the matrix, beta oxidation cuts the chain two carbons at a time from its end.
Each cut releases one acetyl co A, a two carbon fragment bound to coenzyme A.
Each cut also loads one F A D and one N A D plus with hydrogen, making F A D H two and N A D H.
The shorter chain picks up a new coenzyme A and goes round again, until it has all become acetyl co A.
It is like slicing a long loaf two slices at a time until nothing is left.
An eighteen carbon fatty acid passes through eight rounds.
The last round splits a four carbon piece into two.
The result is nine acetyl co A, with eight F A D H two and eight N A D H.
The acetyl co A enters the Krebs cycle.
All the hydrogen goes to the electron transport chain, where it drives A T P production and oxygen finally accepts it.
Because of this route, fat can be burned only with oxygen.
Glucose can make some A T P without oxygen in glycolysis, but fatty acids have no route outside the mitochondrion.
Their loaded carriers can be emptied only when oxygen takes the hydrogen at the end of the chain.
In the end, the glycerol and fatty acid routes meet in the citric acid cycle, which gives A T P, carbon dioxide and more hydrogen.
All the hydrogen flows to the electron transport chain.

## 6. ATP from one fat molecule

Adding up both routes gives the yield of one whole triacylglycerol.
Each eighteen carbon fatty acid converts one hundred and forty-seven A D P to A T P during beta oxidation and the Krebs cycle.
This counts the A T P from its eight N A D H, eight F A D H two and nine acetyl co A, minus the cost of activation.
One glycerol, through glycolysis and the Krebs cycle, gives nineteen A T P.
The three fatty acids, each of eighteen carbons, go through beta oxidation and the Krebs cycle.
Together they give three times one hundred and forty-seven, or four hundred and forty-one A T P.
Add glycerol's nineteen, and the total is four hundred and sixty A T P for each triacylglycerol.
One glucose, fully oxidised, nets thirty-two A T P.
A fat molecule therefore yields over fourteen times as much, mainly because it is much larger.
These fat totals use the older values of three A T P per N A D H and two per F A D H two.
On the modern count they would be about a fifth lower, but fat stays far richer than glucose.

## 7. Why fat is the slower fuel

Despite its huge store, fat cannot release energy as fast as carbohydrate, for two reasons.
The first is that its route is long.
Fat must be mobilised, carried, taken up, activated and moved into the mitochondria before it yields any A T P.
The second is that it needs more oxygen for each A T P than carbohydrate does.
The reason is that a fatty acid contains almost no oxygen of its own.
With a limited oxygen supply, fat makes A T P more slowly.
As a result, fat alone can make A T P only about half as fast as carbohydrate can, so it supports only about half the exercise intensity.
This is why pace drops when muscle glycogen runs out in a long race.
Fat also burns only while the Krebs cycle keeps turning, and that depends on carbohydrate.

## Summary

Fat is the body's largest fuel store, with sixty thousand to one hundred thousand kilocalories in adipocytes.
It supplies thirty to eighty per cent of the energy for biologic work.
Muscle draws fat from droplets in its own fibres, from lipoproteins in the blood, and from free fatty acids released by adipose tissue.
Hormone sensitive lipase, switched on by exercise hormones, splits each triacylglycerol into glycerol and three fatty acids.
The fatty acids travel to muscle bound to albumin.
Glycerol enters glycolysis at three-phospho-glyceraldehyde and yields nineteen A T P, or the liver makes glucose from it.
Each fatty acid is activated with coenzyme A, carried into the mitochondrion by carnitine, and cut two carbons at a time by beta oxidation.
Each round gives one acetyl co A, one N A D H and one F A D H two, which the Krebs cycle and electron transport chain oxidise.
That is why fat burns only with oxygen.
An eighteen carbon fatty acid yields one hundred and forty-seven A T P.
A whole triacylglycerol yields four hundred and sixty, against thirty-two for one glucose.
Because its route is long and costly in oxygen, fat alone sustains only about half the power of carbohydrate.
