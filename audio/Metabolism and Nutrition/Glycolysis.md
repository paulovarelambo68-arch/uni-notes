## Glycolysis

Every movement you make runs on energy taken from food, and sugar is the fuel the body can reach most quickly.
In this note, we'll follow the first pathway that breaks sugar down, how it earns a small but very fast profit of energy, and how the cell decides how hard to run it.

## 1. The energy in glucose

Let's start with the fuel itself.
Carbohydrate's main job in the body is to supply energy for cellular work, mostly in the form of glucose, a simple sugar.
Broken down completely, glucose reacts with oxygen to give carbon dioxide, water and energy.
A glucose molecule is made of six carbons, twelve hydrogens and six oxygens.
One mole of glucose reacts with six moles of oxygen to give six moles of carbon dioxide, six moles of water and six hundred and eighty-six kilocalories.
Only part of those six hundred and eighty-six kilocalories is captured in A T P, the cell's energy currency.
The rest escapes as heat.
That reaction describes the complete breakdown of glucose, and oxygen is needed only at its very end.
There, oxygen collects the hydrogen removed from the fuel.
Before that point, glucose is broken down in stages.
The first stage, glycolysis, frees a small part of the energy with no oxygen at all.
That makes carbohydrate the only fuel that can yield A T P without oxygen.
Fat, by contrast, releases all of its energy in the later stages that need oxygen.
Carbohydrate also helps fat to burn.
Its breakdown products keep the Krebs cycle turning, and fat can only be burned while that cycle runs.

## 2. Three phases, key steps only

Glycolysis means sugar splitting.
It is a chain of ten reactions, each run by its own enzyme.
All of them take place in the cytosol, the fluid that fills the cell outside the mitochondria.
Together they turn one glucose, with six carbons, into two molecules of pyruvate with three carbons each.
The ten steps are easiest to follow as three phases, called investment, split and payoff.
Think of the pathway as a small business that invests two A T P, earns four A T P and keeps a profit of two A T P.
The full detail of all ten steps is for looking up, not for learning by heart.
We'll focus on the key ones.
In the investment phase, the cell spends two A T P to prime the sugar.
Priming makes the sugar more reactive and ready to split.
In step one, the enzyme hexokinase moves a phosphate from A T P onto glucose, making glucose six-phosphate.
The phosphate gives the molecule an electric charge, and the cell membrane does not let charged molecules through.
As a result, glucose is now trapped inside the cell.
Step two only reshapes this molecule.
Step three is run by the enzyme phosphofructokinase, or P F K, which adds a second phosphate from another A T P.
This makes fructose one six-diphosphate.
At steps one and three, A T P is turned into A D P.
In the split phase, step four cuts this six carbon sugar into two molecules of three carbons each.
The two halves are not quite the same at first, and step five converts one into the other.
After step five, both are three-phosphoglyceraldehyde, and the pathway runs as two parallel tracks, one for each half.
From here on, every reaction happens twice for each glucose.
The payoff phase begins at step six, where each three carbon molecule is oxidised.
Oxidised means it loses hydrogen.
A pair of hydrogens is removed and handed to N A D plus, a carrier that picks up hydrogen and becomes N A D H.
In each half, N A D plus becomes N A D H, together with a hydrogen ion.
That N A D H is passed on to the electron transport chain.
Because step six happens twice, glycolysis makes two N A D H per glucose.
Steps seven and ten pass a phosphate straight from the intermediate molecule to A D P, which makes A T P.
This is called substrate level phosphorylation.
It means A T P made by passing a phosphate directly from a substrate to A D P.
A substrate is simply the molecule an enzyme works on.
No oxygen and no electron transport chain are involved.
Each of the two steps runs twice and makes two A T P, giving a payoff of four A T P per glucose.
The enzyme at step ten, the last step, is pyruvate kinase.
The pathway ends with two molecules of pyruvate.
Two other links are worth knowing.
Glycogen joins the pathway at glucose six-phosphate.
And after the last step, the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase links pyruvate and lactate in both directions, turning each into the other.

## 3. The balance sheet

Now we can tally up.
Glycolysis makes four A T P and spends two, so it leaves a net gain of two A T P per glucose.
Splitting A T P releases about seven point three kilocalories per mole.
Two times seven point three gives fourteen point six kilocalories per mole of glucose.
The other products are two N A D H and two pyruvate.
The whole pathway can be written in one line.
On one side are glucose, two A D P, two inorganic phosphates and two N A D plus.
On the other are two pyruvate, two A T P, two N A D H, two hydrogen ions and two water molecules.
It is a small return, only about five per cent of the energy in the glucose molecule.
It is about the same share, five per cent, of the A T P that complete breakdown would give.
Most of the energy is still locked inside the two pyruvates and the N A D H.
The value of glycolysis lies in its speed and in not needing oxygen, which is why it is called rapid glycolysis.
Glycogen, the stored form of glucose, can also feed glycolysis.
It is broken down to glucose six-phosphate, a process called glycogenolysis.
A glucose unit from glycogen enters already carrying a phosphate.
It skips the hexokinase step and saves one A T P.
Glycolysis from glycogen therefore nets three A T P instead of two.

## 4. Two fates of pyruvate

Strictly speaking, glycolysis ends at pyruvate, but pyruvate then goes one of two ways.
In anaerobic glycolysis, known as rapid glycolysis, pyruvate is converted to lactate.
The reason lies back at step six, which needs N A D plus to collect hydrogen.
Without enough oxygen, the mitochondria cannot take that hydrogen back off N A D H.
Instead, pyruvate takes it, becoming lactate and freeing N A D plus.
The freed N A D plus lets glycolysis keep running without oxygen.
Lactate is not wasted either.
Tissues such as the heart burn it for A T P.
A recent study in mice suggests that the heart can even carry lactate straight into its mitochondria.
A commentary on this by Thome and Arany, from twenty twenty-six, was shared by Doctor Shafat.
These findings are new and preliminary, and they are still debated.
In aerobic glycolysis, known as slow glycolysis, pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is converted to acetyl co A, a two carbon fuel molecule.
Acetyl co A then passes through the Krebs cycle and electron transport, which release the remaining energy.
The name slow glycolysis therefore covers the whole aerobic route after pyruvate.
The ten steps themselves are the same in both cases.
What decides between the two fates is whether the mitochondria can take up pyruvate, and the hydrogen on N A D H, as fast as glycolysis makes them.
When they keep up, pyruvate takes the aerobic route.
When glycolysis runs faster, as in an all-out sprint, the surplus is turned into lactate.

## 5. How glycolysis is regulated

Three factors set how fast glycolysis runs.
The first factor is the set of key enzymes.
They are hexokinase, phosphofructokinase and pyruvate kinase, which run steps one, three and ten.
These steps cannot run backwards, which makes them act like one-way valves and natural control points.
A fourth enzyme, phosphorylase, controls how much fuel enters by releasing glucose units from glycogen.
Phosphorylase is the enzyme missing in McArdle disease.
Of these, phosphofructokinase is the main rate-limiting enzyme, the slowest step that sets the pace of the whole pathway.
It senses the cell's energy state.
If muscle uses A T P fast, there is a build-up of A D P, A M P and inorganic phosphate.
Together, they switch phosphofructokinase on.
Plentiful A T P, on the other hand, slows the enzyme down.
The second factor is the level of fructose one six-diphosphate, the molecule that phosphofructokinase makes at step three.
As phosphofructokinase speeds up, this molecule builds up and switches on pyruvate kinase at step ten.
In this way, the end of the pathway speeds up to match the start, so the molecules in between do not pile up.
The third factor is oxygen, because oxygen in abundance inhibits glycolysis.
Mitochondria with plenty of oxygen make A T P in large amounts, and that high A T P level holds phosphofructokinase back.
The upshot is that glycolysis speeds up when energy demand outruns the oxygen supply, and slows down when the mitochondria can keep up.

## Summary

Let's pull it all together.
Complete breakdown of one mole of glucose releases six hundred and eighty-six kilocalories.
Glycolysis is its first stage, ten enzyme steps in the cytosol that need no oxygen.
Its first phosphate traps glucose in the cell.
After that, the cell invests two A T P at steps one and three, run by hexokinase and phosphofructokinase, to prime the sugar.
Step four splits the six carbon sugar into two halves of three carbons each.
At step six, each half is oxidised, giving two N A D H in all.
At steps seven and ten, substrate level phosphorylation passes phosphates directly to A D P, making four A T P.
The balance is a net two A T P, or fourteen point six kilocalories per mole, plus two pyruvate.
The net gain is about five per cent of glucose's energy.
The net gain rises to three A T P when the glucose comes from glycogen.
Without enough oxygen, pyruvate becomes lactate in rapid glycolysis.
With oxygen, it becomes acetyl co A and releases the rest of its energy through the Krebs cycle in slow glycolysis.
The pace is set by the key enzymes, above all the rate-limiting phosphofructokinase, which speeds up as A D P, A M P and inorganic phosphate rise.
Fructose one six-diphosphate and oxygen set the pace too, and oxygen slows it.
Glycolysis matters because it is the fastest way, and the only way without oxygen, to make A T P from fuel.
