## Hydrolysis, Condensation and Redox

Every pathway that releases energy from food is built from a few simple kinds of chemical reaction.
This note covers how molecules are split and joined using water.
It also covers how electrons pass between molecules, which is how the body takes energy out of food.

## 1. Hydrolysis: splitting with water

Two simple reactions explain how the body takes large molecules apart and builds new ones.
The first is hydrolysis, which means splitting with water.
Hydrolysis breaks a large molecule into smaller ones by adding water across a chemical bond.
The water is split too.
Its hydrogen attaches to one fragment as a hydrogen ion, and its hydroxyl group attaches to the other.
Hydrolysis is the basis of digestion.
Digestion degrades complex carbohydrates, lipids and proteins into simpler forms that the body can absorb and assimilate.
Take sucrose, which is table sugar.
Sucrose reacts with water to give glucose and fructose.
A second example is a dipeptide, which is two amino acids joined by a peptide bond.
That peptide bond is the link that holds amino acids together in a protein.
With water, a dipeptide splits into two separate amino acids.
Fat is broken down in the same way.
A T P also releases its energy by hydrolysis.

## 2. Condensation: joining and releasing water

Condensation is the reverse of hydrolysis, and it is how the body builds.
In condensation, simple components bind together to form a more complex molecule.
Each new bond releases one molecule of water.
That water is made from a hydrogen of one partner and a hydroxyl group of the other.
The examples mirror those of hydrolysis.
Two glucose molecules join to give maltose and water.
Two amino acids join to give a dipeptide and water, linked by a new peptide bond.
Condensation is the basis of synthesis, because the body builds glycogen, fats and proteins this way.
These two reactions therefore form a pair.
Hydrolysis takes molecules apart and belongs to catabolism, the breaking down of molecules.
Condensation builds them and belongs to anabolism, the building up of molecules.

## 3. Oxidation and reduction

The second kind of reaction moves electrons rather than water, and it is the one that releases energy from food.
Oxidation always involves a loss of electrons.
Reduction always involves a gain of electrons.
A simple memory aid is that oxidation is loss and reduction is gain.
Oxidation reactions can transfer oxygen, hydrogen atoms or electrons.
In the body, oxidation usually means removing hydrogen atoms.
Removing a hydrogen atom counts as oxidation, since the atom is one proton and one electron, and its electron goes with it.
Losing or gaining electrons changes an atom's valence, which is its charge number.
Electrons are negative, so losing electrons gives a net gain in valence, and gaining them gives a net decrease.
Iron shows how this works.
When iron two plus becomes iron three plus, it has lost one electron.
Its valence has risen from two to three, and it has been oxidised.
Oxidation and reduction always happen together, because an electron given up by one substance must be taken by another.
This is why they are called redox reactions.
Electrons are like a hot potato.
They are never dropped, only handed from one molecule to the next.
Every redox reaction therefore has two partners.
The reducing agent is the substance that donates electrons, and it is itself oxidised as a result.
The oxidising agent is the substance that gains electrons, and it is itself reduced in the process.

## 4. The hydrogen carriers

In metabolism, enzymes called dehydrogenases remove pairs of hydrogen atoms from fragments of food, which oxidises those fragments.
The hydrogens are not released loose.
Instead, they are handed to coenzymes.
Two coenzymes do this in every energy pathway.
N A D plus takes both electrons of the pair but only one proton, and the spare proton stays free as a hydrogen ion.
Written as a reaction, N A D plus takes the two hydrogen atoms removed from the food and gives N A D H and a hydrogen ion.
F A D accepts both hydrogen atoms and becomes F A D H two.
The loaded forms, N A D H and F A D H two, are reduced and rich in energy.
The empty forms, N A D plus and F A D, must be regenerated.
Without a free carrier, the reactions that need it stop.
The carriers are like delivery vans that load hydrogen in the energy pathways and must unload before they can load again.
They unload in the respiratory chain, the final common pathway of aerobic metabolism.
There, the electrons carried by hydrogen are passed along a chain of carrier molecules to oxygen, which accepts them and forms water.
Oxygen gains those electrons, which means oxygen itself is reduced.
In gaining them, oxygen pulls electrons away from the carriers, which is what an oxidising agent does.
This makes oxygen the body's final oxidising agent.

## 5. Why redox carries the energy

The electrons in food molecules hold a lot of potential energy, meaning stored energy.
As they are passed step by step toward oxygen, they fall to a lower energy level.
The energy released by that fall is what the cell captures.
So redox reactions are the basis of all the body's energy transfer.
Energy leaves food as electrons riding on hydrogen atoms.

## Summary

Hydrolysis splits a molecule by adding water, as when sucrose gives glucose and fructose.
It is the basis of digestion and catabolism.
Condensation joins molecules and releases water, as when two glucose molecules give maltose.
It is the basis of synthesis and anabolism.
Oxidation is loss of electrons and reduction is gain of electrons, and the two always occur together as redox reactions.
The reducing agent donates electrons and is oxidised, while the oxidising agent gains electrons and is reduced.
Dehydrogenases hand hydrogen from food to the carriers N A D plus and F A D.
These become N A D H and F A D H two, and they must unload in the respiratory chain to be regenerated.
In that chain, oxygen is the final oxidising agent and forms water.
The electrons falling toward oxygen release the energy the cell captures.
