## Krebs Cycle

Splitting glucose in two releases only a small part of the energy it holds.
In this note, we'll follow the leftover pieces into the cell's power stations, where a repeating cycle takes them apart completely.
The cycle makes little energy itself.
Yet it collects the hydrogen that later pays for most of the cell's energy.

## 1. Why a second stage is needed

Glycolysis splits one glucose, with six carbons, into two pyruvate molecules of three carbons each.
This happens in the cell's fluid, the cytoplasm.
When glycolysis runs fast, it releases only about five per cent of the energy in glucose.
The rest is still locked in the two pyruvates.
To release it, the cell takes them apart inside the mitochondria.
These are the compartments of the cell that use oxygen to make A T P.
Each pyruvate is first converted to a two-carbon molecule called acetyl co A.
Acetyl co A then enters a circular series of reactions, the Krebs cycle.
It is also called the citric acid cycle, or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, T C A for short.
It is named after Hans Krebs, who worked out its steps.
The cycle is the second stage of carbohydrate breakdown, and it produces carbon dioxide and hydrogen atoms.
Because this route needs oxygen, it is also called slow glycolysis.
Keep one idea in mind throughout.
The cycle makes very little A T P itself.
Its job is to release the carbon as carbon dioxide and load the hydrogen onto carrier molecules for the next stage.

## 2. The link step

The conversion begins once glycolysis is over and pyruvate has moved into the matrix, the inner space of the mitochondrion.
There, the enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase trims each three-carbon pyruvate down to two carbons.
It does three things at once.
First, it snips off the end carbon of pyruvate.
That end carbon already carries two oxygen atoms, so it simply breaks away as a molecule of carbon dioxide.
This is the first carbon dioxide released in glucose breakdown.
Second, it takes a pair of hydrogen atoms and loads them onto N A D plus, the carrier that becomes N A D H.
Finally, it hands the remaining two-carbon piece, an acetyl group, to coenzyme A, or co A.
The whole step can be summed up in a single line.
Pyruvate, N A D plus and co A give acetyl co A, carbon dioxide, N A D H and a hydrogen ion.
Of the pair of hydrogen atoms removed, N A D plus takes both electrons but only one proton.
A proton is the nucleus of a hydrogen atom.
The second proton is left in the surrounding fluid as a free hydrogen ion.
Because one glucose gives two pyruvates, the step runs twice.
Together, the two runs give two acetyl co A, two molecules of carbon dioxide, two N A D H and two hydrogen ions.
Since the step cannot run backwards, acetyl co A can never become pyruvate again.
This limits which fuels the body can turn into glucose.

## 3. One turn of the cycle

The easiest way to follow one turn is to count carbons.
The enzyme citrate synthase joins the two-carbon acetyl group to a molecule waiting in the matrix, oxaloacetate, which has four carbons.
The result is citrate, with six carbons, and coenzyme A is set free to collect another acetyl group.
Citrate is rearranged into isocitrate, which has the same atoms in a different layout.
Two carbons are now removed.
Isocitrate loses one as carbon dioxide and becomes alpha ketoglutarate, with five carbons.
Alpha ketoglutarate loses a second as carbon dioxide and becomes succinyl co A, with four carbons.
Each of these two steps also loads one N A D plus.
The rest of the turn rebuilds oxaloacetate.
Succinyl co A becomes succinate, and the energy released makes one G T P, guanosine triphosphate.
G T P is a close relative of A T P that passes its end phosphate to A D P.
In this way the cycle makes one A T P per turn directly, by substrate level phosphorylation.
That term means a phosphate is moved straight from one molecule onto A D P.
Succinate becomes fumarate and loads a second carrier, F A D, which becomes F A D H two.
Fumarate takes up water to become malate.
Last of all, malate becomes oxaloacetate and loads the third N A D plus.
At the end of the turn, the cell is back where it started, with one molecule of oxaloacetate.
Oxaloacetate has not been used up.
The turn took in two carbons from acetyl co A and gave out two carbons as carbon dioxide.
Oxaloacetate is left with the same four carbons it began with.
It is ready to pick up the next acetyl co A and go round again.
This is why the pathway is called a cycle.
Its last product is also its starting point, so the same oxaloacetate is used over and over.
A roundabout gives the picture, with oxaloacetate as a car that keeps circling.
At the entrance, it picks up an acetyl group as a passenger.
On the way round, the two carbons of the passenger get off as carbon dioxide.
Hydrogen is unloaded onto carriers at four places.
The car arrives back at the entrance empty, ready for the next passenger.

## 4. The tally

One turn of the cycle means one acetyl co A going round it once.
Adding up the reactions, each turn releases two molecules of carbon dioxide, loads three N A D H and one F A D H two, and makes one A T P.
Each pyruvate gives one acetyl co A.
The two pyruvates from one glucose therefore give two acetyl co A, and the cycle turns twice for every glucose.
Now add the link step to these two turns.
The link step, for two pyruvates, gives two molecules of carbon dioxide and two N A D H, with no F A D H two and no A T P made directly.
From the two turns of the Krebs cycle come four molecules of carbon dioxide, six N A D H, two F A D H two and two A T P made directly.
Altogether, one glucose gives six molecules of carbon dioxide, eight N A D H, two F A D H two and two A T P made directly.
The same result can be counted in hydrogen atoms, since each loaded carrier stands for one pair.
The two pyruvates give up four hydrogen atoms as they become acetyl co A.
Each turn loads four carriers, three N A D H and one F A D H two, and each carrier takes a pair of hydrogen atoms.
One turn therefore gives up eight hydrogen atoms.
The two turns give up sixteen.
Adding the four from the link step gives twenty hydrogen atoms, or ten pairs, carried by eight N A D H and two F A D H two.
The six molecules of carbon dioxide account for all six carbons of the original glucose.
Nothing of its skeleton is left.

## 5. What the cycle is for

Two A T P per glucose is a small direct return.
The main function of the cycle lies elsewhere.
It produces reduced coenzymes, the loaded carriers N A D H and F A D H two.
They take their hydrogen to the electron transport chain, a series of proteins in the inner membrane of the mitochondrion.
There they are oxidised, meaning their hydrogen is removed.
The energy released makes most of the cell's A T P.
The work of the mitochondrion can be split into two phases.
In phase one, pyruvate from glycolysis joins coenzyme A to become acetyl co A, which feeds the citric acid cycle.
As the cycle turns, it gives off carbon dioxide, one A T P and many hydrogen ions.
In phase two, the reduced coenzymes pass that hydrogen to the electron transport chain.
Pairs of electrons step down three levels, and each step makes A T P from A D P and inorganic phosphate.
At the end, two hydrogen ions and half a molecule of oxygen form water.
The cycle has two wider roles.
It is the common final route for acetyl co A from fat and protein as well as carbohydrate.
Its intermediates can also be drawn off to build other molecules, such as some amino acids.

## 6. Why it needs oxygen

Although the cycle belongs to the oxygen-using stage of metabolism, no step of it uses oxygen.
The oxygen in its carbon dioxide comes from the intermediates themselves and from water, not from the oxygen we breathe.
Yet the cycle runs only when oxygen is present.
The reason is the carriers.
The cell has only a small stock of N A D plus and F A D, and each turn needs four empty ones.
They are emptied only when N A D H and F A D H two unload onto the electron transport chain, which ends at oxygen.
Without oxygen, the chain stops, the carriers stay loaded, and the cycle stops within moments.
Glycolysis has an escape route.
In the cytoplasm, N A D H can unload onto pyruvate to form lactate.
The mitochondrion has no equivalent reaction.

## Summary

Let's pull it together.
Fast glycolysis releases only about five per cent of the energy in glucose.
For that reason, its two pyruvates are broken down further inside the mitochondria.
In the matrix, pyruvate dehydrogenase turns each pyruvate into acetyl co A.
The step releases one molecule of carbon dioxide and loads one N A D H, and it cannot be reversed.
Acetyl co A, with two carbons, joins oxaloacetate, with four, to form citrate, with six.
Citrate loses two carbons as carbon dioxide to become a four-carbon molecule again.
The rest of the turn rebuilds oxaloacetate, making the pathway a cycle that accepts acetyl groups from carbohydrate, fat and protein.
Each turn yields two molecules of carbon dioxide, three N A D H, one F A D H two and one A T P.
The A T P is made by way of G T P.
One glucose turns the cycle twice.
With the link step, one glucose gives six molecules of carbon dioxide, eight N A D H, two F A D H two and two A T P.
Together, these account for twenty hydrogen atoms and all six carbons.
The real purpose of the cycle is to load N A D H and F A D H two.
Their hydrogen drives most A T P production in the electron transport chain.
No step uses oxygen, but the cycle runs only with oxygen.
Only the chain that ends at oxygen frees N A D plus and F A D for the next turn.
