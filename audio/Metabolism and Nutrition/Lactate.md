## Lactate

Hard-working muscle can break down sugar faster than its mitochondria can finish the job with oxygen.
In this note, we'll follow what the cell does with the leftover hydrogen.
We'll then see why the result, lactate, is a useful fuel rather than a waste.

## 1. Glycolysis needs its NAD+ back

Let's start with the problem glycolysis faces.
Glycolysis is the chain of reactions in the cytosol that splits one glucose into two pyruvate.
At step six, it strips hydrogen from the sugar.
A carrier molecule called N A D plus must accept that hydrogen, and it becomes N A D H.
Without a free N A D plus, step six cannot happen.
The difficulty is that a cell holds only a small amount of N A D plus.
Each glucose turns two N A D plus into two N A D H.
The supply soon runs out if nothing empties the N A D H again.
Glycolysis would stop, however much glucose was available.
The cell has two ways to unload N A D H.
With enough oxygen, and mitochondria that can keep up, N A D H hands its hydrogen to the respiratory chain in the mitochondria.
When that route cannot keep up, N A D H hands its hydrogen to pyruvate instead.
This second route is anaerobic or rapid glycolysis, meaning glycolysis that does not use oxygen.
It ends with pyruvate becoming lactate.

## 2. Pyruvate takes the hydrogen

The transfer is carried out by an enzyme called lactate dehydrogenase, or L D H.
It moves two hydrogens onto pyruvate, taken from N A D H together with a hydrogen ion, written H plus.
Pyruvate becomes lactate, and N A D H becomes N A D plus again.
As a reaction, pyruvate, N A D H and a hydrogen ion turn into lactate and N A D plus.
The reaction can run both ways, which matters for what happens to lactate later.
For each glucose, two pyruvate become two lactate, and two N A D plus return to step six.
Glycolysis can therefore keep running at full speed.
It keeps giving its net two A T P per glucose with no oxygen at all.
Overall, glucose plus two A D P plus two inorganic phosphate gives two lactate, two A T P and two water molecules.
Put simply, lactate is a temporary parking place for hydrogen.
The whole loop can be followed from the beginning.
Glucose passes through glycolysis, and A D P becomes A T P.
Alongside, two N A D plus pick up hydrogen and become two N A D H and two hydrogen ions.
At the end, lactate dehydrogenase adds that hydrogen to two pyruvate, giving two lactate.
The two N A D plus are regenerated and carried back to the start for the next glucose.
That regeneration loop is the whole point of the reaction, and lactate is simply where the hydrogen ends up.

## 3. When lactate accumulates

Some lactate is made all the time, even at rest.
Red blood cells, which carry oxygen in the blood, have no mitochondria.
For them, glycolysis ending in lactate is the only source of A T P.
In light and moderate exercise, lactate is removed as fast as it is made.
Its level in the blood therefore stays low.
Intense exercise is different.
Glycolysis makes pyruvate and N A D H faster than the mitochondria can take them in.
More muscle fibres that are rich in glycolytic enzymes are also brought into use.
As a result, pyruvate binds hydrogen to form lactate, and lactate builds up in muscle and blood.
The intensity at which blood lactate begins to climb is called the lactate threshold.
Lactate forms whenever glycolysis outruns the mitochondria.
That is not the same as a total lack of oxygen, because working muscle can make lactate while still receiving oxygen.

## 4. Lactic acid or lactate

As lactate builds up, it is often called lactic acid.
The two names, though, describe slightly different things.
An acid is a molecule that can give up a hydrogen ion.
Lactic acid forms during anaerobic glycolysis.
Inside the body, lactic acid gives up its hydrogen ion almost the instant it forms.
This splitting is called dissociation.
What remains after the split carries a negative charge.
This charged remainder pairs with a positively charged ion, such as sodium or potassium.
An acid's remainder paired with a positive ion in this way is called a salt.
The salt of lactic acid is lactate.
That is why the molecule actually found in muscle and blood is lactate, not lactic acid.
The same split explains where the acidity comes from.
Lactate itself is not acid.
It is the hydrogen ions set free that make working muscle and blood more acid.
The body's buffers, chemicals that soak up hydrogen ions, keep this change in check.

## 5. Where lactate goes

Lactate is not a dead end, because the L D H reaction can run backwards.
Lactate leaves the working muscle cell and enters the blood.
From there, it has two main fates.
Part is taken up by the heart, by less active muscle fibres and by other tissues.
These tissues turn it back into pyruvate with L D H and burn it in their mitochondria through the Krebs cycle.
The other part is taken up by the liver, which rebuilds it into glucose.
The liver stores this glucose as glycogen, or releases it into the blood for the muscles to use again.
Building new glucose from smaller molecules such as lactate is called gluconeogenesis.
The round trip, from glucose to lactate in muscle and from lactate back to glucose in the liver, is the Cori cycle.
Tracing the cycle once makes it clear.
An active muscle cell turns glucose into pyruvate, and pyruvate into lactate.
The lactate is carried in a vein to the liver.
In the liver, it becomes pyruvate, then glucose, and perhaps glycogen.
The glucose leaves in an artery and returns to the muscle.
It works like a recycling service that turns spent fuel back into fresh glucose.

## 6. Lactate as a fuel

For most of the last two centuries, lactate was seen as a waste.
It was first found in sour milk in seventeen eighty.
It was later thought to form when oxygen runs short, and to be a possible cause of fatigue.
The modern picture is different.
Even at rest, blood plasma holds about one millimolar of lactate, which is one thousandth of a mole per litre.
Lactate is turned over faster than any other abundant fuel molecule.
Tissues all over the body constantly release it, and others constantly take it up.
It is either turned back into sugar in the liver and kidneys, or fully oxidised to carbon dioxide and water in mitochondria.
During heavy exercise, lactate can become the heart's main fuel, ahead of fatty acids.
Recent work in heart muscle has also changed the view of how lactate reaches the mitochondria.
The usual route has four steps.
A newly proposed shortcut lets lactate enter the mitochondrion before it is turned into pyruvate, as described by Thome and Arany in twenty twenty-six.
In a heart muscle cell, those four steps run like this.
Lactate enters the cell through a transporter protein called M C T one, short for monocarboxylate transporter one.
In the cytosol, L D H turns it into pyruvate, while N A D plus becomes N A D H.
Pyruvate, which glycolysis also makes, crosses the inner mitochondrial membrane through the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier, or M P C.
In the matrix, which is the mitochondrion's inner space, the enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase, or P D H, turns it into acetyl co A.
Acetyl co A feeds the Krebs cycle, also known as the T C A cycle.
The N A D H formed drives oxidative phosphorylation to make A T P.
In the shortcut, lactate passes through a second M C T one, this one in the inner mitochondrial membrane.
It becomes pyruvate inside the matrix.
This shortcut may matter when the heart is under stress, and it is still debated.

## Summary

Let's pull it all together.
Glycolysis needs a free N A D plus at step six to accept hydrogen.
A cell holds so little that glycolysis would stop if its N A D H were never unloaded.
When the mitochondria cannot take that hydrogen fast enough, as in intense exercise, lactate dehydrogenase passes it to pyruvate, which becomes lactate.
That returns two N A D plus per glucose, so glycolysis keeps making A T P without oxygen.
Overall, glucose plus two A D P plus two inorganic phosphate gives two lactate, two A T P and two water molecules.
Lactate builds up whenever glycolysis outruns the mitochondria.
The intensity at which blood lactate starts to climb is the lactate threshold.
Lactic acid at once releases a hydrogen ion, so the molecule in the body is its salt, lactate.
The hydrogen ions are what make muscle acid.
Because L D H works both ways, lactate is burned by the heart and less active muscle, or rebuilt into glucose by the liver through gluconeogenesis.
That loop is called the Cori cycle.
Far from being a waste, lactate circulates at about one millimolar even at rest, and can become the heart's main fuel in heavy exercise.
Lactate enters cells through M C T one and is turned into pyruvate by L D H.
Pyruvate enters mitochondria through the M P C and is turned into acetyl co A by P D H.
In the heart, lactate may also slip into mitochondria directly through M C T one.
