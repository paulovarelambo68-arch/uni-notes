## Oxidative Phosphorylation

Almost all the energy in food is finally turned into A T P on one membrane deep inside the mitochondria.
In this note, we'll follow the hydrogen collected by earlier pathways as its electrons fall towards oxygen and their energy is captured.
We'll also see why the body needs oxygen at all.

## 1. Two names for one process

Food releases its energy mainly through redox reactions, in which electrons pass from one molecule to another.
Earlier pathways have stripped hydrogen from the fuel and loaded it onto two carriers, N A D H and F A D H two.
Most of it comes from the Krebs cycle, which is also called the citric acid cycle or the T C A cycle.
In cellular oxidation, the mitochondria take the electrons from this hydrogen and pass them along until they reach oxygen.
A T P is made during these redox reactions.
The process has two halves that always run together.
The first is electron transport, also called the respiratory chain.
It means the passing of electrons along specific carrier molecules to oxygen.
The second is oxidative phosphorylation, the making of A T P from A D P with the energy that electron transport releases.
Together they form the final common pathway of aerobic metabolism.
This means that hydrogen taken from carbohydrate, fat and protein all ends up here.
It is also the largest source of energy, because more than ninety per cent of the body's A T P is made this way.
The earlier pathways make little A T P themselves, because their main job is to load the carriers that this pathway cashes in.

## 2. The setting: the inner membrane

Since all of this takes place inside the mitochondrion, its layout comes first.
A mitochondrion is wrapped in two membranes, a smooth outer one and a folded inner one.
The narrow gap between them is the intermembrane space.
The space inside the inner membrane is the matrix, where the Krebs cycle runs.
The carriers of the electron transport chain are built into the inner membrane, and so is the enzyme that makes A T P.
Hydrogen ions cannot cross this membrane by themselves.
This matters because the chain will push hydrogen ions to one side of the membrane.
If they could slip straight back, their energy would simply be lost as heat.
Instead, they stay crowded on one side, and their only way back is through the enzyme that makes A T P.
In this way the membrane stores potential energy, the energy held by position.
It works like the wall of a dam, which holds water back so that it can turn a turbine only by passing through the gate.

## 3. Electrons step down to oxygen

The chain begins when N A D H and F A D H two reach the inner membrane and give up their hydrogen.
Each hydrogen atom splits into an electron and a hydrogen ion, which is a proton, also written H plus.
The hydrogen ions stay in the matrix and the electrons enter the chain.
N A D H hands its electrons over at the top, and F A D H two hands them over one step lower.
The electrons then pass from one carrier to the next, like a bucket along a line of people.
Many carriers are cytochromes, proteins that contain iron.
This iron is held in the same kind of structure as the iron of haemoglobin, the oxygen carrier of red blood cells.
Each iron atom is reduced when it accepts an electron, and oxidised again when it passes that electron on.
Each carrier pulls on electrons a little more strongly than the one before.
As a result, the electrons move one way only, losing a little energy at each step.
This is important because energy can be captured only in manageable packets.
A river that comes down a hillside over several small waterfalls can turn a turbine at each one.
A single great fall would release the same energy at once and waste most of it.
At the end of the chain, oxygen is the final electron acceptor.
Each pair of electrons that reaches the end is taken up by one oxygen atom, which is therefore reduced.
Electrons are negatively charged, which leaves the oxygen carrying two extra negative charges.
These attract two of the positive hydrogen ions left in the matrix, and together they form water.
In symbols, two H plus and two electrons combine with half O two to give water.
Oxygen is written as half O two, meaning half an oxygen molecule, because the oxygen we breathe comes as molecules of two atoms, and each pair of electrons needs only one atom.
The emptied carriers, N A D plus and F A D, return to glycolysis and the Krebs cycle to collect more hydrogen.
Picture the chain as a staircase of energy, with higher potential energy at the top and lower potential energy at the bottom.
In industry, water falls down three steps and turns a turbine at each.
In the body, hydrogen arrives on N A D H plus H plus at the top, and on F A D H two one step lower.
N A D plus and F A D are left behind.
Pairs of electrons then step down a staircase of cytochromes.
A T P is released at three levels, just as power is released at each turbine.
At the bottom, the electrons combine with two H plus and half O two to form water.

## 4. Pumping hydrogen ions

The energy of the falling electrons does not make A T P directly.
Instead, at three points along the chain, it is used to pump hydrogen ions out of the matrix into the intermembrane space.
This is uphill work, because the ions are pushed towards the side where they are already crowded.
As a result, hydrogen ions pile up in the intermembrane space, where their concentration is high and the p H is low, meaning the space is more acidic.
In the matrix they become scarce, leaving the p H there high.
Because each ion carries a positive charge, the pumping also leaves the outer side of the inner membrane positive compared with the matrix side.
This electrical difference is the membrane potential.
The concentration difference and the membrane potential together make up the proton gradient, a store of potential energy.
The ions tend to flow back into the matrix, but the membrane holds them.
The gradient is like water held behind a dam, ready to do work once a gate opens.

## 5. ATP synthase, the turbine

The only way back into the matrix is through A T P synthase, an enzyme that forms a channel through the inner membrane.
As hydrogen ions flow down their gradient through it, the enzyme uses the energy of the flow to join A D P and inorganic phosphate into A T P.
Inorganic phosphate is written P I.
A T P synthase is the turbine at the foot of the dam.
Making A T P by a flow of ions across a membrane is called chemiosmosis.
This explains the word coupling, which means that one process cannot run without the other.
The energy releasing reactions of electron transport build the proton gradient.
The stored energy of that gradient, plus the membrane potential, couples electron transport to A T P synthesis.
In other words, the chain and A T P synthase are two separate machines, and the gradient is the only thing connecting them.
If the chain stops pumping, the gradient runs down and A T P synthase stops too.
The whole process runs in four stages, which are pump, store, flow back and make A T P.

## 6. How much ATP each carrier gives

Each loaded carrier is worth a certain amount of A T P, and two counts are in use.
The classic count gives three A T P for each N A D H.
In symbols, N A D H and H plus react with three A D P, three P I and half a molecule of oxygen.
These give N A D plus, water and three A T P.
Put simply, oxidising one N A D H with one oxygen atom releases enough energy to make three A T P.
The same count gives two A T P for each F A D H two.
Careful measurement gives lower averages, about two point five A T P for each N A D H and about one point five for each F A D H two.
They are lower because some of the gradient's hydrogen ions are used for other tasks in the mitochondrion instead of passing through A T P synthase.
These values lie behind the modern total of thirty-two A T P per glucose.
F A D H two yields less because it joins the chain below the first pump.
Its electrons drive only two of the three pumping points, so fewer hydrogen ions are pumped.
Overall, only about a third of the energy released, thirty-four per cent, is captured in A T P, and the rest becomes heat.

## 7. The role of oxygen

Everything in this chain depends on the oxygen waiting at its end.
Oxygen is the major oxidising agent in the tissues, meaning the main acceptor of electrons.
It also ensures that energy transfer reactions proceed at an appropriate rate.
The reason is that the chain runs only as fast as oxygen clears electrons from its end.
The oxygen level in the mitochondria therefore drives the chain.
This gives aerobic metabolism its precise meaning.
It refers to the energy generating catabolic reactions, those that break fuel down, in which oxygen is the final electron acceptor of the respiratory chain.
There, oxygen combines with hydrogen to form water.
When oxygen is missing, the electrons have nowhere to go.
The chain backs up like a blocked conveyor belt.
N A D H and F A D H two stay loaded, so the Krebs cycle stops for lack of N A D plus and F A D.
Only glycolysis can carry on, by turning its end product, pyruvate, into lactate, which frees N A D plus.
That yields just two A T P per glucose.
Nearly all the oxygen a person breathes in is consumed at the end of this chain.
That is why oxygen uptake measured at the mouth reports how hard the mitochondria are working.
The water of metabolism, made by the body's own chemistry, also forms here.
The carbon dioxide we breathe out, by contrast, comes from the Krebs cycle.

## Summary

Glycolysis and the Krebs cycle strip hydrogen from fuel and load it onto N A D H and F A D H two, which carry it to the inner mitochondrial membrane.
There, each hydrogen splits into a hydrogen ion and an electron.
The electrons fall in small steps down a chain of iron containing cytochromes.
At three points, the energy of this fall pumps hydrogen ions from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
This builds a proton gradient, a store of energy like water behind a dam.
The ions flow back only through A T P synthase, which uses the flow to join A D P and inorganic phosphate into A T P, a process called chemiosmosis.
At the end of the chain, oxygen is the final electron acceptor and forms water.
This frees N A D plus and F A D to collect more hydrogen.
Each N A D H yields about two point five A T P, and each F A D H two about one point five.
This pathway makes more than ninety per cent of the body's A T P.
Without oxygen, the chain backs up, the Krebs cycle halts, and only glycolysis ending in lactate remains.
