## Protein and the Metabolic Mill

Muscles mostly burn carbohydrate and fat, but protein can be burned too.
In this note, we'll see how protein is prepared as a fuel, and how all three fuels meet at one central crossroads inside the cell.
We'll also look at which fuels can be turned into which, and why fat burns fully only when some carbohydrate is present.

## 1. Protein as a fuel

Protein is mainly a building material for muscle fibres, enzymes and other structures.
Its amino acids, however, can also be burned.
An amino acid is a carbon skeleton carrying an amino group, a small cluster of atoms that contains nitrogen.
The skeleton holds the usable energy, but the nitrogen cannot be burned.
The nitrogen has to be removed first, in a step called deamination.
The body has two ways to do it.
In oxidative deamination, the amino group is pulled off as ammonia, N H three, and a keto acid is left behind.
A keto acid is simply the bare carbon skeleton, an amino acid without its nitrogen.
In transamination, the amino group is instead handed to a different keto acid, which thereby becomes a new amino acid.
Ammonia is toxic and must not be allowed to build up.
The blood carries it to the liver, which combines it with carbon dioxide to make urea, a far less harmful molecule.
The kidneys excrete the urea in the urine.
Once the nitrogen is gone, the skeleton can be burned exactly as if it had come from glucose.
Most skeletons already are, or can easily be reshaped into, one of the molecules that glucose passes through as it is broken down.
These molecules belong to glycolysis and to the Krebs cycle, which is also called the citric acid cycle or the T C A cycle.
From that point, the same enzymes strip off the skeleton's hydrogen.
Its carbon leaves as carbon dioxide, and the hydrogen is used with oxygen to make A T P.

## 2. Glucogenic and ketogenic amino acids

Each of the twenty amino acids has its own shape, so each skeleton enters these pathways at its own point.
That entry point decides what the skeleton can become.
Only some points lead back towards glucose.
Let's take the two groups in turn.
Glucogenic amino acids, when deaminated, yield pyruvate or intermediates of the Krebs cycle.
These intermediates are the molecules made partway round the cycle.
For example, alanine becomes pyruvate, and glutamate enters the Krebs cycle directly.
Both are points from which the liver can run the pathway backwards and build new glucose.
This process is called gluconeogenesis.
The word glucogenic comes from this, because it means glucose forming.
Ketogenic amino acids, when deaminated, yield acetyl co A or acetoacetate.
Acetyl co A is a two carbon fragment carried by a helper molecule called coenzyme A.
It is the form in which most fuel enters the Krebs cycle.
Acetoacetate is a small fuel molecule built from two acetyl co A units.
These products cannot become glucose.
They can, however, be burned or built into triacylglycerol, also called triglyceride, the storage form of fat.

## 3. The metabolic mill

All three fuels end up at the same place.
The Krebs cycle is the vital link between the energy in the macronutrients and the chemical energy of A T P.
Carbohydrate arrives through glycolysis as pyruvate, which is turned into acetyl co A.
Fat arrives mostly as acetyl co A, made by beta oxidation, which chops fatty acids into two carbon pieces.
Fat's small glycerol part joins glycolysis and arrives through pyruvate.
Protein arrives after deamination, at pyruvate, at acetyl co A or straight into the cycle.
The cycle also works in reverse.
Its intermediates can leave the mitochondrion, cross into the cytosol and be used to build new molecules for maintenance and growth.
So the Krebs cycle is also a metabolic hub, like a station where traffic both arrives and departs.
The Krebs cycle is known as the metabolic mill.
Like a mill that grinds whatever grain is brought to it, the cycle burns whichever fuel arrives.
Protein's nitrogen is removed as ammonia, made into urea, and finally leaves in the urine.
Its carbon skeletons join the main route at different points.
Alanine joins at pyruvate, glycine joins further along the route, and glutamate joins at the cycle itself.
Acetyl co A joins oxaloacetate to make citrate, the step that opens every turn of the cycle.
Acetyl co A has a second route too, leading to ketone bodies.
These are small fuel molecules the liver makes when acetyl co A arrives faster than the cycle can use it.
Most of the steps in the mill run both ways.
This is the hub sending traffic out as well as taking it in.
The one exception is the step from pyruvate to acetyl co A, which runs one way only.
Traffic can pass that step downwards but never back up.
There is also two way traffic between each fuel store and its building blocks.
Protein exchanges with amino acids, glycogen with glucose, and fat with glycerol and fatty acids.
After the Krebs cycle, hydrogen loaded onto carriers goes on to oxidative phosphorylation, the final stage, which takes place in the mitochondria.
There, oxygen is used, water is formed and most of the A T P is made.
Every pathway covered in this series of notes fits within this one overall scheme.

## 4. What can become what

Because the routes meet and many steps run both ways, one fuel can often be rebuilt into another.
Three predominant interconversions stand out.
First, carbohydrates can be converted to fats, a process called lipogenesis, or to nonessential amino acids.
Second, fats can be converted to nonessential amino acids.
Third, proteins can be converted to carbohydrates or fats.
Each has a simple route.
Surplus glucose becomes pyruvate, then acetyl co A, and acetyl co A units are joined two carbons at a time into fatty acids.
That is why excess carbohydrate is stored as fat.
Carbohydrate and fat can both supply keto acids.
A keto acid becomes an amino acid when it receives an amino group by transamination.
What a protein can become depends on its amino acids.
Glucogenic ones can give glucose, and ketogenic ones can give fat.
Only some amino acids can be made in this way.
The body can make eleven of the twenty amino acids itself, and these are called nonessential.
The other nine are essential.
The body cannot make their keto acids, which means they must come from food.

## 5. The one way door

One step breaks the pattern of two way traffic.
The step from pyruvate to acetyl co A cannot be reversed.
Pyruvate loses a carbon as carbon dioxide at this step, and no enzyme can undo it.
This single fact decides what fat can become.
A fatty acid yields nothing but acetyl co A.
To make glucose, the liver must start from either pyruvate or oxaloacetate, the four carbon molecule of the Krebs cycle.
Acetyl co A can never go back to pyruvate, which closes the route through pyruvate.
It can enter the cycle, but its two carbons are lost as carbon dioxide during the same turn.
The cycle therefore ends each turn with exactly the oxaloacetate it started with.
None is left over to make glucose.
Only the small glycerol part of a fat can become glucose, since glycerol joins glycolysis above the one way step.
In short, glucose can be turned into fat, but fat cannot be turned back into glucose.

## 6. Fats burn in a carbohydrate flame

Fatty acids also depend on carbohydrate in a second way.
They need it to keep burning.
Acetyl co A can enter the Krebs cycle only by joining oxaloacetate.
Oxaloacetate is remade at the end of each turn, but some is steadily lost as intermediates leave the cycle.
Its stock is topped up mainly from pyruvate, in other words from carbohydrate.
The enzyme responsible is pyruvate carboxylase.
It is a ligase, an enzyme that joins two molecules using energy from A T P.
When carbohydrate runs short, oxaloacetate runs low.
This can happen in glycogen depletion, in starvation or on a diet very low in carbohydrate.
As a result, the cycle slows, and the acetyl co A arriving from fat cannot all be burned.
The liver turns the surplus into ketone bodies, small fuel molecules such as acetoacetate that other tissues can use.
Fat needs a steady trickle of carbohydrate to burn completely.
That is the meaning of the saying, fats burn in a carbohydrate flame.
Two consequences for performance follow.
Fat alone generates about one half of the power achieved with carbohydrate as the main aerobic fuel.
Depleting muscle glycogen therefore considerably lowers a muscle's maximum aerobic power, because the muscle must then rely mainly on fat.
The same principle reappears in McArdle disease.
This is an inherited condition in which muscle cannot use its own glycogen, and as a consequence also burns fat poorly.

## 7. Alcohol and the full map

Alcohol also carries energy, about seven kilocalories per gram.
Yet it is not grouped with the macronutrients.
The reason is that the body neither needs it nor can store it.
The liver oxidises it in two steps to acetate.
Each step loads N A D plus with hydrogen to form N A D H.
The acetate becomes acetyl co A, the same point at which a fatty acid enters the mill.
Alcohol can be burned or turned into fat, but never into glucose.
The real map of human metabolism is far larger than the pathways in these notes.
It holds thousands of reactions, with the Krebs cycle near its centre.
The pathways in this series are a deliberately simple sketch of it.

## Summary

Protein is mainly a building material, but its amino acids can be burned once deamination removes their nitrogen.
The nitrogen leaves either as ammonia or by being handed to another keto acid.
The liver turns the toxic ammonia into urea, which leaves in the urine.
Glucogenic skeletons yield pyruvate or Krebs cycle intermediates and can make glucose.
Ketogenic ones yield acetyl co A or acetoacetate and cannot.
All three fuels meet at the Krebs cycle.
It is the metabolic mill, turning their energy into A T P, and also a hub that sends intermediates out to build new molecules.
Carbohydrate can become fat or nonessential amino acids, and fat can become nonessential amino acids.
Protein can become carbohydrate or fat.
Since the step from pyruvate to acetyl co A is one way, fatty acids can never become glucose, and only glycerol can.
Acetyl co A must also join oxaloacetate, which is kept up mainly from carbohydrate.
That is why fats burn in a carbohydrate flame.
When glycogen runs out, fat burns incompletely, ketone bodies form and maximum aerobic power falls.
