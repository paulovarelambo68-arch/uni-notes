## Thermoregulation

Every working cell makes heat, and working muscle makes a great deal of it.
In this note, we'll see where body heat comes from and how it leaves the body.
And we'll see how the brain keeps the deep body at a steady temperature by balancing heat made against heat lost.

## 1. Core, shell and the safe range

It helps to picture the body as two layers.
The core is the deep tissues of the head and trunk, and it is held close to thirty-seven degrees Celsius.
The shell is the skin and the tissues just under it, and its temperature is allowed to vary.
In the cold, the core shrinks back to the head and trunk.
The limbs then join a thick, cool shell at about twenty-eight to thirty-four degrees Celsius, which insulates the core like a coat.
In warmth, the core extends into the limbs and the shell is thin.
Core temperature normally sits at about thirty-six to thirty-eight degrees Celsius.
Strenuous exercise takes it to about thirty-eight to forty-one degrees, and regulation still works there.
Above about forty-one degrees lies heat stroke, and below about thirty-five degrees lies hypothermia.
In both zones, temperature regulation is impaired.
The limits of survival are about thirty degrees and forty-five degrees Celsius.

## 2. Where the heat comes from

Muscle turns fuel into movement in two steps, and each step loses energy as heat.
In the first step, the cell burns fuel with oxygen to make A T P, the molecule that carries energy to where it is needed.
Only forty-two per cent of the fuel's energy is stored in A T P, and the other fifty-eight per cent escapes as heat straight away.
Counting A T P more conservatively gives thirty-four per cent instead of forty-two.
In the second step, the muscle spends the A T P to contract.
Less than twenty-five per cent of that energy becomes movement, and at least seventy-five per cent becomes heat.
A T P spent on other jobs, such as pumping ions across membranes or building glycogen and protein, also ends as heat.
Which tissues make the heat depends on what the body is doing.
Let's go region by region, comparing rest with heavy exercise.
The brain is two per cent of body mass.
It makes sixteen per cent of the heat at rest, but only one per cent in heavy exercise.
The trunk organs, or viscera, are thirty-four per cent of body mass.
At rest they make fifty-six per cent of the heat, falling to eight per cent in heavy exercise.
Muscle and skin together are fifty-six per cent of body mass.
They make eighteen per cent of the heat at rest, and ninety per cent in heavy exercise.
Everything else is eight per cent of body mass, making ten per cent of the heat at rest and one per cent in heavy exercise.
Put together, the brain and trunk organs make about seventy per cent of the heat at rest, although they are only a third of body mass.
In heavy exercise, muscle makes up to ninety per cent.
The blood flowing through working muscle is warmed and carries that heat round the body, which raises core temperature.

## 3. Four ways to exchange heat

Heat moves between the body and its surroundings by four routes.
The first is radiation, which carries heat as infrared rays.
Through radiation, the body loses heat to the sky and the ground and gains it from the sun.
The second is conduction, which passes heat by direct contact, such as the feet on the ground.
Conduction makes only a small contribution, which depends on how well the tissues conduct heat.
The third is convection, which carries heat in a moving fluid, such as air over the skin.
Inside the body that fluid is blood, so the heat it carries depends on blood flow and on the temperature gap between tissue and blood.
The fourth is evaporation, which removes heat as sweat on the skin and water in the airways turn to vapour.
The first three routes can run either way, adding heat when the surroundings are hotter than the skin.
Evaporation only removes heat.
During exercise, evaporative cooling accounts for almost all heat loss.
Sweat cools only if it evaporates.
Sweat that drips off gives no cooling, and neither does humid air that slows evaporation.
Not all energy leaves as heat, though.
A small share leaves the body as mechanical work against gravity.

## 4. Sensors and the thermostat

Temperature is sensed in two places.
In the skin, the sensors are peripheral thermoreceptors, which are free nerve endings.
They report both the temperature and how it is changing.
Cold receptors are the most numerous, and they lie nearest the surface, in or just under the outer layer of skin, the epidermis.
They signal through thin myelinated A delta fibres, which conduct quickly.
Warm receptors are about ten times fewer, and they lie slightly deeper, in the dermis.
They signal through slower unmyelinated C fibres.
Deep in the body, the hypothalamus itself and other deep sites sense core temperature.
All these signals converge on the preoptic anterior hypothalamus, or P O A H, which works like a thermostat.
It compares core temperature, known as T c, with a set point, known as T set, which is the temperature it aims for.
The difference between them is the thermal error signal.
The P O A H combines this error signal with skin temperature, known as T s k, and the result drives four effectors.
First, the cerebral cortex creates thermal discomfort, which drives behaviour such as seeking shade or adding clothes.
Second, the sweat glands start to secrete, and the skin vessels widen, which is called vasodilation.
Third, the skin arterioles and superficial veins constrict, or narrow, which keeps heat in.
Fourth, skeletal muscle makes heat by shivering.
The set point itself can move.
Pyrogens raise it, and these are the toxins and immune signals that cause fever, such as cytokines.
Exercise training and heat acclimation, the body's adjustment to repeated heat, lower it.
The set point follows a daily, or circadian, rhythm as well.

## 5. Responses to heat and to cold

Both responses are negative feedback loops, meaning that the response cancels the change that caused it.
In heat, the hypothalamus senses a rise in blood temperature and acts through the sympathetic nervous system.
The smooth muscle of the skin arterioles relaxes, so more warm blood reaches the skin and loses its heat there.
The sweat glands become active, which raises evaporative heat loss.
Body temperature falls back.
In cold, a fall in blood or skin temperature is sensed.
The sympathetic nervous system constricts the skin arterioles, which reduces heat loss.
The somatic motor nervous system makes the muscles shiver.
Shivering raises metabolism and makes heat, which is called shivering thermogenesis.
Body temperature rises back.
Shivering lifts heat production above its resting level, the basal metabolic rate.
This is the energy the body uses at complete rest.
Measured per square metre of body surface, this rate falls steeply through childhood and slowly after about twenty years of age.
It is also lower in women than in men, at about forty against forty-five watts per square metre at age twenty.
These mechanisms form three zones along a scale of skin temperature.
Below a skin temperature of about twenty degrees Celsius is metabolic regulation, where heat production rises to nearly five times basal.
That heat comes from shivering and from nonshivering thermogenesis.
Nonshivering thermogenesis is heat made without muscle contraction, mainly in brown fat.
From about twenty to thirty-five degrees is vascular regulation.
Heat production stays basal, and skin blood flow alone controls temperature.
This vascular range runs from maximal vasoconstriction at about twenty degrees to maximal vasodilation at about thirty-five degrees.
This band is the thermoneutral zone.
Above about thirty-five degrees, sweating takes over.
Across skin temperatures from ten degrees below zero to forty-five degrees, core temperature hardly changes, shifting only at the extremes.
Heat production stays at the basal level between about twenty and thirty-five degrees, and climbs as the skin gets colder.
The body is like a house that first shuts its windows and only then turns up the heating.
Across the whole range, core temperature barely moves, which is the point of the system.

## Summary

The body is a warm core held near thirty-seven degrees Celsius, inside a shell that cools and thickens in the cold.
Regulation works from about thirty-six to forty-one degrees.
It is impaired in heat stroke above forty-one degrees and in hypothermia below thirty-five.
Only forty-two per cent of fuel energy is captured in A T P, and less than twenty-five per cent of that becomes work, leaving most to end as heat.
The trunk organs make most of the heat at rest, and muscle makes up to ninety per cent in exercise.
Heat is exchanged by radiation, conduction, convection and evaporation.
Evaporation of sweat removes almost all heat in exercise.
The P O A H compares core temperature with a set point, which pyrogens raise and training and heat acclimation lower.
Heat is met by skin vasodilation and sweating, and cold by vasoconstriction and shivering, all by negative feedback.
Across the thermoneutral zone, at skin temperatures of about twenty to thirty-five degrees, skin blood flow alone holds temperature steady.
