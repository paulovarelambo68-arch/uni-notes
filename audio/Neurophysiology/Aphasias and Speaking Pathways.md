## Aphasias and Speaking Pathways

The aphasias are language disorders, and they typically follow a stroke or a lesion in the relevant area.
Those same areas also serve as the stations of the two speaking pathways.
Whether you are repeating a word you've heard or reading a written word aloud, speech runs through these stations as an ordered sequence.
That sequence ends at the motor cortex.
We'll go through the three aphasias first, before following each of the two pathways in turn.

## Aphasias

First up is Broca's aphasia, which follows a lesion of Broca's area.
It produces laboured, slow speech in a monotone, with impaired articulation.
Comprehension, however, survives.
Wernicke's aphasia follows a lesion of Wernicke's area, and it reverses that pattern.
The speech is now fluent and melodic, yet it contains errors in word choice, and comprehension becomes difficult.
Finally, there is conduction aphasia.
This reflects damage to the connection between the two areas, rather than to either area itself.
As a result, simple speech is intelligible and comprehension remains intact.
Repeating sentences, on the other hand, is difficult.
That's because repetition must be relayed directly from Wernicke's area to Broca's area.

## Speaking a heard word

Now let's follow the route for speaking a word that has been heard.
It starts at the auditory cortex, which receives the sound of the word.
The word is then passed to Wernicke's area, where it is understood.
From there it is relayed to Broca's area, which plans the utterance.
Lastly, it is delivered to the motor cortex for execution.
Put simply, the route runs from the auditory cortex to Wernicke's area, on to Broca's area, and ends at the motor cortex.

## Speaking a written word

What about a written word?
Reading aloud adds two steps at the front of that same route.
This time, the written word is received by the visual cortex.
Next, the angular gyrus converts it into its spoken form.
From that point on, it follows exactly the same sequence as a heard word, through Wernicke's area and Broca's area to the motor cortex.
So the full route runs from the visual cortex to the angular gyrus, and from there to Wernicke's area, Broca's area and the motor cortex.
Notice that the auditory cortex isn't needed in this case.

## Summary

Let's sum up.
Aphasias are language disorders that follow a stroke or lesion of the relevant area.
Broca's aphasia gives laboured, slow speech in a monotone.
Wernicke's aphasia gives fluent, melodic speech, but with errors in word choice and poor comprehension.
Conduction aphasia, meanwhile, impairs repetition alone.
As for the pathways, speaking a heard word runs from the auditory cortex to Wernicke's area, then Broca's area, and finally the motor cortex.
Speaking a written word begins instead at the visual cortex and passes through the angular gyrus, before joining that same route.
And that completes the aphasias, along with the two routes that speech takes to the motor cortex.
