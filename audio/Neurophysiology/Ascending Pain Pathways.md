## Ascending Pain Pathways

The anterolateral system is the ascending system of the spinal cord that carries pain and temperature to the brain.
It forms the pain and temperature arm of the ascending tracts.
From the thalamus onwards, it works as two parallel pathways that share the same start.
In both, the A-delta and C afferents synapse in the dorsal horn, and the second order axons cross and ascend to the thalamus.
Where the two differ is in where they relay and where they end.
The spinothalamic tract, the component that runs directly from cord to thalamus, is the sensory discriminative route.
In other words, the tract is the anatomy, and the discriminative aspect is its function.
The motivational and affective dimension, by contrast, travels in the remaining projections of the system, which have no single tract name.
These relay in the medial thalamic nuclei and end in the cingulate cortex, the insula and the prefrontal cortex.
We'll follow the discriminative route first, then the motivational and affective one, and finish by tracing a burnt finger through the whole pathway.

## 1. The anterolateral system and the spinothalamic tract

To begin, the anterolateral system, the ascending system for pain and temperature, takes its name from its position in the anterolateral white matter of the spinal cord.
Its direct component, the one linking the cord with the thalamus, is the spinothalamic tract.
Pain and temperature afferents, namely the A-delta and C fibres, enter the cord at different levels depending on where they come from.
Those from the lower body enter at lumbar levels, while those from the upper body, excluding the face, enter at cervical levels.
They then synapse on second order neurons in the dorsal horn.
The axons of these neurons cross to the opposite side and ascend as the spinothalamic tract.
On the way up, they pass through the brain stem, travelling via the caudal and middle medulla, the pons and the midbrain.
They synapse in the ventral posterior lateral nucleus of the thalamus.
From there, third order neurons reach the primary somatic sensory cortex, also called S one.
This is why the spinothalamic tract and the sensory discriminative aspect are the same pathway.
The tract names the anatomy, and the aspect names the function.
So what does that function analyse?
It works out where the pain is, how intense it is and what it feels like, in other words its location, intensity and quality.
Quality is the character of the sensation, whether the pain feels sharp, dull, burning or aching.
Within the somatosensory cortex, the signal is distributed between S one and the secondary somatosensory cortex, or S two.
S one receives the fine spatial and intensive detail of the stimulus, resolving exactly where it is and how strong it is.
S two, for its part, elaborates the information further, contributing to the recognition and interpretation of the stimulus.
Two terms are worth pinning down here.
A localised stimulus is one the person can assign to a precise point of the body.
A characterised stimulus is one whose intensity and quality are consciously identified.

## 2. The motivational and affective dimension of pain

Now for the second pathway, the motivational and affective dimension, which covers the cognitive, emotional and motor aspects of the experience.
Put simply, it concerns how the pain is judged, the unpleasantness it carries and the behaviour it provokes.
Unlike the first route, this one has no tract name of its own.
Instead, it is formed by the remaining projections of the anterolateral system.
Their nociceptive drive reaches the medial nuclei of the thalamus, from which third order neurons project to each target.
They terminate in the prefrontal cortex and the anterior cingulate cortex, where the signal is evaluated and acquires its emotional weight.
Another target is the insula, which senses the internal state of the body.
The insula gives pain its felt unpleasantness, as opposed to its location.
Beyond these, the projections reach the amygdala and the hippocampus, which attach feeling and memory to the experience.
They engage the descending pain pathway as well.
This pathway projects back down to the dorsal horn and modulates the nociceptive input arriving from the A-delta and C fibres.
As for the motor aspect, it is served by the motor cortex and the cerebellum.
The cerebellum is activated by pain not to perceive it, but to coordinate the movements that pain provokes, such as withdrawal, guarding and escape.

## 3. Worked example: burning the top of a finger

That brings us to a worked example, touching a hot surface with the top of a finger.
This one event runs the whole pathway in order.
The heat opens the transient receptor potential vanilloid one channels, known as T R P V one, in the nociceptive free nerve endings.
Their threshold is about forty-three degrees Celsius.
This transduces the stimulus into action potentials in the A-delta and C fibres, whose cell bodies sit in the dorsal root ganglion.
Their central axons enter the cord at cervical level, since the finger belongs to the upper body.
They terminate in their fibre specific laminae of the dorsal horn.
A-delta fibres end in laminae one and five, and C fibres in laminae one and two.
There, the terminals release glutamate and substance P onto the second order neurons.
The axons of these neurons cross to the opposite side within the cord, and ascend as the spinothalamic tract through the brain stem.
They reach the ventral posterior lateral nucleus of the thalamus, from which the third order neurons project to S one and S two of the somatosensory cortex.
It is here that the burn is localised to the fingertip and characterised as burning.
Meanwhile, in parallel and without waiting for any of this, the A-delta input drives the flexion, or withdrawal, reflex at spinal level.
This reflex contracts the agonist and silences the antagonist.
As a result, the finger is pulled away before the pain is even consciously felt.

## Summary

Pulling the threads together, the anterolateral system is the ascending system for pain and temperature.
Its direct component, the spinothalamic tract, is the sensory discriminative route, one pathway named both by its anatomy and by its function.
Along it, afferents synapse on second order neurons in the dorsal horn, and the axons of these neurons cross and ascend through the brain stem.
They reach the ventral posterior lateral nucleus of the thalamus, and third order neurons continue to S one and S two.
S one resolves precisely where the stimulus is and how strong it is, while S two elaborates its recognition.
The motivational and affective dimension, on the other hand, has no tract name.
The remaining projections of the system relay through the medial thalamic nuclei.
From there, they distribute the signal to the prefrontal cortex, the anterior cingulate cortex, the insula, the amygdala and the hippocampus.
Together, these serve the cognitive, emotional and motor aspects of pain.
These projections also engage the descending pain pathway, which projects back to the cord to modulate incoming nociceptive signals.
So the anterolateral system comes down to one starting point in the dorsal horn and two different destinations in the brain.
