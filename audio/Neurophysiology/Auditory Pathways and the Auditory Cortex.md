## Auditory Pathways and the Auditory Cortex

The auditory pathway is the chain of stations that carries the signal generated in the cochlea up to the cerebral cortex.
It runs from the auditory nerve to the brain stem, then to the medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, and on to the auditory cortex.
Because most fibres cross over in the brain stem, the representation of each ear is mainly contralateral, meaning it lies on the opposite side of the brain.
Even so, some fibres from each ear also reach both sides of the brain.
We'll follow the signal out of the cochlea, see where its fibres cross, branch and turn back, and finish in the auditory cortex itself.

## From the cochlea to the cortex

The journey starts with the inner hair cells, which synapse on more than ninety per cent of the ganglion cells.
The remaining ganglion cell afferents synapse with the outer hair cells, whose connections are almost entirely efferent.
So virtually all the auditory information that reaches the brain comes from this one receptor population.
The axons of the ganglion cells form the auditory nerve, which carries the signal to the brain stem.
From there, the signal ascends to the medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, and on to the auditory cortex.
The medial geniculate nucleus is the final thalamic relay for hearing.
It is the equivalent of the lateral geniculate nucleus in vision.

## Decussation, collaterals and retrograde fibres

Now, within the brain stem, the majority of fibres decussate, which means they cross to the opposite side.
As a result, the signal carried onward is mainly contralateral, and each hemisphere deals chiefly with the opposite ear.
Yet the crossing is not complete.
Some fibres from each ear go to both sides of the brain, so every station above the brain stem receives input from both ears.
Along the way, the ascending fibres give off side branches, known as collaterals.
These collaterals innervate the reticular activating system and the vermis of the cerebellum.
They mediate general sensory and motor responses to sound, rather than perception.
The reticular activating system produces arousal, which is the alerting reaction to a sound.
The vermis of the cerebellum, meanwhile, handles the motor side, coordinating the body's response to the sound.
Importantly, the pathway does not run in one direction only.
Inhibitory retrograde fibres run from each level of the auditory system back to the cochlea.
In this way, each level can dampen the levels below it, right down to the receptor itself.
Frequency is mapped in order along the cochlea.
For that reason, the descending fibres act on selected frequency channels rather than on the whole cochlea.
At the cochlear end, they terminate on the outer hair cells of their own region, turning down the cochlear amplifier for just those frequencies.
Suppressing the unwanted bands raises the attended sound above the background.
Put simply, this lets the system tune in to a specific sound.
It is the same general strategy as lateral inhibition in the somatosensory system.
Both improve the quality of sensory information by silencing what surrounds the signal.
The difference is that here the inhibition is not fixed between neighbouring pathways.
Instead, it is directed from higher levels and shifted as needed.

## The auditory cortex

That brings us to the auditory cortex, which lies in the temporal lobe.
The primary auditory cortex is flanked by the secondary auditory cortex.
These secondary regions are called belt areas, because they ring the primary cortex like a belt.
Across the primary auditory cortex, the represented frequencies are laid out in sequence.
They run from five hundred hertz, through one thousand, two thousand, four thousand and eight thousand, up to sixteen thousand hertz.
The low frequency end corresponds to the apex of the cochlea, and the high frequency end to its base.
In other words, the frequency map of the cochlea is redrawn on the cortex.
The primary auditory cortex therefore has a tonotopic organisation.
Its neurones respond to specific frequencies of sound, and neighbouring positions represent neighbouring frequencies.
Like the visual cortex, this cortical sheet is also divided into columns.
Here, though, the columns alternate in how the two ears drive them.
One column is excited by both ears, while the next is excited by one ear and inhibited by the other.
The upshot is that alternating columns are responsive to input from both ears.
By comparing the two ears column by column, the cortex obtains the differences between them.
These differences, in turn, support sound localisation.
Beyond frequency, the surrounding areas that form the secondary auditory cortex are involved in elaborating other aspects of sound.
Their output divides into two pathways.
One is a parietal pathway for direction, that is, where the sound comes from.
The other is a temporal pathway for meaning, which is what the sound is.

## Summary

Drawing the threads together, almost all auditory information reaching the brain originates at the inner hair cells, which synapse on more than ninety per cent of the ganglion cells.
The signal ascends from the auditory nerve through the brain stem and the medial geniculate nucleus to the auditory cortex.
Most fibres cross in the brain stem, yet some fibres from each ear reach both sides.
Collaterals to the reticular activating system and the cerebellar vermis give general sensory and motor responses to sound.
Meanwhile, inhibitory retrograde fibres from every level back to the cochlea let the system tune in to a specific sound.
The primary auditory cortex keeps the cochlear frequency map in tonotopic form, and it compares the two ears in alternating columns for sound localisation.
Finally, the surrounding areas elaborate sound further, along a parietal pathway for direction and a temporal pathway for meaning.
Our journey from the cochlea to the auditory cortex ends here.
