## Classification of Nerve Fibres

Nerve fibres are classified by how fast they conduct, but as we'll see, the classification also reflects what they do.
Our first stop is the three properties behind it, followed by the two naming systems that label the fibres.
After that, we'll take the six fibre types in turn, then the points you're likely to be asked, and a memory hook to finish.

## What the classification is based on

Three linked properties determine how fast a fibre conducts.
One is diameter, because the larger the axon, the lower its internal resistance, and therefore the faster the conduction.
Another is myelination.
Myelin insulates the axon and forces the action potential to jump between the nodes of Ranvier.
This is called saltatory conduction, and it's much faster than the continuous conduction of unmyelinated fibres.
The third property is conduction velocity, which is the measurable result of the other two.
For myelinated fibres, a useful rule of thumb is that the velocity in metres per second is roughly six times the diameter in micrometres.
So here is the key logic to remember.
Thick fibres with myelin are the fastest, and thin fibres without myelin are the slowest.
Between those extremes, myelin tips the balance towards faster.

## The two naming systems

Now, there are two naming systems for these fibres.
The general classification, from Erlanger and Gasser, uses the letters A, B and C, and it applies to both motor and sensory fibres.
Within it, the A fibres are further subdivided into alpha, beta, gamma and delta.
The sensory classification, from Lloyd and Hunt, uses the Roman numerals one, two, three and four instead.
This one applies only to afferent, or sensory, fibres.
In fact, the two systems describe the same fibres with different labels.
Group one roughly corresponds to the A-alpha afferents, and group two to A-beta.
Likewise, group three roughly corresponds to A-delta, and group four to C.

## The fibre types

With the labels in place, let's go through the fibre types, beginning with A-alpha, the largest and fastest.
These fibres are myelinated, twelve to twenty micrometres in diameter, and they conduct at seventy to one hundred and twenty metres per second.
Their motor arm is formed by the alpha motor neurons, which supply the extrafusal skeletal muscle fibres.
Those extrafusal fibres are the ones that actually produce force.
Their sensory arm is the same as group one.
It's made up of the group one A afferents from the primary endings of the muscle spindle, which signal muscle length and the speed of stretch.
Alongside them are the group one B afferents from the Golgi tendon organs, which signal muscle tension.
Put together, the role of A-alpha fibres is movement and proprioception.
Next up are the A-beta fibres, which are myelinated, five to twelve micrometres in diameter, and conduct at thirty to seventy metres per second.
They are the afferents for touch and pressure from the skin's mechanoreceptors, along with those from the secondary spindle endings.
They're equivalent to sensory group two.
Moving on, the A-gamma fibres are myelinated, two to eight micrometres in diameter, with velocities of ten to fifty metres per second.
They're motor only, belonging to the gamma motor neurons that supply the intrafusal muscle fibres of the spindle.
These fibres do not produce movement.
Instead, they adjust the spindle's sensitivity, so that it keeps working at all muscle lengths.
A-delta fibres, the last of the A group, are thinly myelinated, one to five micrometres in diameter, and conduct at three to thirty metres per second.
They carry fast pain, which is sharp, pricking and well localised.
This is the first pain, the one you feel immediately after an injury.
They also carry cold, and they're equivalent to sensory group three.
After the A fibres come the B fibres, which are myelinated but thin, one to three micrometres in diameter, and conduct at five to fifteen metres per second.
They are the preganglionic autonomic fibres.
Here's a classic exam point.
Although they're myelinated, B fibres are slower than most A fibres, which shows that the classification reflects function as well as speed.
That leaves the C fibres, the smallest and slowest.
They're unmyelinated, under one micrometre in diameter, and conduct at under two metres per second.
They include the postganglionic autonomic fibres.
They're also the afferents for slow pain, which is dull, burning and poorly localised, the so-called second pain.
Warmth, and temperature in general, travel along them as well.
C fibres are equivalent to sensory group four, and they're sometimes labelled simply as group four.

## Points you are likely to be asked

That brings us to the points you're likely to be asked about.
Top of the list is why fast fibres are fast.
A large diameter lowers internal resistance, and myelin enables saltatory conduction between the nodes of Ranvier.
Then there's the contrast between fast and slow pain.
A-delta fibres give immediate, sharp, localised pain, whereas C fibres give delayed, dull, diffuse pain.
In other words, the same stimulus is carried by two fibre types.
Another point is how the A fibres divide into motor and sensory types.
A-alpha and A-gamma are the motor types, supplying the extrafusal and the intrafusal fibres respectively.
A-delta, on the other hand, is sensory, carrying pain and cold, and A-beta is sensory too, carrying touch and pressure.
As for the autonomic fibres, B fibres run before the ganglion and are myelinated, while C fibres run after it and are unmyelinated.
Finally, there's the clinical relevance.
Local anaesthetics block small fibres first, so pain disappears before touch.
Conversely, losing the function of large fibres, namely A-alpha and A-beta, impairs proprioception, vibration sense and reflexes.
This matters for movement control in clinical exercise settings.

## Memory hook

To round things off, there's a memory hook that ties the whole sequence together.
Speed and size decrease as you move from A-alpha to A-beta, on to A-gamma and A-delta, and finally to B and C.
Along the way, function shifts from movement and proprioception at the fast end to pain, temperature and autonomic control at the slow end.
With that, we've met every type of nerve fibre, from the thick, fast A-alpha fibres to the thin, slow C fibres.
