## Cutaneous Mechanoreceptors

Cutaneous mechanoreceptors are the sensory receptors of the skin.
They transduce mechanical deformation into electrical signals, and in doing so they provide tactile information.
That information is carried mostly by large diameter, myelinated A-beta axons.
These axons conduct rapidly, and they give the fine spatial and temporal resolution that touch requires.
We'll sort the four receptor types along two axes, meet them one by one, see how they share the work of reading braille, and finish with a recap.

## The four cutaneous mechanoreceptors

Four types of mechanoreceptor mediate touch, and they are classified along two axes.
The first is the speed of adaptation, and here a receptor is either slowly adapting or rapidly adapting.
The second is the size of the receptive field, which is either small or large.
Slowly adapting receptors keep firing for as long as the stimulus is applied, which means they signal its sustained features.
Rapidly adapting receptors, by contrast, fire only at the onset and offset of the stimulus, and therefore signal change and motion.
As for field size, small receptive fields give high spatial acuity.
Large receptive fields sacrifice detail in exchange for sensitivity over a wide area.
The four types also differ in depth.
Merkel disc receptors and Meissner's corpuscles sit in the superficial layers of the skin.
Ruffini endings and Pacinian corpuscles, on the other hand, lie in the deep layers.
These deep receptors have larger receptive fields, which can extend across a whole finger or much of the hand.

## Merkel receptors

First up are the Merkel receptors, which are slowly adapting and have a small receptive field.
They pick up form and texture.
This is the kind of discrimination that lets you tell cotton from silk.
Their effective stimuli are edges, points, corners and curvature.
Because they adapt slowly, they sustain their response to maintained indentation.
The result is a continuous readout of fine spatial detail.
These are the receptors that let you tell an apple from an orange by form and texture, and that let you feel the corners of a table.

## Meissner corpuscles

Next come the Meissner corpuscles, which are rapidly adapting and likewise have a small receptive field.
They mediate fine touch and motion detection, and their effective stimulus is skin motion.
They contribute to grip control as well.
As an object slips across the skin, they produce a burst of firing, and that burst is what allows grip force to be adjusted.
Since they adapt rapidly, they do not respond to sustained indentation, only to changes.
This explains why a gentle, unchanging touch of the fingertips on the forearm fades after a few seconds.
After all, rapidly adapting receptors signal only change, so a constant stimulus quickly stops evoking a response.

## Pacinian corpuscles

Turning to the deep receptors, Pacinian corpuscles are rapidly adapting, with a large receptive field.
They respond to pressure.
Lying in the dermis and deeper tissues, they can have a receptive field that covers an entire finger or hand.
Their effective stimulus is vibration.
This allows us to perceive distant events through vibrations transmitted by objects held in the hand, as happens when we use tools.

## Ruffini endings

Finally among the four, Ruffini endings are slowly adapting and have a large receptive field.
What they register is stretch of the skin.
In this way, they give a sustained signal of tangential force, of hand shape and of the direction of motion across the skin.

## Reading braille

All four mechanoreceptor types respond when someone reads braille, but they contribute in different ways.
Merkel and Meissner recognise the details of the pattern.
They combine the sustained spatial detail from Merkel with the motion related change from Meissner.
Meanwhile, Pacinian and Ruffini track the movement and position of the fingers as they scan across the text.
It's worth looking at each role more closely.
Merkel's disc is the pattern reader.
Its contribution is the actual spatial image of the braille characters.
That's because its fine acuity and its sustained response to each dot supply the form and texture information that identifies the letter.
Meissner's corpuscle is the motion detector.
It signals each dot as a transient event, that is, the skin motion of dot edges crossing the finger.
It also controls grip and scanning pressure, so that the finger keeps gliding steadily over the text.
Ruffini's ending, for its part, is the stretch reporter.
It contributes the tangential force and direction of the scanning movement, signalling how the skin deforms and which way the finger is moving across the dots.
Last of all, the Pacinian corpuscle is the vibration reporter.
Its contribution is the vibration generated by the scanning motion itself, which in this case comes from friction between the skin and the paper.
This helps to track the movement of the finger across the surface.
It's for this reason that the Pacinian corpuscle fires almost throughout, rather than only at the dots.
Taken together, this is the division of labour that Purves describes.
The small field pair reads what is under the finger, while the large field pair reads what the finger is doing.

## Summary

To sum up, tactile information travels mainly on large myelinated A-beta axons.
It is extracted by four mechanoreceptors, which divide the labour according to adaptation speed and receptive field size.
Merkel receptors are slowly adapting with a small field, and they handle form and texture.
Meissner corpuscles are rapidly adapting with a small field, serving fine touch, motion detection and grip control.
Pacinian corpuscles, rapidly adapting with a large field, respond to pressure and vibration.
Ruffini endings complete the set, slowly adapting with a large field and detecting skin stretch.
The small field pair sits superficially and resolves detail.
The large field pair, however, lies deep and covers wide areas.
In braille reading, Merkel and Meissner read the pattern detail, while Pacinian and Ruffini track finger movement and position.
That rounds off the four cutaneous mechanoreceptors and the way they share out the work of touch.
