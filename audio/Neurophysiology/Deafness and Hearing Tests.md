## Deafness and Hearing Tests

Deafness is a partial or complete loss of hearing.
Sound is only perceived once the information gathered by the ear reaches the auditory cortex.
A failure at any stage of that chain, from the outer ear to the auditory nerve, can therefore produce deafness.
These failures fall into two main types, conductive and sensorineural.
The hearing tests that follow exist to tell them apart.
Along the way, we'll cover noise damage, the audiogram and the grading of severity.

## Noise damage and high frequency deafness

Noise is a good place to begin.
Prolonged or frequent exposure to excessive noise degenerates the hair cells, which is exactly the danger of loud music.
Hair cells do not regenerate, so the damage is permanent.
The most vulnerable are the hair cells at the base of the cochlea.
Since the base encodes high frequencies, their degeneration produces high frequency deafness.
In other words, thresholds rise first and worst for high pitched sounds.

## The two types of deafness

Now to the two types themselves.
Conductive deafness is a failure of transmission through the outer or middle ear.
The cochlea and the auditory nerve are intact, yet vibrations never reach them efficiently.
Typical causes include wax blocking the ear canal, a ruptured eardrum, or fixation of the middle ear bones.
With the sensorineural machinery still working, conductive loss is the usually treatable one.
A hearing aid amplifies sound to compensate for the inefficient conductive apparatus.
Standard procedures, such as clearing the blockage or surgery on the ossicles, can also restore transmission directly.
Sensorineural deafness, by contrast, is a failure of the inner ear or the auditory nerve.
Most often the cochlear hair cells die, as in noise damage, or the nerve itself is damaged.
As a result, even a vibration delivered perfectly to the cochlea cannot be transduced or conveyed onwards.
Amplification cannot restore a neural signal that was never generated, so hearing aids are of limited help here.

## Hearing tests

Turning to hearing tests, two routes deliver sound to the cochlea during testing.
The first is air conduction, which presents the sound through the air, as in normal listening.
Before the tone can be perceived, it must pass through the outer, middle and inner ear, together with the whole auditory pathway.
A fault at any point along that chain raises the measured threshold.
Bone conduction, on the other hand, uses a vibrator pressed against the skull to shake the cochlea directly through the bones of the head.
That way it bypasses the outer and middle ear, and tests only the inner ear and beyond.
Comparing the two tells us whether the fault lies before the cochlea or within it.
The same comparison can be made at two levels of precision.
The quick version is the bedside tuning fork test, which distinguishes conductive from sensorineural loss with no equipment beyond a vibrating fork.
In the Weber test, the fork is pressed against the midline of the skull, which lets both cochleas receive the vibration equally through bone.
A patient with conductive loss hears it louder in the affected ear.
That's because the blockage stops the vibration escaping back out of the skull through the ear canal on that side.
A patient with sensorineural loss, meanwhile, hears it louder in the intact ear.
In this case, the reason is that the damaged cochlea or nerve cannot turn the vibration into a neural signal.
The precise version is audiometry.
It measures the hearing threshold, meaning the quietest pure tone the patient can detect, at each frequency.
This is done separately for air and for bone conduction, and the results are plotted as a pure tone audiogram.
Reading one is the next step.

## Reading the pure tone audiogram

An audiogram is a chart of hearing thresholds, plotting hearing level in decibels against frequency in hertz.
Frequency runs along the chart from low pitch on the left to high pitch on the right.
The decibel scale, however, is drawn the unusual way round.
Zero decibels sits at the top, meaning normal hearing, and the numbers increase downwards.
Each plotted point is the threshold at one frequency.
The further down the chart a point sits, then, the louder the tone had to be before the patient heard it.
Put simply, a curve that sinks lower on the chart means a raised threshold, not a better one.
Every chart carries two curves.
Air conduction is drawn in blue or marked with crosses, whereas bone conduction is drawn in red or marked with arrows.
With normal hearing, both curves run along the top of the chart, within a few decibels of zero at every frequency.
In some patients, though, the two curves part company.
Here, bone conduction stays close to normal, but the air conduction thresholds are raised across the range.
They are worst at low frequencies, where the air curve sinks to about fifty decibels at two hundred and fifty hertz.
Towards high frequencies they recover.
The gap between the air and bone conduction curves is therefore widest at low pitch, and it narrows as the two curves rejoin.
Sound is reaching the cochlea normally through bone, but not through the air route.
That is the hallmark of a conductive problem.

## Severity of deafness

Next, severity is graded by how far the threshold has fallen.
The bands run straight across all frequencies, since severity depends on the threshold level alone.
Starting from the best hearing, normal spans zero to fifteen decibels, and slight runs from fifteen to twenty-five decibels.
Mild covers twenty-five to forty decibels, and moderate forty to fifty-five decibels.
After that comes moderately severe, from fifty-five to seventy decibels, followed by severe, from seventy to ninety decibels.
Finally, profound is anything over ninety decibels.

## The three audiogram patterns

That brings us to the three patterns an audiogram can show.
In normal hearing, both curves overlap within about ten decibels of the top, across the whole range from one hundred and twenty-five to eight thousand hertz.
That's because the air and bone routes both work.
In conductive deafness, air conduction thresholds are raised while bone conduction is preserved.
The bone curve stays near zero decibels at every frequency, and the air curve sits around forty-five to sixty decibels.
As a result, the gap between the curves extends across the whole range.
The loss is roughly flat, as the blockage attenuates all sound rather than selectively destroying frequency tuned hair cells.
In sensorineural deafness, both curves are raised together, with no gap between them.
They slope downwards towards the high frequencies, falling from near normal at low pitch to about fifty-five decibels at four thousand hertz.
This is precisely the curve predicted by the loss of basal hair cells.
It is the classic picture of noise induced and age related deafness.

## Summary

Pulling the threads together, deafness comes in two forms.
The first is conductive deafness, a failure of transmission through the outer or middle ear.
Given that the cochlea is intact, hearing aids and standard procedures can usually correct it.
The second is sensorineural deafness, a death of hair cells or damage to the nerve that amplification cannot undo.
Its most famous example is the high frequency loss produced by loud noise.
Air conduction tests the whole chain from ear canal to cortex, while bone conduction bypasses the outer and middle ear.
For that reason, comparing the two curves on an audiogram both separates the types and grades the severity, from normal to profound.
That covers deafness and the tests that tell its two forms apart.
