## EEG and the Stages of Sleep

The electroencephalogram, or E E G, is a record of the electrical potential changes of the brain.
These changes are produced by the synchronous firing of large populations of cortical neurons.
On the trace, they appear as waves of different frequency and amplitude.
The E E G is also the recording by which the stages of sleep are defined.
We'll see first why synchrony shapes the trace, then meet the named rhythms, and finally follow how sleep divides into its stages.

## The EEG and Synchrony

Each electrode on the scalp sums the activity of thousands of neurons, so the trace depends on their timing.
When cells fire independently, they fluctuate out of phase and cancel out.
All that remains is a small, fast residue.
Populations firing together, by contrast, arrive in phase and summate into large waves.
Yet such waves can only change as fast as the single rhythm driving them, and in sleep that rhythm is slow.
So both properties follow from one cause.
Put simply, the more synchronised the neuronal firing, the larger the amplitude and the lower the frequency.
That is why the alert, desynchronised brain gives a fast, low voltage trace.
Deep sleep, on the other hand, is synchronised to one slow beat, and it gives a slow, high voltage trace.

## EEG Rhythms

Along that scale, four named rhythms mark the descent from waking into sleep.
In the alert brain, the beta rhythm dominates.
It is fast and small, at thirteen to sixty hertz and five to ten microvolts.
During inattention, meaning relaxed waking with the eyes closed, the alpha rhythm takes over.
Its frequency is eight to thirteen hertz, and its amplitude thirty to fifty microvolts.
Sleep and anaesthesia then bring the slow rhythms of large amplitude.
These are theta, at four to seven hertz, and delta, at zero point five to four hertz.
Delta's amplitude is over seventy-five microvolts.

## The Division of Sleep

Now, sleep is not merely a state of reduced brain activity.
Rather, it is an actively regulated succession of states, each with its own E E G pattern.
A night's sleep divides into slow wave sleep and rapid eye movement sleep, or REM sleep.
Slow wave sleep accounts for seventy-five per cent of it, and REM sleep for the other twenty-five per cent.
By current consensus, the non REM stages number three, called N one to N three.
Previously, four were distinguished.

## The Stages of Slow Wave Sleep

Let's begin with N one, the lightest stage and the transition into sleep.
Here the rhythm slows to four to six hertz, entering the theta band, and the amplitude increases.
Next comes N two, which deepens the trend.
Its waves are more irregular and slower, at one to five hertz, straddling theta and delta, and their amplitude is larger still.
Against this slow background, three signature events stand out.
The first are sleep spindles, short bursts of rhythmic activity at twelve to sixteen hertz.
They are alpha like in frequency, but they are of thalamic origin, and their function is not fully understood.
Alongside the spindles appear K-complexes, large, isolated waveforms that rise above the background rhythm.
They are particularly evident in frontal cortex.
A K-complex can be evoked by a meaningful stimulus, such as the sleeper's own name or a baby crying.
It is as if the sleeping brain were deciding whether to wake.
The third event belongs to a different scale altogether.
These are hippocampal ripples, very brief oscillations of eighty to one hundred and twenty hertz generated in the hippocampus.
In fact, they are faster even than beta.
That leaves N three, the deepest stage and proper slow wave sleep.
In this stage the rhythm settles fully into delta, with the slowest and largest waves.
They run at zero point five to three hertz, with amplitudes of over seventy-five microvolts.
Meanwhile, spindles become very rare, and there are no K-complexes.

## Summary

In summary, the E E G turns sleep into a readable sequence.
As cortical firing synchronises, the waves slow and grow.
They shift from beta and alpha in waking to theta and delta in sleep.
The core mapping to retain is slow wave sleep descending through N one, N two and N three across seventy-five per cent of the night.
The remaining twenty-five per cent is given to REM sleep.
And that's the sleeping brain, read wave by wave.
