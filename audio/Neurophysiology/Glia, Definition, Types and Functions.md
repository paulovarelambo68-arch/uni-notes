## Glia, Definition, Types and Functions

The name glia comes from the Greek word for glue, after the old idea that these cells hold the nervous system together.
Glia are the non-neuronal cells of the nervous system, acting as the protectors and support cells of neurons.
Unlike neurons, they are not electrically excitable.
Instead, they heighten the functional capacity of neurons by insulating them, controlling their surroundings, building the brain and defending it.
They are also far more numerous, with ten to fifty times more glia than neurons.
In what follows, we'll meet the six types of glia, see what each one does, and then ask where they come from.

## 1. The six types of glia

The simplest way to place the six types is by where they act and what they look after.
First up are the Schwann cells, the myelin-forming glia of the peripheral nervous system, or P N S.
A single Schwann cell wraps itself around a segment of axon, laying down the insulating sheath.
Their counterparts are the oligodendrocytes, the myelin-forming glia of the central nervous system, or C N S.
Put another way, they are the Schwann cell equivalent inside the brain and spinal cord.
Next come the astrocytes, star-shaped glia of the C N S that look after the extracellular environment around neurons.
The ependymal cells line the ventricles, and they work with the astrocytes in controlling that same environment.
Radial glia, by contrast, are developmental glia, active while the brain is being built.
Finally, the microglia are the immune cells of the C N S.

## 2. Glial functions

Now let's match those types to their functions.
Myelin is made by Schwann cells in the P N S and by oligodendrocytes in the C N S.
Wrapping axons in insulating membrane is what allows fast saltatory conduction.
So these cells quite literally heighten the functional capacity of neurons.
Control of the extracellular environment around neurons belongs to the astrocytes and the ependymal cells.
They keep the fluid bathing the neurons chemically stable, which neurons need in order to signal reliably.
Brain development, meanwhile, is the job of the radial glia.
And immune function belongs to the microglia.

## 3. Origins: microglia vs macroglia

That brings us to origins, because not all glia are born the same way.
The microglia are the outsiders.
They are derived from macrophages outside the C N S, so they belong to the immune lineage rather than the neural one.
Fittingly, their job matches that origin.
They are phagocytes, activated by infection and injury, and they clear debris and dead cells from damaged tissue.
Macroglia, on the other hand, are all the other glial groups, namely the astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells, ependymal cells and radial glia.
These are derived from neural stem cells, which means they share their lineage with neurons themselves.
The lineage runs from self-renewing stem cells, which give rise to early progenitor cells, and these in turn produce neurons, oligodendrocytes and astrocytes.

## Summary

Pulling the threads together, glia are the non-neuronal support and protector cells of the nervous system, and their name means glue.
They are ten to fifty times more numerous than neurons.
The six types map onto four core functions.
Schwann cells in the P N S and oligodendrocytes in the C N S make myelin.
Astrocytes and ependymal cells control the extracellular environment, while radial glia act in brain development.
As for immune defence, that falls to the microglia.
By origin, microglia are macrophage-derived phagocytes from outside the C N S.
All the other glia are macroglia, born from neural stem cells just like neurons.
And there you have the glial family, with its six types, four jobs and two origins.
