## Hair Cells and Cochlear Transduction

The hair cells of the Organ of Corti are the receptor cells of hearing.
They sit upon the basilar membrane, and their hairs are coupled to the tectorial membrane above.
Cochlear transduction is the conversion of sound-driven vibration into an electrical signal.
Bending of the stereocilia opens or closes potassium channels, so the hair cell depolarises or hyperpolarises in step with the sound.
We'll follow the vibration from the stapes to the basilar membrane, meet the two kinds of hair cell, and then see how bending their hairs produces a signal.

## From stapes to basilar membrane

Sound reaches the cochlea as a mechanical chain.
Vibration of the stapes causes vibration of the perilymph, and the moving fluid in turn vibrates the elastic basilar membrane.
To make what happens next easier to picture, imagine the cochlea uncoiled.
The living cochlea is always wound into its spiral.
But drawn straight, the two scalae and the basilar membrane lie in a line, and the path of the wave is easy to follow.
Importantly, the disturbance does not spread everywhere at once.
Instead, it propagates as a travelling wave along the basilar membrane.
The wave begins at the cochlear base, where it is driven by the stapes at the oval window.
From there it travels towards the apex, where the scala vestibuli and the scala tympani meet at the helicotrema.

## Inner and outer hair cells

Let's turn now to the hair cells themselves.
They sit in the Organ of Corti, standing on the basilar membrane with their hairs connected to the tectorial membrane.
That means any relative movement of the two membranes bends the hairs.
There are sixteen thousand of them in all, though some counts put the figure nearer twenty thousand.
They divide into two populations with distinct jobs.
One row of inner hair cells forms the true sensory receptors, and these cells send the signal towards the cerebral cortex.
Meanwhile, three to four rows of outer hair cells, or five rows in some people, serve signal amplification.
They shorten when depolarised and lengthen when hyperpolarised, feeding energy back into the basilar membrane so that its vibration grows larger.
Seen under an electron microscope, the two populations are easy to tell apart.
The single inner row carries straight bundles of hairs, whereas the outer rows carry bundles in a V-shaped arrangement.
Even so, the arrangement is not fixed.
It varies with position along the cochlea, with the rows and bundles organised differently at the base and at the apex.

## Shearing force bends the stereocilia

So how does a vibration bend the hairs?
The basilar and tectorial membranes are anchored at different points.
As a result, when the basilar membrane vibrates, the two membranes slide past each other, and the bundle is caught between them.
In the upward phase, the shear bends the stereocilia towards their tall edge, and the hair cell depolarises.
In the downward phase, the shearing force reverses.
This time the stereocilia bend away from their tall edge, and the cell hyperpolarises.
Each vibration of the basilar membrane therefore produces one depolarising swing and one hyperpolarising swing.
Put simply, the electrical signal rises and falls in step with the sound wave.
Here is where the outer hair cells earn the amplifying role we met earlier.
By shortening when depolarised and lengthening when hyperpolarised, they feed energy back into the basilar membrane, and its vibration grows larger.

## Transduction at the hair bundle

Zooming in further, the hairs of one cell pack together into a hair bundle.
Inside the bundle, the stereocilia stand in rows of increasing height, like a staircase.
Each shorter one is tied to the taller one beside it by a fine thread called a tip link.
At the tall edge of the staircase stands the kinocilium, the tallest hair of all.
Movement of the stereocilia opens or closes potassium channels.
Bending towards the tall edge pulls the tip links tight and the channels open, while bending back lets them close.
When the channels open, potassium ions enter the cell and depolarise it.
This can happen because the endolymph bathing the bundle is unusually rich in potassium.
Depolarisation then admits calcium ions at the base of the cell.
In turn, synaptic vesicles release transmitter onto the afferent nerve fibre, and the signal leaves for the brain.
The change in the hair cell itself is a graded receptor potential.
In other words, its size follows how far the bundle bends.
The action potentials, by contrast, arise only in the afferent nerve fibre.

## Summary

To sum up, sound is transduced by mechanics.
The chain runs from the stapes, through the perilymph and the basilar membrane, and ends in shear.
Bending of the stereocilia gates the potassium channels.
Consequently, the hair cell swings between depolarisation and hyperpolarisation with each vibration.
Finally, the inner hair cells pass the resulting signal on towards the cortex.
That completes the path from a vibrating stapes to a signal on its way to the brain.
