## Language and Hemisphere Dominance

Language is a lateralised cortical function, dominated by the left hemisphere.
The other hemisphere, meanwhile, is concerned with spatial and temporal relations.
Within the dominant hemisphere, a small connected network of areas implements both the comprehension and the production of language.
In what follows, we'll look at which hemisphere is dominant, the evidence for that dominance, and the areas that make up the network.

## Hemisphere dominance

In the great majority of people, it is the left hemisphere that is dominant for language.
The other hemisphere, for its part, deals with spatial and temporal relations.
This dominance is not absolute, however.
A small percentage of people, who are typically left-handed, have right hemisphere dominance for language instead.

## Evidence for dominance

So how do we know which side is in charge?
Four lines of evidence establish this dominance.
The first comes from aphasias, which are language disorders that arise from damage to the language areas of the left hemisphere.
The second is imaging and direct recording.
Here, three methods all show language tasks activating areas in the left hemisphere.
They are positron emission tomography, functional magnetic resonance imaging, and the direct recording of electrical activity during surgery.
The third is the Wada procedure, in which one hemisphere is anaesthetised.
Doing so reveals whether the awake hemisphere alone can sustain language.
The fourth comes from split brain patients, in whom the corpus callosum has been severed.
As a result, the two hemispheres can no longer communicate, and each one can be studied independently.

## The language implementation system

Now for the network itself.
Four areas form a connected map, and each one is anchored to a nearby sensory or motor region.
Wernicke's area lies in the posterior part of the superior temporal lobe, just behind the auditory cortex.
It handles the comprehension of auditory and visual information.
Next, the arcuate fasciculus is the fibre tract that connects Wernicke's area with Broca's area.
It carries this understood information forwards into the frontal lobe.
Broca's area itself lies in the frontal lobe, in front of the lower part of the motor cortex, and it plans and coordinates vocalisation.
Finally, the angular gyrus lies in the parietal lobe, above and behind Wernicke's area and just in front of the visual cortex.
Functionally, it converts written words into their verbal form.
It does so by matching the visual symbols from reading to their auditory language representation.
That is why damage to the angular gyrus produces some forms of dyslexia.
Stepping back, there is a reason Wernicke's area sits where it does.
The somatic, auditory and visual interpretative areas all converge on it in the posterosuperior temporal lobe.
This makes it the meeting point at which the senses are interpreted.
Broca's speech area, by contrast, lies forward in the frontal lobe.
It sits beside the prefrontal area, which plans the intended message, and in front of the motor area, which executes it.

## Summary

To sum up, language is dominated by the left hemisphere, while the other hemisphere handles spatial and temporal relations.
A small minority, typically left-handed, show right hemisphere dominance instead.
This picture is supported by aphasias, by imaging and direct recording, by the Wada procedure and by split brain patients.
As for the implementation system, it begins at Wernicke's area, which lies behind the auditory cortex and comprehends auditory and visual information.
From there, the arcuate fasciculus leads to Broca's area, which lies in front of the motor cortex and plans and coordinates vocalisation.
Alongside them, the angular gyrus near the visual cortex is implicated in some forms of dyslexia.
With that, we've seen which hemisphere leads on language and the small network it relies on.
