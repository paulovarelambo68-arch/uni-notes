## Lateral Inhibition and Adaptation

Lateral inhibition and adaptation are two coding strategies of the somatosensory system.
Lateral inhibition enhances the quality of sensory information by suppressing the pathways that flank the most active one.
Adaptation, on the other hand, codes for stimulus duration.
It does so through the way receptor firing declines, or persists, during a sustained stimulus.
We'll work through lateral inhibition first, then adaptation and its two classes of receptor, before a brief summary.

## 1. Lateral inhibition

Picture a localised stimulus, for instance a pin on the skin.
It activates several primary sensory neurons at once, and the response of each one is proportional to stimulus strength.
So the pathway closest to the stimulus fires the most, its immediate neighbours fire less, and distant ones remain at their tonic level.
At the relay, the pathway closest to the stimulus inhibits its neighbours.
In doing so, it pushes the flanking pathways below that tonic level.
The effect is contrast enhancement.
In other words, inhibition of the lateral neurons enhances perception of the stimulus.
That happens because the difference between the strongly active central pathway and its silenced surroundings is exaggerated.
This is how lateral inhibition sharpens the quality of sensory information.

## 2. Adaptation

Now we turn to adaptation, which describes how the receptor potential and the firing rate evolve while a stimulus is maintained.
It is the code for stimulus duration.
Receptors split into two classes by their temporal response, namely rapidly adapting and slowly adapting.

## 3. Rapidly adapting receptors

Let's take the rapidly adapting class first.
Here, the receptor potential decreases in response to a sustained stimulus.
As a result, the afferent produces only an on-off burst of action potentials at the start of stimulation and again at its end, and then falls silent.
For this reason, these receptors convey information on changes in ongoing stimulation, rather than on the steady stimulus itself.
The classic example is the Pacinian corpuscle.

## 4. Slowly adapting receptors

Slowly adapting receptors behave the opposite way.
They keep firing, with sustained activity for as long as the stimulus is present.
This makes them suited to convey information on the spatial attributes of the stimulus, such as its size and shape.
The example given for this class is nociceptors.

## Summary

To sum up, lateral inhibition enhances the quality of sensory information.
The pathway closest to the stimulus inhibits its neighbours, which exaggerates contrast and sharpens perception of the stimulus.
Adaptation, meanwhile, codes for stimulus duration.
Rapidly adapting receptors, like the Pacinian corpuscle, lose their receptor potential during a sustained stimulus.
They fire only an on-off burst of action potentials, signalling changes in ongoing stimulation.
Slowly adapting receptors, like nociceptors, fire throughout the stimulus and signal its spatial attributes, such as size and shape.
With that, you have both coding strategies, one for contrast and one for duration.
