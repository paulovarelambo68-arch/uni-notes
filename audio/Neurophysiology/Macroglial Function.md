## Macroglial Function

Macroglia are the glial cells derived from neural stem cells.
This group includes the astrocytes, the oligodendrocytes, the Schwann cells, the ependymal cells and the radial glia.
Far from being passive support, they build the brain during development, maintain its environment, and actively take part in signalling between neurons.
We'll go through their roles one by one, from simply holding neurons in place all the way to signalling at the synapse.

## 1. Support and insulation

Let's begin with structural support.
Macroglia form the physical framework that holds neurons in place.
On top of that, they insulate axons by wrapping them in myelin.
Schwann cells do this in the peripheral nervous system, while oligodendrocytes do it in the central nervous system.
The myelin isolates the axon and speeds up conduction.

## 2. Blood brain barrier

Next comes the blood brain barrier, the selective wall between the blood and the brain's tissue.
Here, the endothelial cells of brain capillaries are sealed together by tight junctions.
As a result, most substances cannot pass between these cells, and the brain is shielded from toxins and from fluctuations in the blood.
The macroglial contribution comes from the astrocytes.
The terminal swellings of their processes, known as the astrocytic end feet, wrap around the outside of these capillaries.
These end feet are required for both the formation and the maintenance of the barrier.

## 3. Nourishing and protecting neurons

Beyond that, macroglia release growth factors, and these nourish and protect neurons.
The growth factors in question are the neurotrophins.
One example is glial-derived neurotrophic factor, often shortened to G D N F.

## 4. Role in the anatomical development of the brain

Turning to the developing brain, macroglia guide migrating neurons and the outgrowth of axons.
Take neuronal migration first.
Radial glia stretch long processes across the whole thickness of the developing brain.
These run from the ventricular zone, where new neurons are born, right up to the pial surface, forming a scaffold.
Migrating neurons press against these processes and climb along them to reach their final position, for example in the cortex or the hippocampus.
In the cerebellum, the same job is done by Bergmann glia.
Axon guidance is the second part of the story.
A growing axon ends in an exploratory tip called the growth cone.
The growth cone probes its environment with fine processes, reading molecular signals that can be adhesive, attractive or repulsive.
A glial cell beside it can release attractive signals.
The growth cone detects them and extends towards their source.
In this way, glia actively steer axons towards their correct targets.
Without these glial cues, the anatomical wiring of the brain would not form.
Last of all, macroglia are required for synaptogenesis, the formation of synapses.

## 5. Promoting efficient signalling between neurons

Finally, macroglia make synaptic transmission work cleanly, and they manage this in two ways.
The first is clearing neurotransmitter from synapses, glutamate being one example.
Removing transmitter from the cleft terminates the signal and stops it from accumulating around neurons.
The second is responding to neuronal signalling by releasing glial factors.
Glia detect what neurons are doing and answer back chemically, so communication at the synapse is two-way.
Three diffuse modulatory systems matter here, using noradrenaline, acetylcholine and dopamine.
Their projections spread widely through the brain, and glia sense them.
All three share the same design.
A small cluster of neurons in the brainstem or the basal forebrain sends axons that fan out and branch widely across the whole brain.
Rather than carrying precise point-to-point messages, these systems bathe large regions in their transmitter and tune the overall state of the brain.
Glia sense this activity and respond by releasing glial factors.
The molecules they release are called gliotransmitters.
That name reflects the fact that they are the very same molecules neurons use as neurotransmitters.
The main ones are glutamate, gamma-aminobutyric acid and adenosine triphosphate.
The last two are better known as GABA and A T P.
D-serine is another classic example.

## Summary

All in all, macroglia support and insulate neurons, and they form the blood brain barrier.
They nourish neurons with neurotrophins like G D N F.
During development, they guide migrating neurons and growing axons, and they drive synaptogenesis.
They keep synaptic signalling efficient too, by clearing neurotransmitter, glutamate included, and by releasing glial factors in response to neuronal activity.
And that brings our look at macroglial function to a close.
