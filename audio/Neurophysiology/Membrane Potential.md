## Membrane Potential

The membrane potential is the voltage difference across the cell membrane, given as the charge inside the cell relative to the outside.
It is measured directly, with a recording microelectrode inserted into the cytosol and a reference electrode in the extracellular fluid, both connected to a voltmeter.
A typical resting nerve or muscle cell reads about minus seventy millivolts, which means the inside is negative relative to the outside.
This potential exists because ions are distributed unequally across the membrane, and because the membrane's permeability to each ion is selective.
Nerve and muscle cells are electrically excitable, and their electrical current results from the movement of ions across the membrane.
When channels open and ions flow, this voltage changes in a controlled way.
That voltage change is the signal with which neurons communicate, and with which muscles are triggered to contract.
Over the coming sections, we'll see where the resting potential comes from, one factor at a time, and then how equilibrium potentials explain its value.

## Resting membrane potential

First up is the resting membrane potential, the voltage difference across a cell membrane when the cell is at rest.
It arises from three factors working together.
One is an unequal distribution of ions across the membrane, namely sodium, potassium and chloride.
Another is a membrane that is more permeable to potassium than to sodium.
The third is the activity of the sodium-potassium pump.
The result is that the inside of the cell is electrically negative relative to the outside.
Typical values range between minus sixty and minus eighty millivolts.
As for the ions themselves, sodium and chloride are concentrated outside the cell, whereas potassium is concentrated inside.
These differences create two forces acting on every ion.
The first is the concentration gradient, which makes the ion diffuse from where it is abundant to where it is scarce.
The second is the electrical gradient, through which the ion is attracted or repelled by the charge on the other side of the membrane.
The net movement of an ion depends on the balance between these two gradients.

## Greater permeability to K⁺ than Na⁺

Now, at rest the membrane allows potassium to cross far more easily than sodium.
In fact, potassium permeability is roughly sixty times that of sodium.
Measured in centimetres per second, the values are six times ten to the power of minus eleven for potassium, and one times ten to the power of minus twelve for sodium.
Water and chloride, too, are far more permeant.
Potassium leaks out of the cell down its concentration gradient, while sodium barely leaks in, so positive charge steadily leaves the cell.
As a consequence, the interior becomes negative.

## Large anions inside the cell

Meanwhile, the intracellular fluid contains large, negatively charged proteins that cannot cross the membrane at all.
These trapped anions add to the negative charge inside the cell.
They also help to hold potassium within, reinforcing the negative resting potential.

## The Na⁺/K⁺ electrogenic pump

Next, we come to the sodium-potassium electrogenic pump, a transporter that consumes adenosine triphosphate.
In each cycle, it moves three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions into it.
The pump has two roles.
For one thing, it maintains the concentration gradients, by continuously restoring the sodium and potassium that leak across the membrane.
For another, it exports three positive charges for every two it imports, and that makes it electrogenic.
In other words, it directly contributes a small net negative charge to the cell interior.

## Inside negative relative to outside

Taken together, three effects leave more positive charge outside the cell than inside.
They are potassium leaving down its gradient, the impermeant anions trapped within the cell, and the electrogenic pump.
The interior of the resting cell is therefore negative relative to the exterior.
And that is exactly what the resting membrane potential measures.

## Equilibrium potential

That leads us to the equilibrium potential.
For any single ion, there is one membrane voltage at which the electrical gradient pulling the ion in exactly balances the concentration gradient pushing it out.
At that voltage, net movement of the ion stops, and this voltage is known as the ion's equilibrium potential.
Equilibrium potentials are calculated with the Nernst equation.
It takes the ion's charge and its concentrations inside and outside the cell, and returns the voltage at which the two gradients cancel.
The resting potential, at around minus sixty to minus eighty millivolts, sits close to the potassium equilibrium potential.
The reason is that the membrane is most permeable to potassium at rest.
When sodium permeability suddenly rises during an action potential, however, the membrane potential shifts towards the sodium equilibrium potential, which is positive fifty millivolts.
This largely explains the value of the action potential voltage.
Here's a key principle for the exam.
The resting membrane potential is always closest to the equilibrium potential of the ion to which the membrane is most permeable, and at rest, that ion is potassium.

## Summary

So, to sum up, the resting membrane potential is about minus sixty to minus eighty millivolts, with the inside negative.
Several things combine to produce it.
Sodium, potassium and chloride are unevenly distributed, and the membrane leaks potassium far more readily than sodium.
On top of that, impermeant anions are trapped inside the cell.
Finally, the electrogenic sodium-potassium pump, moving three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in, maintains the gradients while adding a small negative charge of its own.
Hold those four pieces together, and the negative resting potential makes sense.
