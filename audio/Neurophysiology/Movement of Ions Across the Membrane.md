## Movement of Ions Across the Membrane

Ions have three ways of getting across the cell membrane.
These are passive diffusion through ion channels, facilitated diffusion, and active transport.
For each one, we'll see how it works, how fast it goes, whether it costs energy and what it's for, before comparing all three.

## Passive diffusion through ion channels

Let's begin with passive diffusion through ion channels.
Here, ions flow through the pore of an open channel, strictly down their electrochemical gradient.
That gradient is the combined pull of the concentration gradient and the electrical gradient.
At no point is any energy consumed.
The channel's gate simply opens in response to voltage, a ligand or mechanical stretch, and ions flood through at millions per second.
The purpose of this route is rapid signalling.
It's what generates the electrical currents underlying action potentials and synaptic potentials.
The same route produces the resting potassium leak, which sets the resting membrane potential.

## Facilitated diffusion

Next comes facilitated diffusion.
Like the channel route, it is passive and runs down the gradient, with no adenosine triphosphate, or A T P, involved.
This time, though, the ion or solute crosses via a carrier protein rather than an open pore.
The carrier binds the molecule on one side, changes shape, and releases it on the other.
Because of that shape change on every cycle, it's far slower than a channel.
What's more, it can be saturated when all the carriers are occupied.
Its purpose is to let charged or polar substances cross the membrane down their gradient in a controlled, selective way.
It fills this role when a simple pore isn't the route used.

## Active transport

Now for active transport, the only mechanism that moves ions against their electrochemical gradient.
It's also the only one that costs energy.
In primary active transport, a pump burns A T P directly.
The sodium-potassium adenosine triphosphatase works this way, ejecting three sodium ions and importing two potassium ions per cycle.
In secondary active transport, by contrast, the downhill flow of one ion is harnessed to drag another one uphill.
That happens in the sodium-calcium exchanger, where three sodium ions entering the cell drive one calcium ion out.
Per protein, active transport is the slowest of the three, yet it runs continuously.
Its purpose is to build and maintain the ion gradients, so that sodium and calcium stay low inside the cell and potassium stays high inside.
It restores those gradients after activity, too.
In this way, it makes all passive signalling possible in the first place.

## Main differences

So, how do the three compare?
Channels and facilitated diffusion both dissipate gradients passively.
The only difference between them is the protein used, an open pore in one case and a carrier that changes shape in the other.
That's why channels are fast and carriers are slower.
Active transport, on the other hand, is the slow, energy-dependent builder of those very same gradients.
And that's how ions move across the membrane, with two routes that run gradients down and one that pays to build them.
