## Narcolepsy and Sleep Disorders

Sleep disorders are disturbances of the normal sleep cycle.
A substantial fraction of the population experiences one at some point in life, and they range from the merely annoying to the life threatening.
The most significant are insomnia, the parasomnias and narcolepsy.
Narcolepsy is a chronic disorder in which the loss of orexin signalling, also known as hypocretin signalling, lets sleep intrude into the waking day.
We'll look first at how common these disorders are, then meet orexin, and finally see what causes narcolepsy and how it presents.

## Prevalence

Insomnia is the inability to sleep for long enough, or deeply enough, to produce a subjective sense of refreshment.
It is by far the most common sleep disorder, affecting about fifteen per cent of the population.
Next come the parasomnias, which are abnormal behaviours that intrude into sleep.
Together, they account for roughly ten per cent.
Within this group, excessive sleepiness affects about two per cent, and somnambulism, or sleepwalking, two point five per cent.
Sleep talking affects six per cent and bedwetting three per cent.
Narcolepsy, meanwhile, is rare, at only zero point zero five per cent.
The remaining parasomnias are confusional arousals, night terror attacks and incubus, along with rapid eye movement behaviour disorder, or REM behaviour disorder.
Last on the list is sleep paralysis, in which the muscle atonia of REM sleep persists into wakefulness, leaving the sleeper conscious yet unable to move.

## Orexin and hypocretin

Now for the peptide behind narcolepsy.
Orexin, also called hypocretin, is a peptide released by hypothalamic neurones.
Their axons terminate in areas such as the brainstem and the cerebrum.
In the brainstem, orexin regulates cholinergic and aminergic neurones.
Through these targets, it plays a role in sleep and wakefulness, in vigilance and in hunger.
It promotes wakefulness and, in parallel, it increases food intake.
Consistent with this, levels of hypocretin, or orexin, are reduced in the cerebrospinal fluid of human narcoleptics.
That finding links the symptoms directly to the loss of this peptide.

## Causes of narcolepsy

Why, then, does orexin signalling fail?
The answer differs between species.
In dogs, the disorder is genetic, produced by a mutation of the orexin receptor.
So the peptide is present, but it cannot act.
In humans, by contrast, narcolepsy is an autoimmune disease in which the orexin releasing neurones are destroyed.
This explains why the peptide itself is reduced.

## Symptoms of narcolepsy

That brings us to the symptoms.
Narcolepsy shows itself as irresistible sleep episodes and excessive daytime sleepiness.
Alongside these comes cataplexy, a sudden loss of muscle control while awake.
Sleep, too, takes an abnormal shape.
REM sleep begins rapidly, entered directly from wakefulness, and dreams are very lucid.
These features can be framed as a possible imbalance of cholinergic and aminergic activity in the brainstem.
Without orexin regulation, the switch that normally separates wakefulness from REM sleep becomes unstable.
As a result, sleep, with its dreams and its loss of muscle tone, invades waking life.

## Summary

To sum up, sleep disorders are widespread.
Insomnia, the inability to sleep enough to feel refreshed, leads at fifteen per cent.
The parasomnias follow at about ten per cent, while narcolepsy is rare at zero point zero five per cent.
Narcolepsy arises from failed orexin signalling, which in humans comes from an autoimmune destruction of the orexin neurones.
Losing this hypothalamic input unbalances cholinergic and aminergic activity in the brainstem.
The result is irresistible sleep episodes, daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, rapid REM onset and very lucid dreams.
Those are the main sleep disorders, with common insomnia at one end and rare narcolepsy at the other.
