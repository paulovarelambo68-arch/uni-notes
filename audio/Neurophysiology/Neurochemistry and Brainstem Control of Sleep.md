## Neurochemistry and Brainstem Control of Sleep

Sleep is governed by the chemistry and the circuitry of the brainstem.
This control works through three mechanisms.
First, sleep-promoting and wake-promoting biochemicals gate the onset of slow wave sleep.
Second, the reticular formation controls arousal.
And third, a switch between brainstem nuclei governs the alternation between slow wave sleep and rapid eye movement sleep, or REM sleep.
We'll go through them in that order, ending with the bursts of activity that drive REM sleep.

## Onset of SWS

The first question is what triggers the onset of slow wave sleep, and it remains unresolved.
A sleep-inducing peptide is suspected.
That would be a substance produced within the body itself that actively promotes sleep.
Many biochemicals are implicated, and many of them have an immune role.
Among them, two increase sleep.
One is gamma-aminobutyric acid, or GABA, the main inhibitory neurotransmitter of the brain.
The other is adenosine, a byproduct of cellular energy use.
Histamine, on the other hand, decreases sleep.
It is a monoamine that is also active in immune responses.

## The reticular formation

Now, the reticular formation is a network of neurones in the brainstem.
It can influence arousal through the Reticular Activating System.
The two ends of the brainstem have opposing roles.
The rostral, or upper, brainstem is necessary for wakefulness, while the caudal, or lower, brainstem is necessary for sleep.
The fibres of the reticular formation run in both directions.
Ascending fibres innervate the cerebrum, including the hypothalamus, the thalamus and the forebrain.
Descending fibres, meanwhile, innervate the body, such as skeletal muscle.
The network itself lies in the core of the brainstem.
Sensory input converges on it from below.
This input arrives through the ascending general sensory tracts, which carry touch, pain and temperature, and it is joined by visual and auditory impulses.
The network's output leaves in both directions.
Radiations fan upwards to widespread areas of the cerebral cortex, whereas descending motor projections run down to the spinal cord.
In other words, the reticular formation lies between the sensory inflow and its two targets, the cerebrum above and the body below.
Back at the upper end, stimulating the rostral brainstem can wake a person, even under anaesthesia.
The reason is that its ascending drive to the thalamus and forebrain restores arousal.

## The switch between SWS and REM

Turning to the two kinds of sleep, the transition between slow wave sleep and REM sleep behaves as an on-off switch between brainstem nuclei.
A nucleus, in this sense, is a cluster of neurones with a shared function.
One side of the switch is cholinergic, which means its neurones release the neurotransmitter acetylcholine.
Acetylcholine induces REM sleep, and so these cholinergic neurones are the REM-on cells, active during REM.
They sit in two nuclei of the tegmentum, the core region of the brainstem.
These are the laterodorsal tegmental nucleus and the pedunculopontine tegmental nucleus.
The other side of the switch is aminergic, meaning its neurones release monoamine neurotransmitters.
It includes the dorsal raphe nucleus, a midline brainstem nucleus that releases serotonin.
It also includes the locus ceruleus, a small brainstem nucleus that releases noradrenaline.
These aminergic neurones are the REM-off cells.
They are quiescent in REM and active in slow wave sleep, though less so than in wakefulness.

## The neurochemistry of REM

As for REM sleep itself, it appears to be driven by phasic bursts of activity.
These are brief surges of neuronal firing, rather than a steady rhythm.
The clearest marker of these bursts is the ponto-geniculo-occipital spike, or P G O spike, a sharp electrical signal.
It begins in the pons, a region of the brainstem, and more precisely in its dorsolateral part.
That part is the nucleus reticularis pontis oralis of the reticular formation.
From there, the spikes propagate rostrally, towards the front of the brain.
They arrive first at the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, which is the visual relay station.
Then they reach the occipital cortex, the visual region at the back of the brain.
Pons, geniculate and occipital are the three stations that give the spike its name.
The spikes correlate with the onset of the rapid eye movements and the other changes of REM sleep.

## Summary

Pulling the threads together, sleep onset is chemical.
GABA and adenosine promote sleep, and histamine opposes it.
Arousal, by contrast, is anatomical.
The rostral brainstem sustains wakefulness, and the caudal brainstem enables sleep, through the ascending and descending fibres of the reticular formation.
Slow wave sleep and REM sleep alternate through an on-off switch.
On one side are the cholinergic REM-on cells, set against the aminergic REM-off cells on the other.
Finally, REM itself is driven by phasic P G O spikes.
They run from the dorsolateral pons through the lateral geniculate nucleus to the occipital cortex.
Those are the brainstem's controls on sleep, from its chemistry to its circuitry.
