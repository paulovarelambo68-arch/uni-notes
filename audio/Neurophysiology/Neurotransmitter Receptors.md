## Neurotransmitter Receptors

Once a neurotransmitter has crossed the synaptic cleft, it binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
Each of these receptors falls into one of two types, ionotropic or metabotropic.
In what follows, we'll look at how each type works, and after that at why both of them exist.

## Ionotropic Receptors

Ionotropic receptors come first.
Each one is a single membrane protein, made of several protein subunits arranged around a central pore.
The transmitter binding site sits on the outer face of the same protein that forms the channel.
In other words, the receptor and the channel are one molecule.
Here's how it works.
When the transmitter binds, the subunits change shape and the pore opens.
Ions then flow straight through, down their electrochemical gradients, with no intermediate steps.
The channel stays open only while the transmitter is bound, and transmitter is cleared from the cleft quickly.
The current therefore lasts milliseconds.
Now for the standard examples.
One is the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor.
Two more are glutamate receptors, namely AMPA and NMDA.
Their names stand for alpha-amino-three-hydroxy-five-methyl-four-isoxazolepropionic acid and N-methyl-D-aspartate.
Last of all come the GABA type A and glycine receptors, GABA being short for gamma-aminobutyric acid.

## Metabotropic Receptors

Metabotropic receptors work differently, since here the receptor and the ion channel are separate proteins.
The receptor acts through a G protein, a three part switch with alpha, beta and gamma subunits, sitting on the inner face of the membrane.
Let's follow the mechanism step by step.
It begins when the transmitter binds the receptor on the outside.
The activated receptor then switches on the G protein, and the alpha subunit separates from beta and gamma and moves along the membrane.
Next, the alpha subunit activates an effector protein, which is an enzyme.
This effector generates intracellular messengers, also called second messengers.
Finally, the messengers act on the ion channel from the inside, opening or closing it, and ions flow.
This indirect design has three consequences.
The first is slow activation.
Several protein to protein steps take place before any ion moves, which is why the response takes from tens of milliseconds to seconds to begin.
The second is a long duration of action.
The messengers persist inside the cell after the transmitter has left the receptor, and as a result the effect lasts seconds to minutes.
The third is amplification.
One receptor can activate many G proteins, and each effector makes many messenger molecules.
That way, the signal is magnified and can spread through the whole cell.

## Why both exist?

So why do both types exist?
Ionotropic receptors give speed and precision.
They convert the presynaptic signal into a postsynaptic potential almost instantly, and they are ideal for rapid, point to point transmission.
Metabotropic receptors, on the other hand, trade speed for reach.
They are slow and long lasting.
Because they work through diffusible messengers, they tune the overall excitability and chemistry of the cell, rather than simply firing it.
To sum up, there are two kinds of neurotransmitter receptor, one built for speed and the other for reach.
