## Nociceptors and Inflammatory Mediators

Nociceptors are the free nerve endings that detect noxious, potentially tissue-damaging stimuli, and they give rise to pain.
They fall into two classes, told apart by their axons and by which stimuli they respond to.
About twenty per cent are A-delta nociceptors, with small-diameter myelinated fibres that respond to mechanical stimuli and tissue injury.
The rest, about eighty per cent, are C nociceptors, with small-diameter unmyelinated fibres that are polymodal, responding to mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli.
Their endings carry ion channel receptors tuned to each type of stimulus.
On top of that, their sensitivity is raised by inflammatory mediators, which reduce the pain threshold of the nerve.
We'll meet the two classes first, then the channels and firing at the ending, and lastly the mediators of inflammation.

## 1. Aδ nociceptors

Let's begin with the A-delta nociceptors, which make up about twenty per cent of nociceptors.
They respond to mechanical stimuli and to tissue injury.
Their axons are small-diameter myelinated fibres, so they conduct relatively fast.
The pain they produce is sharp, prickling, bright and well localised.
It's the immediate "ooch" that comes with withdrawal from the stimulus.

## 2. C nociceptors

Next come the C nociceptors, which make up the remaining eighty per cent.
They are polymodal, meaning a single ending responds to mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli.
Their axons are small-diameter unmyelinated fibres, the slowest of the sensory afferents.
The pain they carry is slow, burning, aching and diffuse.
Think of it as the lingering second pain that follows the sharp first impression.
The classic demonstration of this polymodality is capsaicin, the irritant of hot peppers.
Capsaicin activates polymodal C nociceptors through a channel called transient receptor potential vanilloid one, or T R P V one.
Acid and tissue heating open that very same channel.
That's why chilli is perceived as burning heat rather than as a taste.

## 3. Ion channel receptors and firing

Zooming in, the free nerve endings of nociceptors express ion channel receptors.
These transduce each class of noxious stimulus into a graded potential.
When a noxious stimulus opens the channels, sodium and calcium ions flow into the ending and depolarise it.
If this depolarisation reaches threshold, the nociceptor's voltage-gated sodium channels, Nav one point eight and Nav one point nine, drive action potentials that travel along the axon.
Firing frequency rises with stimulus intensity, and that is how the strength of the stimulus is coded.
In other words, the stronger the noxious stimulus, the higher the rate of action potentials sent to the spinal cord.
Now, a quick word for the exam.
The individual names of all these pain ion channels don't need to be memorised.
What matters is that each stimulus type has its own channels, and that opening them depolarises the ending.
It also matters that Nav one point eight and Nav one point nine drive the action potentials.

## 4. Inflammatory mediators

That leaves the inflammatory mediators.
Tissue injury sets off a self-reinforcing cascade around the nociceptor ending, and the mediators involved act to reduce the pain threshold of the nerve.
Damaged cells release their intracellular contents, including potassium, serotonin and bradykinin, which activate the nociceptor directly.
The activated ending in turn releases substance P, which feeds back onto two targets.
It stimulates mast cells to degranulate and release histamine.
It also dilates nearby blood vessels, producing the redness and swelling of inflammation.
Histamine adds further activation of the nociceptor, closing the loop.
Meanwhile, the membranes of the injured cells supply the sensitising arm of the process.
Phospholipase converts membrane phospholipids into arachidonic acid, which is then taken down two enzymatic paths.
Along one, cyclooxygenase produces prostaglandins, and along the other, lipoxygenase produces leukotrienes.
Unlike the mediators we've just met, prostaglandins and leukotrienes don't activate the nociceptor themselves.
Instead, they sensitise it.
They lower its threshold, so that stimuli that would normally be subthreshold now produce pain.
This is why cyclooxygenase inhibitors, known as COX inhibitors, work as analgesics.
By blocking cyclooxygenase, they cut prostaglandin synthesis and remove the sensitisation.

## Summary

Pulling the threads together, nociceptors are free nerve endings for noxious stimuli, and they come in two classes.
A-delta nociceptors are twenty per cent of the total, with small myelinated fibres.
They respond to mechanical stimuli and tissue injury, giving sharp, prickling, well localised pain with withdrawal.
C nociceptors are the other eighty per cent, with small unmyelinated polymodal fibres that respond to mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli.
Their pain is slow, burning, aching and diffuse, and the same ending is activated by capsaicin, acid and heat through T R P V one.
The endings carry ion channel receptors matched to each stimulus.
Heat acts on T R P V one, two and three, as well as on TREK one.
Chemical stimuli such as acid act on T R P V one and on the acid-sensing ion channel, or A S I C.
A third channel for chemical stimuli is the dorsal root acid-sensing ion channel, or D R A S I C.
For mechanical stimuli, the channels are the mammalian degenerin channel, or M D E G, alongside D R A S I C and TREK one.
Cold, finally, acts on transient receptor potential melastatin eight, or T R P M eight.
Opening these channels admits sodium and calcium, depolarising the ending until Nav one point eight and Nav one point nine fire action potentials.
The frequency of those action potentials codes stimulus intensity.
On the inflammatory side, the pain threshold is lowered through two arms.
In the first, damaged cells release potassium, serotonin and bradykinin, which activate the nociceptor.
Substance P from the ending drives mast cells to release histamine, and it dilates blood vessels too.
In the second arm, cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase turn arachidonic acid into prostaglandins and leukotrienes.
These sensitise the ending without activating it.
That sensitisation is the step COX inhibitors block, which is how they act as analgesics.
And with that, we've followed the nociceptor from the sharp first pain to the inflammation that lowers its threshold.
