## Pain Experience and Descending Modulation

The pain experience is not a direct readout of the nociceptive input.
The signal from injury is amplified by sensitisation, and it is modulated at spinal level.
It is then interpreted by a brain whose genetics, context, mood and cognitive set all shape what is finally felt.
We'll work through three parts, namely the factors that shape the pain experience, the way stress modulates pain, and the modulation of pain in the spinal cord.

## 1. Factors shaping the pain experience

Our starting point is the nociceptive input, which reaches the spinal cord through the A-delta and C fibres of the nociceptors.
On top of that, injury adds peripheral and central sensitisation, which amplify the nociceptive input before it ascends.
This amplified input then undergoes nociceptive modulation in the cord.
Only the result travels up to the brain, where the pain experience itself is generated.
That experience is shaped by several classes of factors.
Genetics sets the baseline sensitivity of the system.
Context, that is pain beliefs, expectation and placebo, changes how the same signal is judged.
Mood, particularly depression, anxiety and catastrophising, colours how intense and unpleasant the experience becomes.
Cognitive set, which covers hypervigilance, attention and distraction, determines how much the signal is attended to.
Finally, chemical and structural changes in the brain itself alter the experience directly, because the brain is the organ that generates it.
One such change is neurodegeneration, and another is altered metabolism in the opioidergic and dopaminergic systems.
A third is maladaptive plasticity, meaning lasting changes in how the pain circuitry is wired.
Central sensitisation is worth a closer look.
The nociceptive terminal releases glutamate, substance P and brain-derived neurotrophic factor.
These act on receptors of the dorsal horn neuron and switch on intracellular cascades involving calcium and kinases.
The cascades leave the neuron more excitable, so the same input now produces a larger signal.

## 2. Stress modulation of pain

Turning now to stress, the kind meant here is psychological stress.
The brain processes it as cognitive and emotional states, and these act on pain through the descending system.
The direction of the effect depends on duration.
Acute stress decreases pain through descending inhibition.
In an immediately threatening or demanding situation, like combat or a sports injury, the brain engages descending pathways that suppress nociceptive transmission in the cord.
As a result, pain is dampened while the situation is dealt with.
Chronic stress, on the other hand, increases pain through descending facilitation.

## 3. Spinal modulation of pain

Down in the spinal cord, there are interneurons that release inhibitory transmitters which may be antinociceptive.
These transmitters are endogenous opioids, endogenous cannabinoids, noradrenaline and serotonin, and gamma-aminobutyric acid, or GABA.
All of these neurons show tonic activity.
In other words, the cord keeps a constant background level of inhibition over nociceptive transmission, rather than engaging only when pain arrives.
This inhibition is controlled from two directions.
One is modulation by the descending pain pathway from the brain above.
The other is incoming non-nociceptive transmission from the periphery.
By periphery, we mean the body tissues outside the central nervous system.
In effect, this second route is input arriving from the receptors of the body rather than from the brain.
That route explains why touch and pressure reduce pain.
The mechanoreceptor fibres carrying them enter the same region of the cord and activate the inhibitory interneurons.
These interneurons suppress the nociceptive signal at its first synapse in the dorsal horn, before it can ascend.
Modulation can also be excitatory, however.
In the balance of the pain gate, C fibre activity facilitates activation of the ascending pain pathway instead of dampening it.
As for the projection neuron, the nociceptor excites it through substance P.
Meanwhile, GABA and enkephalin from interneurons, along with noradrenaline from the descending pathway, inhibit it.
These inhibitory inputs act on both the nociceptor terminal and the projection neuron, before the signal ascends to the brain.

## Summary

Stepping back for a moment, the pain experience is shaped by more than the nociceptive input.
The A-delta and C fibre signal, amplified by sensitisation, is modulated in the cord and interpreted by the brain.
The brain, in turn, is influenced by genetics, context, mood, cognitive set and its own chemical and structural state.
Psychological stress acts through the descending system.
Acute stress decreases pain by descending inhibition, while chronic stress increases it by descending facilitation.
In the cord, tonically active interneurons release antinociceptive transmitters, namely endogenous opioids, endogenous cannabinoids, noradrenaline, serotonin and GABA.
Their inhibition is controlled by the descending pathway and by incoming non-nociceptive input.
Lastly, modulation can facilitate transmission as well as inhibit it.
That completes the path from the nociceptive input to the pain that is actually felt.
