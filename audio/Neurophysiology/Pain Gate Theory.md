## Pain Gate Theory

Pain gate theory explains how the transmission of pain is controlled in the dorsal horn, before it reaches the brain.
We'll first locate the gate, then work through the circuit behind it, and see how rubbing, scratching and several therapies make use of it.

## 1. The gate in the substantia gelatinosa

To set the scene, nociceptive afferents synapse in the dorsal horn onto projection neurons.
The axons of these projection neurons ascend in the lateral spinothalamic tract.
The substantia gelatinosa, also known as lamina two, is where this transmission is controlled.
It works as a gate.
C fibre activity opens the gate, whereas A-beta fibre activity closes it.
The balance between the two, mediated by interneurons in the dorsal horn, sets what reaches the brain.

## 2. The circuit

So what does the circuit behind the gate look like?
It is built around an inhibitory local circuit neuron, or interneuron, which sits between the two afferent inputs and the dorsal horn projection neuron.
The C fibre coming from the nociceptor does two things at once.
On the one hand, it excites the projection neuron directly, driving the pain pathway.
On the other, it inhibits the inhibitory interneuron, which removes the brake on the projection neuron.
Both of these actions push the gate open.
The A-beta fibre from the mechanoreceptor does the opposite.
It excites the inhibitory interneuron, and that interneuron then inhibits the projection neuron.
This dampens transmission into the anterolateral system and closes the gate.
In other words, the output of the projection neuron reflects whichever input is dominating.
That is either nociceptive drive from the C fibre or mechanoreceptive drive from the A-beta fibre.

## 3. Everyday and clinical correlates

Turning to everyday life, the theory explains why rubbing or scratching a painful site relieves pain.
These manoeuvres recruit large, myelinated A-beta mechanoreceptor afferents.
Their activity closes the gate and competes with the C fibre signal.
The same principle is put to clinical use in transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, or TENS.
Here, electrodes on the skin deliver electrical pulses that selectively activate the large A-beta fibres.
That closes the gate and reduces the pain reaching higher centres.
Manual therapy relies on this mechanism too.
The pressure, stretch and movement applied to skin and soft tissue drive A-beta mechanoreceptor input into the dorsal horn.
This excites the inhibitory interneuron and suppresses the projection neuron.
Dry needling, likewise, recruits large afferent input.
The needle, once inserted into the muscle, stimulates local mechanoreceptive afferents.
Their activity competes with nociceptive C fibre transmission at the gate.
In doing so, it adds to the inhibitory drive that keeps the gate closed.

## Summary

To sum up, the substantia gelatinosa, or lamina two, acts as a gate on pain transmission.
C fibres open the gate by exciting the dorsal horn projection neuron directly.
At the same time, they inhibit the inhibitory interneuron that normally restrains the projection neuron.
Through both actions, they facilitate activation of the lateral spinothalamic tract.
A-beta fibres close the gate by exciting that same inhibitory interneuron.
The interneuron in turn suppresses the projection neuron and inhibits activation of the tract.
The perceived intensity of pain reflects the balance between these two inputs, a balance set by interneurons in the dorsal horn.
This is why rubbing, scratching and TENS reduce pain, since all of them drive A-beta mechanoreceptor fibres.
That balance between the two kinds of fibre is the heart of pain gate theory.
