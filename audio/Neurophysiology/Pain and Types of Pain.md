## Pain and Types of Pain

The International Association for the Study of Pain defines pain as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with potential or actual tissue damage, or described in terms of such damage.
Pain is therefore not just a sensation but also an emotional experience.
What's more, actual tissue damage is not required for pain to be felt.
We'll first separate nociception from pain, then look at acute somatic and acute visceral pain, and end with chronic pain.

## Nociception versus pain

Let's begin with nociception, which is the process of transmitting a painful stimulus.
It is the neural activity that carries the signal from the site of injury up towards the central nervous system.
It must not be confused with pain itself, because pain is in the brain.
In other words, the unpleasant sensory and emotional experience only arises once the brain processes the incoming signal.
Transmission of the stimulus and the experience of pain are two distinct steps.
Traced from start to finish, the route of a pain signal begins with a noxious stimulus at the skin, which activates a sensory, or afferent, neuron.
This neuron's cell body sits in the dorsal root ganglion.
Its signal is relayed across a synapse into the dorsal horn of the spinal cord.
From there, second order neurons ascend to the thalamus and on to the somatosensory cortex, which is where the pain is actually experienced.
Running the other way, descending pathways from the midbrain and medulla travel back down to the spinal cord and modulate the incoming pain signal.
Alongside these, a motor, or efferent, neuron leaves the cord towards muscle.

## Acute somatic pain

Now for acute pain, which is divided into two kinds, somatic and visceral.
Acute somatic pain arises either from superficial structures, such as the skin, or from deep structures, such as connective tissue and bone.
It is well localised, it plays a protective role, and it promotes healing.
Put simply, the pain warns us of tissue damage and forces us to guard and rest the injured part.
That way, the part is protected from further harm while it repairs.

## Acute visceral pain

Turning to the other kind, acute visceral pain arises from the internal organs.
It is triggered either by distension of an organ, as in colic, or by inflammation, as in appendicitis.
Unlike somatic pain, it is poorly localised.
The reason is that very few dorsal horn neurons in the spinal cord are dedicated to visceral pain alone.
Instead, visceral and cutaneous afferents converge onto the same cells.
This convergence explains the poor localisation, and it is also why the pain can be referred to the skin.
Indeed, the pain can even be felt at a somatic site, which is the basis of referred pain.

## Chronic pain

That leaves chronic pain, which is long term, debilitating pain accompanied by altered pain perception.
Two examples illustrate it, rheumatoid arthritis and phantom limb pain.
Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune inflammation of the joints that produces persistent joint pain.
In phantom limb pain, a person who has lost a limb continues to feel it, and experiences pain as if it were coming from the missing limb.
Because C fibres carry slow, dull, persistent pain, chronic pain conditions are thought to involve ongoing overactivity of C fibre input.
This idea is consistent with altered pain perception and with sensitisation.

## Summary

In summary, pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with potential or actual tissue damage, or described in terms of such damage.
Nociception, the process of transmitting a painful stimulus, is distinct from pain itself, which is experienced in the brain.
Acute somatic pain, whether superficial or deep, is well localised and protective, and it promotes healing.
Acute visceral pain arises from distension or inflammation of internal organs, and it is poorly localised.
Chronic pain is long term and debilitating, with altered pain perception, as in rheumatoid arthritis and phantom limb pain.
Remember, then, that nociception carries the signal, but the pain itself happens in the brain.
