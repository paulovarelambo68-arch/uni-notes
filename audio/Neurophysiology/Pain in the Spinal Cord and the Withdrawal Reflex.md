## Pain in the Spinal Cord and the Withdrawal Reflex

Pain afferents synapse in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord.
The grey matter there is organised into layers that receive different fibre types.
One of these layers, lamina two, also known as the substantia gelatinosa, modulates the nociceptive signal before it is relayed to the brain.
Nociceptive input drives something else as well, namely the flexion reflex, which is otherwise called the withdrawal reflex.
This is a spinal circuit that pulls the limb away from a painful stimulus.
In what follows, we'll see where the pain afferents end, what lamina two does with their signal, and how the withdrawal reflex is wired.

## 1. Where pain afferents terminate

First, a word on the layout of the spinal cord.
Its grey matter is divided into ten layers, known as Rexed's laminae, on the basis of its cell architecture.
Laminae one to six make up the dorsal horn.
The nociceptive afferents do not synapse at random within the dorsal horn.
Instead, each fibre class terminates in its own characteristic laminae.
It's there that each one contacts the second order neurons of the pain pathway.
As a clinical aside, and one that goes beyond the slide content, consider an epidural anaesthetic.
It bathes the spinal nerve roots and blocks conduction in the primary afferents.
Because of this, nociceptive signals never reach the dorsal horn, and the second order projection neurons are never activated.
Pain transmission below the block is therefore abolished.

## 2. Lamina II: the substantia gelatinosa

Now for lamina two, which corresponds to the substantia gelatinosa.
This layer is composed almost entirely of interneurons, and it is important in the modulation of pain.
The second order neurons, however, do not sit here.
They are the projection neurons of laminae one and five, and their axons cross the midline and ascend in the spinothalamic tracts.
So the C fibres that terminate in lamina two synapse onto interneurons.
These interneurons pass the signal, now modulated, on to the projection neurons.
A-delta fibres, by contrast, end in laminae one and five, which means they can contact the projection neurons directly.
At these synapses, the afferent terminals release excitatory neurotransmitters, principally glutamate and substance P.
These transmitters generate excitatory postsynaptic potentials in the postsynaptic membrane.
When those potentials summate to threshold, they trigger action potentials in the second order neuron, and the signal is carried onward.
Taken together, lamina two is not a relay that simply amplifies the signal on its way up.
Its interneurons regulate how much of the incoming nociceptive signal is passed on to the projection neurons.
What's more, this modulation is not constant but conditional.
That's because the output depends on what other inputs are active at the same time.
As a result, the same nociceptive stimulus can be transmitted with different strength under different circumstances.

## 3. The flexion (withdrawal) reflex

That brings us to the flexion reflex, or withdrawal reflex, which produces withdrawal from a painful stimulus.
In this reflex, A-delta activation leads to the activation of both excitatory and inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord.
The excitatory interneurons drive the motor neurons that supply the agonist muscle.
Meanwhile, the inhibitory interneurons silence the antagonist.
The outcome is contraction of the agonist muscle and withdrawal from the stimulus.
Importantly, the reflex is organised at spinal level.
The whole circuit, made up of the afferent, the interneurons and the motor neurons, is completed within the cord.
Consequently, the withdrawal does not depend on the brain, or on consciously feeling the pain.
Even so, the ascending signal travels to the brain in parallel, and the pain is consciously perceived shortly afterwards.

## Summary

To sum up, the grey matter of the dorsal horn is divided into the Rexed laminae, with laminae one to six lying dorsally.
Pain afferents terminate there in a fibre-specific pattern.
A-delta fibres end in laminae one and five, while C fibres end in laminae one and two.
Lamina two is the substantia gelatinosa.
There, afferent terminals release glutamate and substance P, generating excitatory postsynaptic potentials and action potentials in the second order neurons.
The interneurons of lamina two modulate, rather than simply amplify, how much of the signal is relayed onward, depending on concurrent activity.
Finally, the A-delta input drives the flexion, or withdrawal, reflex.
Its excitatory and inhibitory interneurons contract the agonist and silence the antagonist.
That circuit is completed entirely within the cord, independent of the brain and of conscious perception.
And with that, we've followed pain from its arrival in the dorsal horn to the reflex that pulls the limb away.
