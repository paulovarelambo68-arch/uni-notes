## Photopigments and Colour Vision

A photopigment is the molecule in a photoreceptor that absorbs light.
In rods, the pigment is rhodopsin, which is made of an opsin protein bound to retinal.
Two things underlie both the electrical response to light and colour vision.
They are the photoactivation of this pigment, and the varied absorption of the different opsin isoforms found in rods and cones.
We'll first take rhodopsin apart and see how light switches it on.
After that, we'll follow the opsins through to colour vision and, at the end, colour blindness.

## Rhodopsin: opsin plus retinal

Rhodopsin is built from two components.
One is opsin, the protein part, and the other is retinal, the part that absorbs light.
Retinal derives from vitamin A.
It rests within the opsin, positioned to capture photons.
The protein, meanwhile, determines which wavelengths the pigment absorbs best.

## The retinal isoforms and photoactivation

Now, retinal itself exists in two isoforms.
In rhodopsin that has not been activated, the retinal is in the eleven-cis configuration, which is a bent form of the molecule.
More precisely, the eleven-cis molecule is kinked at the double bond between carbons eleven and twelve.
Absorbing a photon breaks that bond, and the chain straightens out.
In other words, light converts eleven-cis retinal into all-trans retinal.
This isomerisation is the single structural change that activates the pigment.
With this change, opsin and retinal split.
The pigment then becomes metarhodopsin two, the activated form of rhodopsin.
It is metarhodopsin two that excites the electrical change in the photoreceptor.

## Opsin isoforms in rods and cones

Turning to the opsins, rods and the different cone types contain different isoforms of opsin.
As a result, their pigments have varied absorption characteristics.
Rods carry scotopsin, while cones carry photopsins.
Colour vision depends on three cone photopsins, each of which absorbs light best at a different wavelength.
The S photopsin, where S stands for short, is the blue one, and it absorbs best at four hundred and twenty nanometres.
Next is M, for medium, which is green and peaks at five hundred and forty nanometres.
Last comes L, for long, the red one, which peaks at five hundred and seventy nanometres.
Each cone expresses just one of these.
So the three cone classes sample the short, medium and long wavelength ranges respectively.

## Colour blindness and X linkage

That brings us to colour blindness, which affects eight per cent of males.
Most of those cases are mild enough to go unnoticed without formal testing.
Females are affected far less often, at only zero point five per cent.
The reason for this gap is genetic.
The L and M opsin genes are X-linked, and together they make up ninety to ninety-five per cent of opsins.
Males carry a single X chromosome, so one faulty copy is enough to produce the defect.
A female, by contrast, would need both copies to be affected.
Problems with the M cones account for seventy-five per cent of cases.
In a normal trichromat, the absorption curves of the S, M and L cones overlap across the spectrum, and red flowers stand out vividly against green foliage.
In protanopia, the L curve is missing, leaving only S and M.
In deuteranopia, it is the M curve that is missing, leaving only S and L.
Either way, the red flowers wash out towards a yellowish grey.
Put simply, losing either X-linked opsin causes red and green confusion.

## Summary

Pulling it all together, rhodopsin is opsin plus retinal.
Light activates it by flipping eleven-cis retinal to the all-trans form.
The two components then split, and the resulting metarhodopsin two excites the photoreceptor's electrical change.
Opsin isoforms tune absorption, with scotopsin in rods and the photopsins in cones.
Among those photopsins, S peaks at four hundred and twenty nanometres, M at five hundred and forty, and L at five hundred and seventy.
Because the L and M opsin genes are X-linked and make up ninety to ninety-five per cent of opsins, colour blindness affects eight per cent of males.
Yet only zero point five per cent of females are affected, and M cone problems cause seventy-five per cent of cases.
And that's the whole path, from one flipped molecule of retinal to telling red from green.
