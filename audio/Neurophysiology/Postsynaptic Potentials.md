## Postsynaptic Potentials

A postsynaptic potential, or P S P, is the local, graded change in membrane voltage produced on the postsynaptic cell when neurotransmitter opens ion channels there.
Unlike an action potential, it is not a signal in itself.
Rather, it is a vote, either excitatory or inhibitory, and that vote only matters once all the votes are added up at the axon hillock.
Over the next few parts, we'll meet the excitatory and the inhibitory kinds, and then see how the hillock adds them up to decide whether the cell fires.

## 1. EPSP: excitatory postsynaptic potential

Our first kind is the excitatory postsynaptic potential, or E P S P.
Excitatory neurotransmitters produce depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane.
Ligand-gated channels open, sodium ions flow in, and the membrane moves towards threshold.
An E P S P may favour an action potential, but it is not in itself one, for two reasons.
The first is that there is no propagation.
It is a local potential, which decays with distance from the synapse.
The second is that it gives graded responses.
Its size is proportional to the amount of transmitter released and to the number of channels opened.
It has no threshold and no all-or-none behaviour.
That is exactly why these potentials can be added together.

## 2. IPSP: inhibitory postsynaptic potential

Now for the opposite kind, the inhibitory postsynaptic potential, or I P S P.
Inhibitory neurotransmitters produce hyperpolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane.
Typically, chloride flows into the cell or potassium flows out of it.
This happens through glycine receptors, or through receptors for gamma-aminobutyric acid, known as GABA.
GABA opens chloride channels, and chloride is concentrated outside the cell.
Chloride therefore flows in and makes the inside more negative.
An I P S P makes it more difficult to trigger an action potential, because the membrane is pushed further away from threshold.
As a result, the excitatory input has to work harder to reach it.

## 3. Summation at the axon hillock

That brings us to the place where all these potentials meet, the axon hillock.
The axon hillock is the integration site.
It carries the highest density of voltage-gated sodium channels, so it has the lowest threshold, and it is where the action potential is actually born.
Each synapse lies far from the hillock, and each single P S P is small.
For that reason, one E P S P alone almost never fires the cell.
Instead, the hillock reads the algebraic sum of all the incoming excitatory and inhibitory potentials at that instant.
If the summed depolarisation reaches threshold, an action potential follows.
If it does not, nothing happens.

## 4. Spatial and temporal summation

This adding up can happen in two ways.
In spatial summation, two or more presynaptic inputs at different sites fire at the same time.
Each one alone is too small.
Their currents, however, flow to the hillock simultaneously and add, reaching threshold together.
Temporal summation, by contrast, involves one input firing repeatedly in rapid succession.
A single postsynaptic potential lasts tens of milliseconds.
So if the next E P S P arrives before the previous one has decayed, the second rides on the tail of the first.
In this way, the depolarisation builds up step by step until it reaches threshold.

## Summary

Pulling it all together, postsynaptic potentials are local, graded potentials that do not propagate.
Excitatory ones depolarise the membrane and favour firing, while inhibitory ones hyperpolarise it and oppose firing.
Because they are graded and long-lasting compared with an action potential, they can summate at the axon hillock.
This summation can be spatial, with several inputs at once, or temporal, with one input firing repeatedly.
Only when their sum crosses threshold does the postsynaptic neuron fire.
And that is how a neuron weighs up its votes.
