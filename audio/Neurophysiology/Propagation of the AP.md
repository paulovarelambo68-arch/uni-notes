## Propagation of the AP

How does an action potential make its way along a neuron?
To set the scene, we'll first trace the direction a signal takes through the neuron.
After that, we'll see how local circuit currents carry the action potential forward, and why it travels only one way.
Finally, we'll turn to saltatory conduction in myelinated axons, and to the myelin that makes it possible.

## 1. The neuron and the direction of the signal

The neuron consists of dendrites, a soma, the axon hillock, and the axon, which ends in terminals.
The soma, or cell body, contains the nucleus.
Dendrites receive incoming signals and carry them towards the soma.
The soma, in turn, integrates all incoming signals.
The axon hillock acts as the trigger zone.
If the summed depolarisation reaches threshold there, an action potential is initiated at that point.
From the hillock, the action potential travels along the axon to the terminals, which pass the signal on to the next cell.
So, in the living neuron, information flows in one direction only.
It goes from dendrite to soma, on to the axon hillock, along the axon, and out to the terminal.

## 2. Local circuits and propagation of the action potential

Now, suppose an action potential is generated at one point of the membrane.
Sodium ions rush in, and the inside of that region becomes positive.
These positive charges spread passively along the inside of the fibre, forming what are called local circuit currents.
The local current depolarises the neighbouring regions of membrane.
Once a neighbouring region reaches threshold, its voltage-gated sodium channels open, and a complete new action potential is generated there.
This repeats from one segment to the next, and in this way the action potential propagates along the whole axon.
Because it is regenerated at every new site, and because it is all or none, the action potential does not weaken with distance.
Consider, too, a fibre that is stimulated at its midpoint.
In that case, the local current spreads to both sides, and two action potentials travel away from the stimulation site in opposite directions.

## 3. The refractory period makes propagation one way only

In principle, then, local currents can spread in both directions.
In the body, however, the action potential normally starts at the axon hillock and travels towards the terminals.
What guarantees this is the refractory period.
Immediately after firing, the stretch of membrane just behind the advancing action potential is refractory.
In other words, its sodium channels are inactivated and cannot reopen for a short time.
A refractory region can't be brought to threshold again, which means the impulse can never turn back on itself.
The refractory period therefore ensures that propagation runs in one direction only.
It also keeps successive action potentials as separate events.
On top of that, it sets the maximum firing frequency of the neuron.

## 4. Saltatory conduction

That brings us to myelinated axons, where the myelin sheath insulates the membrane.
In these axons, voltage-gated sodium channels are concentrated at the nodes of Ranvier.
The nodes are the gaps between myelin segments, and they are spaced about one to three millimetres apart.
As a result, action potentials are generated only at the nodes.
The influx of sodium at one active node depolarises the inside of the cell for up to three millimetres along the axon.
This depolarisation is enough to bring the next node to threshold.
The action potential thus appears to jump from node to node, and this is called saltatory conduction.
It is much faster than the continuous conduction seen in unmyelinated fibres.
It is also cheaper in energy terms.
That's because ion exchange is confined to the nodes, and less sodium has to be pumped back out of the cell afterwards.

## 5. Myelin: production, structure and function

As for myelin itself, it is produced by glial cells.
These are Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system and oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system.
Structurally, myelin consists of many layers of glial membrane wrapped around the axon.
These layers contain sphingomyelin, a lipid that acts as an electrical insulator.
This insulation is precisely what makes saltatory conduction possible.
That completes the action potential's journey, from the trigger zone out to the terminals.
