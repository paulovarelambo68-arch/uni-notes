## Receptive Field and Two Point Discrimination

The receptive field of a sensory neuron is the area over which a stimulus produces a change in the rate of action potential firing.
Receptive fields are what set the spatial accuracy of somatic sensation.
Two point discrimination, in turn, is the test that measures that accuracy.
In what follows, we'll define the receptive field, see how fields overlap and converge, relate their size to the density of afferents, and finish with the two point test itself.

## 1. Definition and spatial accuracy

To begin, a stimulus inside a neuron's receptive field changes its firing rate, whereas one outside it does not.
It follows that the field determines the spatial accuracy of sensory stimuli.
It does so because the nervous system localises a touch by knowing which afferent is firing, and so which patch of skin the signal comes from.
Field size also differs across body regions, and this difference underlies regional differences in acuity.

## 2. Overlapping fields and summation at the second order neuron

Receptive fields are not separate tiles, though.
Those of neighbouring primary afferents overlap, meaning that a single point of skin lies within several fields.
These afferents converge centrally, and their inputs summate at the second order neuron.
The receptive field of that second order neuron includes all the overlapping primary fields that feed it.
As a result, it is larger than any single one of them.

## 3. Field size versus afferent density

Now, there is an inverse relationship between the size of the receptive field and the number of afferents supplying the area.
Densely innervated regions, such as the fingertips, lips and toes, are served by many afferents, each with a small field.
The outcome is fine spatial accuracy.
Sparsely innervated regions, such as the forearm and the back, are served by few afferents with large fields.
There, spatial accuracy is coarse.
Hence the illustration from class, that an itch on the back is hard to localise well enough to scratch.
The reason is precisely that the back has few afferents with large receptive fields.
Consequently, the active afferent only narrows the stimulus down to a broad patch of skin.

## 4. Two point discrimination

That brings us to two point discrimination, which is the minimum distance required to perceive two simultaneously applied stimuli as distinct.
It is classically tested with callipers.
The threshold tracks both field size and innervation density.
It is finest at the fingertips, at about two millimetres, and coarsest on the arm, back and leg, at up to roughly forty millimetres.
When the two sides of the body are compared, the small differences between left and right fall within the margin of error of the measurement.
In other words, they carry no real meaning.
What matters is the regional gradient, from about two millimetres at the fingertips to about forty millimetres on the trunk and limbs.
As for the mechanism, it rests on the pattern of activity across afferents with overlapping fields.
Two points close together within one field generate a single focus of activity, and they are felt as one.
Picture three afferents with overlapping fields, stimulated by two calliper points.
With the points close together, only the central afferent fires strongly, and the population shows a single peak of activity.
As the points move apart, the neighbouring afferents join in.
Only when the points are far enough apart to fall in different fields does the population show two separate foci of firing.
The firing profile then splits into two distinct peaks.
The nervous system reads these as two stimuli, and they are felt as two touches.
This is why two point discrimination depends on receptive field size.
The fingertip is covered in tiny, densely packed fields, so callipers with tips two millimetres apart already land in different fields.
The forearm, on the other hand, has large, broadly overlapping fields.
Here, the callipers must be opened to about forty millimetres before two separate peaks emerge.

## Summary

To sum up, a receptive field is the area over which a stimulus changes a neuron's action potential firing rate, and it determines spatial accuracy.
Primary afferent fields overlap and converge, with summation at the second order neuron.
That neuron's field is the union of the overlapping primary fields.
Field size is inversely related to afferent density.
Small fields and dense innervation give fine acuity, while large fields and sparse innervation give coarse acuity.
Two point discrimination, the minimum distance at which two stimuli are perceived as distinct, measures this directly.
It is about two millimetres on the fingertips, compared with up to forty millimetres on the limbs and trunk.
That is because two points can only be told apart once they fall in different receptive fields and produce two separate foci of activity.
In the end, it all comes down to how small the fields are and how many afferents supply the skin.
