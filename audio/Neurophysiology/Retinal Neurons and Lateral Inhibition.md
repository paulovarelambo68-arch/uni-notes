## Retinal Neurons and Lateral Inhibition

The retina passes visual information through a short chain of neurones.
Running across that chain are the horizontal and amacrine cells, and they mediate lateral inhibition in the retina.
This retinal lateral inhibition enhances the quality of sensory information.
It also provides the contrast information on which visual acuity depends.
We'll follow the chain first, then see what lateral inhibition achieves there, and finally meet the two cell types that carry it out.

## The retinal cell chain

To begin, visual signals flow through the retina in a chain of three neurones.
The rods and cones come first, and they signal to bipolar cells.
The bipolar cells pass the signal on to ganglion cells.
It is the axons of these ganglion cells that form the optic nerve.

## Lateral inhibition in the retina

Next comes lateral inhibition itself.
In general terms, it is a mechanism in which the most active pathway suppresses its flanking neighbours.
For vision, though, what matters is what that mechanism achieves in the retina.
For a start, lateral inhibition enhances the quality of sensory information.
It also sharpens the difference between strongly and weakly stimulated pathways, and in doing so it provides contrast information.
That contrast information, in turn, enables visual acuity.
In other words, seeing a sharp image at all depends on this retinal contrast enhancement, and not just on the optics of the eye.

## Horizontal and amacrine cells mediate lateral inhibition

So which cells carry out these lateral interactions?
The answer is two further types of retinal neurone, which sit across the vertical chain.
The first type is the horizontal cell.
Horizontal cells lie at the level between the photoreceptors and the bipolar cells.
Their processes mediate the lateral inhibition that maintains sensitivity to contrast.
The second type is the amacrine cell, which lies one level deeper, between the bipolar cells and the ganglion cells.
The processes of amacrine cells are postsynaptic to the bipolar cell terminals and presynaptic to the ganglion cell dendrites.
Through these connections, they mediate lateral interactions at that deeper level as well.
Together, the two cell types allow each point in the retinal image to be compared with its surround, before the signal ever leaves the eye.
An example using the letter A makes this function concrete.
After all, a letter is nothing more than dark strokes against a light background.
Reading it therefore depends entirely on detecting the contrast at its edges.
Lateral inhibition exaggerates exactly these edges, so the strokes of the letter A stand out crisply from the white around them.
Thanks to this, letters can still be resolved as they shrink.
Now, the smallest letter that can still be resolved defines visual acuity.
It follows that acuity is a direct product of the contrast information that horizontal and amacrine cells create.

## Summary

To sum up, the retinal signal travels from rods and cones to bipolar cells, and from there to ganglion cells.
The axons of those ganglion cells form the optic nerve.
Across this chain, horizontal and amacrine cells mediate lateral inhibition.
That inhibition enhances the quality of sensory information and supplies contrast information.
This is how it enables visual acuity, as illustrated by resolving the edges of the letter A.
That completes our look at how retinal neurones sharpen contrast before the signal ever leaves the eye.
