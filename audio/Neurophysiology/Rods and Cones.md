## Rods and Cones

Rods and cones are the two types of photoreceptor in the retina, and they divide the work of vision between them.
Rods serve night vision, trading acuity for extreme sensitivity.
Cones serve day vision, trading sensitivity for acuity, colour and temporal resolution.
We'll meet each receptor on its own, then see where they sit and how they connect to the ganglion cells.
After that, we'll cover how they respond to light and how they are built.

## Rods

First up are the rods, which are responsible for night vision.
They are very sensitive to light, with a low threshold and a high level of photopigment.
That lets them respond even in dim conditions.
The price of this sensitivity is low acuity, and it comes from two features.
The first is that there is much amplification of the signal, meaning even a tiny response is boosted.
The second is convergence and summation of the signal at bipolar cells.
Many rods synapse onto the same bipolar cell, and this is the convergence.
That bipolar cell adds together the graded potentials arriving from all of them, and this is the summation.
Weak signals that would go unnoticed in a single rod can therefore combine into a signal strong enough to matter.
Once merged, however, they can no longer be traced back to any individual rod, and as a result fine spatial detail is lost.
Rods also adapt slowly, which is why vision takes time to adjust when light levels change.

## Cones

Cones, by contrast, are responsible for day vision.
They are less sensitive than rods, with a high threshold, which means they only operate when light is abundant.
In return, they give high acuity.
That acuity rests on less signal amplification and little convergence in their circuitry.
What's more, they are concentrated in the fovea, the region of sharpest vision.
Cones come in three types, red, green and blue, which together support colour vision.
On top of that, they have high temporal resolution, resolving rapid changes in the stimulus that rods would blur together.

## Distribution and wiring to the ganglion cells

Next, where do these receptors actually sit?
Rods and cones lie at the very back of the retina, so light must pass through the other retinal layers before reaching them.
Across the retina as a whole, rods outnumber cones, while cones cluster mainly in the fovea.
Just as important, the two receptors are wired differently at each relay of the retinal pathway.
Rods synapse onto shared bipolar cells, which then converge on a single ganglion cell.
In this way, many rods feed each ganglion cell.
The weak signals of the whole group are pooled, raising sensitivity at the cost of precision.
Cones, on the other hand, connect through their own bipolar cells to a separate ganglion cell.
Only a few cones feed each ganglion cell.
Each cone therefore keeps a nearly private line, and precision stays high.
Both kinds of ganglion cell send their axons out through the optic nerve.

## Graded response to light

Now to the way these cells respond to light.
Light hyperpolarises both rods and cones, and it does so as a graded response rather than an action potential.
This graded design works because the cell is small.
The signal only has to spread the short distance from the outer segment to the receptor terminal, so no action potential is needed to carry it.
Instead, the size of the potential simply regulates how much transmitter the terminal releases.
The bipolar cell that receives this signal works with graded potentials as well.
It is the ganglion cells that generate the first action potentials of the visual pathway.
They convert the graded signal into a firing pattern able to travel the length of the optic nerve.
Consider a recording made from inside a single photoreceptor.
The experiment was repeated many times, each time with a flash of a different intensity, always delivered at zero milliseconds.
In the dark, the cell rests at about minus forty millivolts.
Every flash produces a downward dip in the potential, which is a hyperpolarisation.
The least intense flash gives a shallow dip of a few millivolts that recovers quickly.
As intensity rises, the dips grow deeper and slower to recover.
Eventually, the most intense flash drives the potential down to about minus sixty-five millivolts, where the response saturates.
This is the most negative value the cell can reach.
Beyond that point, extra light produces no extra hyperpolarisation.
The conclusion is that the response is proportional to stimulus intensity.
Because both the depth and the duration of the dip grow with the flash, the graded potential encodes how bright the light was.

## Anatomy of a rod and a cone

As for their anatomy, each rod and each cone is a complete neuron in its own right.
Every receptor thus has its own cell body and its own synaptic terminal, rather than being a part of a larger cell.
Both share the same plan.
At the back of the retina sits the outer segment.
It is packed with membranous folds that carry the photopigment and receive the incoming light.
Next comes the inner segment, which holds the nucleus.
The inner segment is crowded with mitochondria to meet the high energy demand.
A narrow ciliary bridge joins the two segments.
At the front, the receptor terminal synapses onto a bipolar cell.
Their outer segments, though, take different shapes.
The rod's is long and cylindrical, whereas the cone's is shorter and tapers, and this shape gives each cell its name.

## Summary

Pulling all of this together, rods and cones are complete retinal neurons built on the same plan.
Each has an outer segment packed with photopigment, long and cylindrical in rods but short and tapering in cones.
Each also has an inner segment crowded with mitochondria, a ciliary bridge, and a receptor terminal synapsing onto bipolar cells.
Rods are sensitive, slow and convergent, built for night vision at the cost of acuity.
Cones are less sensitive but fast and precise.
They cluster in the fovea and are divided into red, green and blue types for day and colour vision.
Both respond to light with a graded hyperpolarisation whose depth and duration encode intensity.
This signal travels as graded potentials until the ganglion cells convert it into the first action potentials of the visual pathway.
And that's how the retina's two photoreceptors share out the work of seeing.
