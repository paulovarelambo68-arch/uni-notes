## Sensation and Sensory Perception

Sensation is the ability of the nervous system to transduce, encode and perceive information from external and internal stimuli.
It's worth unpacking those three words.
Receptors convert the stimulus into electrical signals, and this is called transduction.
The signals are then carried and processed as action potentials, which is known as encoding.
Lastly, the cerebral cortex turns them into conscious experience, and that is perception.
We'll go through the general senses first, move on to the special senses, and finish with the chain of neurons that carries sensory information up to the cortex.

## General senses: somatic and visceral

To start with, the general sensory system is divided in two.
On one side is somatic sensation, which arises from the body surface and its framework.
It responds to mechanical, thermal and chemical stimulation.
Somatic sensation is carried by somatic afferent fibres, and it includes modalities such as touch, temperature and pain.
On the other side is visceral sensation, which arises from the internal organs.
This kind of sensation generally does not reach the level of conscious awareness.

## Special senses

Alongside these general senses stand the special senses.
They are vision, hearing, taste, smell and balance.
Rather than relying on the general sensory receptors of the skin and viscera, they are handled by dedicated sense organs.

## The three neuron relay to the cortex

Now, sensation is perceived in the cerebral cortex.
That means every sensory pathway must carry information from the site of sensation up to the somatosensory cortex.
This is done by a chain of three neurons.
The first-order neuron is the peripheral afferent itself, and its cell body sits in the dorsal root ganglia.
Next comes the second-order neuron, which is located in the spinal cord or the brain stem.
Finally, the third-order neuron is located in the thalamus, which relays the signal to the somatosensory cerebral cortex.

## Summary

To sum up, sensation transduces, encodes and perceives external and internal stimuli.
The general senses split into somatic and visceral sensation.
Somatic sensation responds to mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli, while visceral sensation comes from the internal organs and is mostly unconscious.
Both of these general senses are distinct from the special senses.
Information reaches the cortex through a relay of three neurons.
The first-order neuron comes from the periphery, with its cell body in the dorsal root ganglia.
The second-order neuron lies in the spinal cord or brain stem, and the third-order neuron runs from the thalamus to the somatosensory cortex.
With that, we've followed a sensation all the way from the receptor to conscious experience.
