## Sensory Receptors and Afferent Types

Sensory receptors are the structures that convert stimuli into electrical signals the nervous system can read.
Those stimuli can come from the outside world or from the body itself.
Receptors range from simple free nerve endings to the highly complex receptor cells of the special senses.
Four features distinguish them from one another.
These are the form of the peripheral terminal, the sensitivity to particular stimuli, the diameter of the axon, and the presence of a myelin sheath.
That sensitivity is known as modality specificity, and it includes the threshold.
In what follows, we'll look at each feature, then at the forms a terminal can take, and finally at the types of sensory afferent.

## 1. The four features that distinguish receptors

First comes the form of the peripheral terminal.
The terminal can be a bare free ending, an ending wrapped in a connective tissue capsule, or a separate receptor cell altogether.
This structural plan determines how the stimulus reaches the neuronal membrane.
The second feature is modality specificity, together with threshold.
Each receptor is sensitive to a particular kind of stimulus.
In other words, it responds preferentially, though not always, to one modality, such as touch, pain, temperature or proprioception.
Its sensitivity is expressed as a threshold, which is the minimum stimulus intensity needed to trigger a response.
A receptor has a low threshold for its own modality, so it fires easily to it, while other stimuli simply fail to activate it.
Even so, this specificity is not absolute.
A stimulus of another modality can still activate the receptor if the intensity is high enough.
This explains why very intense stimuli of any kind can become painful.
As intensity rises, the stimulus recruits high-threshold nociceptive afferents.
So a bright enough light hurts the eye, even though the eye is built for seeing.
The last two features are the diameter of the axon and the presence of a myelin sheath.
Together, these two decide conduction velocity.
A larger diameter and myelin mean faster signalling.
As a result, the receptor type already tells you how quickly its information reaches the central nervous system.

## 2. Forms of the peripheral terminal

Now for the three structural plans a terminal can follow.
We begin with the simple receptors, which are free nerve endings.
Here the receptor is the ending of the sensory neuron itself, with no accessory structure.
The axon is unmyelinated and conducts at zero point five to two metres per second.
Pain, temperature and itch are carried this way.
Next are the complex neural receptors, which are encapsulated endings.
In these, the nerve ending is enclosed in a connective tissue capsule that shapes how the stimulus deforms the membrane.
The Pacinian corpuscle is the textbook example.
The axon of an encapsulated ending is myelinated and conducts at twelve to thirty metres per second, much faster than a bare ending.
Third, there are the special sense receptors, which are receptor cells.
In this case, the receptor is a separate cell, not the ending of a neuron.
The stimulus acts on the receptor cell, which forms a synapse onto a sensory afferent.
The cell then releases neurotransmitter onto that afferent, initiating an action potential in it.
The graded potential produced by the stimulus has its own name, depending on the plan.
When a stimulus acts directly on the afferent ending, whether free or encapsulated, it produces a generator potential in that ending.
When it acts on a separate receptor cell, as in the special senses, it produces a receptor potential in the cell.
That receptor potential then drives transmitter release onto the afferent.
Put simply, the logic is the same as before, direct in the first two plans and through a synapse in the third.

## 3. Types of sensory afferents

Moving on, each sensory function is carried by a characteristic pairing of receptor and axon type.
What's more, the axon type predicts both diameter and conduction velocity.
Proprioception uses the muscle spindle, and it is carried by group one A and group two afferents.
These fibres measure thirteen to twenty micrometres in diameter, and they conduct at eighty to one hundred and twenty metres per second.
They are the fastest afferents, because posture and movement need rapid feedback.
Touch uses the encapsulated skin mechanoreceptors, namely the Merkel, Meissner, Pacinian and Ruffini cells.
It is carried by A-beta afferents, six to twelve micrometres across, conducting at thirty-five to seventy-five metres per second.
Pain and temperature use free nerve endings on A-delta afferents.
These are thinly myelinated, one to five micrometres across, and conduct at five to thirty metres per second.
Pain, temperature and itch also use free nerve endings, but this time on C fibres.
C fibres are unmyelinated, zero point two to one point five micrometres across, and conduct at zero point five to two metres per second.
They are the slowest of all.

## Summary

In summary, sensory receptors range from simple free nerve endings to specialised receptor cells.
They are distinguished by four features, which are the form of the peripheral terminal, modality specificity with threshold, axon diameter and myelination.
Structurally, there are three plans.
The first has bare endings on unmyelinated axons, conducting at zero point five to two metres per second.
The second has encapsulated endings on myelinated axons, conducting at twelve to thirty metres per second.
The third relies on separate receptor cells that release neurotransmitter onto a sensory afferent.
Stimulation produces a graded potential.
It is called a generator potential when it arises in the afferent ending itself, and a receptor potential when it arises in a separate receptor cell.
Functionally, proprioception runs on fast group one A and group two afferents, at eighty to one hundred and twenty metres per second.
Touch runs on A-beta afferents, at thirty-five to seventy-five metres per second.
Pain and temperature run on A-delta afferents, at five to thirty metres per second.
Finally, pain, temperature and itch run on slow unmyelinated C fibres, at zero point five to two metres per second.
The rule that ties it together is that the larger and more myelinated the axon, the faster the sense reaches the brain.
And so we have travelled from the shape of a receptor's ending all the way to the speed of its fibre.
