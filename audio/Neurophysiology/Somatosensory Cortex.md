## Somatosensory Cortex

The primary somatosensory cortex, or S one, is the cortical region that brings somatic sensory signals into conscious awareness.
As it does so, it registers both their intensity and their location.
It occupies the postcentral gyrus of the parietal lobe, and it holds an orderly map of the body.
We'll find out where S one sits, look at its body map and its columns, and then follow its information onwards.

## Location and fields

First, where exactly is it?
S one sits in the postcentral gyrus of the parietal lobe, directly behind the central sulcus.
It is divided into four distinct fields, known as Brodmann areas three A, three B, one and two.
Each of these fields contains its own complete representation of the body.

## The somatotopic map: the homunculus

Next comes the body map itself.
The body is mapped onto S one in a somatotopic arrangement.
This means that neighbouring body parts are processed by neighbouring patches of cortex.
Each hemisphere, meanwhile, represents the opposite side of the body.
When this map is drawn onto the cortex, it produces the homunculus, a distorted figure.
Its proportions reflect sensitivity rather than physical size.
Put another way, the amount of cortex devoted to each body part is proportional to how sensitive that part is.
Parts that serve critical functions also receive a greater representation.
The classic example is the face and lips, whose greater share reflects the importance of facial expression.

## Functional columns and modality areas

Turning now to how the cells are organised, neurons in S one are grouped into functionally distinct columns.
These columns run through the depth of the cortex, so the cells within one column share the same response properties.
Fast adapting and slow adapting neurons are found in different areas of the map, rather than being intermixed.
On top of that, the four fields divide the work by modality.
Areas three B and one handle cutaneous stimuli, namely pain and temperature.
Area three A, by contrast, handles proprioception.
And area two processes both tactile input from mechanoreceptors and proprioception.

## Beyond SI

S one, however, is only the first cortical stage.
From here, information travels onwards along several routes.
One route leads to the secondary somatosensory cortex, or S two, which lies in the lateral sulcus.
S two in turn projects to limbic structures, especially the amygdala and the hippocampus.
This gives the stimulus significance for learning and memory.
A second route runs from S one to other parietal areas, which relay onwards to the motor and premotor areas.
This is the route for integrating sensory and motor information.
Through it, proprioception reaches the circuits that guide movement.
Finally, S one sends descending projections back down to the thalamus, the brain stem and the spinal cord.
These projections fine tune the ascending input, regulating the flow of sensory information on its way up.

## Summary

To sum up, S one lies in the postcentral gyrus of the parietal lobe and gives conscious awareness of the intensity and location of somatic signals.
Its somatotopic map, the homunculus, grants more cortex to sensitive and critical parts, such as the face for facial expression.
Its neurons form functional columns, with fast and slow adapting cells in different areas.
Meanwhile, its fields split the modalities, with areas three B and one for cutaneous input, three A for proprioception, and area two for both.
Beyond S one, projections reach S two in the lateral sulcus, and from there the pathway continues to the amygdala and hippocampus, for learning and memory.
Others pass through parietal areas to the motor and premotor cortex, for sensorimotor integration.
Still others descend to the thalamus, brain stem and spinal cord, to fine tune the ascending input.
And that is the somatosensory cortex, from its map of the body to the routes that lead beyond it.
