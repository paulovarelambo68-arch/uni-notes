## Synaptic Transmission

Synaptic transmission is the process by which an action potential in the presynaptic neuron causes the release of neurotransmitter.
That transmitter crosses the synaptic cleft and acts on receptors of the postsynaptic cell.
In this way, an electrical signal is converted into a chemical signal, and back again into an electrical or metabolic response.
The whole process, from the arrival of the action potential to the release of transmitter, takes less than a millisecond.
Over the next few parts, we'll meet the structures of the synapse, follow the release mechanism step by step, see how vesicles are recycled, and finish with how synapses are arranged.

## 2. The synapse’s structures

Starting on the presynaptic side, there is the presynaptic neuron and its axon terminal, also called the synaptic bouton.
This terminal is the swollen ending of the axon, and it contains the machinery for transmitter release.
Clustered inside the terminal are the synaptic vesicles, also known as secretory granules.
These are membrane-bound organelles filled with neurotransmitter.
The presynaptic membrane, or active zone, is the specialised region of the terminal membrane where vesicles dock and fuse.
Beyond it lies the synaptic cleft, a narrow gap of about twenty to forty nanometres that separates the presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes.
Transmitter diffuses across this gap.
On the far side is the postsynaptic membrane, the membrane of the target cell.
It is enriched with receptors, which bind the transmitter and generate the postsynaptic response.
Finally, the postsynaptic neuron is the cell receiving the signal.

## 3. The release mechanism, step by step

Release unfolds as a sequence of eight steps.
It all begins with the arrival of the action potential.
The action potential propagates along the axon and invades the axon terminal, depolarising the terminal's membrane.
Second, voltage-gated calcium channels open.
Depolarisation activates these channels, which are concentrated at the active zone.
Because intracellular calcium is very low at rest, the electrochemical gradient strongly favours calcium entry, so calcium rushes into the terminal.
As a result, the local calcium concentration near the mouths of the channels rises sharply.
The third step is the activation of calcium calmodulin-dependent protein kinase two.
Entering calcium binds a protein called calmodulin.
In turn, the calcium calmodulin complex activates this kinase, which is abundant in the terminal.
Step four is the mobilisation of vesicles from the reserve pool.
In the reserve pool, vesicles are tethered to the cytoskeleton by a protein called synapsin.
The kinase phosphorylates synapsin, which releases its grip on the vesicles and frees them to move towards the membrane.
This is what is meant by mobilisation.
Fifth comes docking.
Mobilised vesicles are brought to the release sites on the presynaptic membrane, where they are attached.
The attachment depends on an interaction between a vesicle protein and two membrane proteins.
The vesicle protein is synaptobrevin, also called vesicle-associated membrane protein, or VAMP.
The membrane proteins are syntaxin and synaptosomal-associated protein twenty-five, known as SNAP twenty-five.
Together, these form the SNARE complex, a name short for soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor.
Step six is priming.
Here the SNARE complex partially assembles and tightens.
This brings the vesicle membrane into very close apposition with the plasma membrane.
The vesicle is now release-ready, which makes it part of the readily releasable pool.
Seventh, fusion is triggered by calcium.
Calcium binds synaptotagmin, the vesicular calcium sensor.
This triggers complete zippering of the SNARE complex, the membranes merge, and a fusion pore opens.
More precisely, once calcium binds synaptotagmin, synaptotagmin inserts into the lipids of the presynaptic membrane and curves it locally.
That local curving is what finally fuses the two membranes and opens the fusion pore.
That brings us to the eighth step, exocytosis.
The contents of the vesicle are discharged into the synaptic cleft by exocytosis.
On electron microscopy, this is visible as the omega-shaped profile of a fusing vesicle.
Transmitter then diffuses across the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors.

## One line summary to memorise

Squeezed into a single line, the chain runs like this.
The action potential depolarises the terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open, and calcium flows in.
Calcium bound to calmodulin switches on the kinase, synapsin is phosphorylated, and the vesicles are mobilised.
They dock, they are primed, and calcium acting on synaptotagmin triggers fusion, which ends in exocytosis.

## 4.1 Vesicle recycling: clathrin mediated Endocytosis

Let's turn now to recycling.
The vesicle membrane must be recovered and reused, and this process is the vesicle cycle.
Coating comes first.
After fusion, patches of membrane are coated on their cytoplasmic side by the protein clathrin, forming a clathrin-coated pit.
Budding follows, as the coated membrane invaginates and pinches off from the surface as a clathrin-coated vesicle.
A protein called dynamin helps to sever the neck.
In the uncoating step, the clathrin coat is removed, which returns a naked vesicle.
Next, in the endosome step, uncoated vesicles may pass through an endosome intermediate, which sorts vesicles and regenerates them by budding.
Then comes transmitter loading.
The regenerated vesicle is refilled with neurotransmitter by specific vesicular transporter proteins in its membrane.
The final step is the return to the reserve pool.
Refilled vesicles rejoin the reserve pool, tethered once more to the cytoskeleton by synapsin.
This leaves them ready for the next cycle of mobilisation, docking, priming and fusion.
The key point is that recycling is continuous and local within the terminal.
It allows transmission to be sustained without new vesicles being made in the cell body.

## 4.2 Vesicle recycling: why two budding steps?

So why bud twice?
The answer is that the vesicle passes through two stations, and each budding has a different job.
The first budding is retrieval.
After exocytosis, the used vesicle membrane is a flat patch in the presynaptic membrane.
Clathrin coats this patch and curves it into a pit, which pinches off back into the terminal.
This step simply recovers the used membrane, and the clathrin coat is then removed, in the step called uncoating.
The second budding, by contrast, is regeneration.
At this stage, the recovered vesicle is the wrong shape, it may carry leftover membrane proteins, and it is empty.
It therefore fuses with the endosome, which acts as a sorting and repackaging station.
From the endosome, a new vesicle of the correct size and protein composition buds off.
Only then is the vesicle refilled with neurotransmitter, in transmitter loading, and returned to the reserve pool.
One caveat is worth keeping in mind.
Some vesicles skip the endosome and are refilled directly after uncoating.
Even so, the slides show the route through the endosome.

## 5. Synaptic arrangement: axons to dendrites

Lastly, a word on the arrangement of synapses.
Most synaptic transmission in the nervous system is axodendritic.
In other words, the axon terminal of one neuron synapses onto a dendrite of the next neuron, often onto small protrusions called dendritic spines.
A single dendrite receives many synaptic contacts from many different axons, which is known as convergence.
This convergence is the anatomical basis for the integration of inputs by the postsynaptic cell.
For comparison, less common arrangements also exist.
One is axosomatic, where an axon synapses onto a cell body.
Another is axoaxonic, where an axon synapses onto another axon terminal, and this is the basis of presynaptic inhibition.
And with that, we've followed synaptic transmission from the arriving action potential all the way to how synapses are arranged.
