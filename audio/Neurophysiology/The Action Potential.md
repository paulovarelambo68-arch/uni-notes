## The Action Potential

An action potential is a rapid, transient reversal of the membrane potential in an excitable cell, that is, a neuron or a muscle fibre.
The interior flips from about minus seventy millivolts, the resting membrane potential, to about plus thirty millivolts, and back again.
All of this happens in roughly one millisecond.
It is the unit of long-distance electrical signalling along axons.
To work, it requires voltage-gated ion channels, which are proteins built from subunits.
Channels can also be ligand-gated or mechanosensitive.
In what follows, we'll look at threshold, the all or nothing law and propagation, before moving on to the three phases, the channels behind them and the refractory periods.

## Threshold

Let's begin with threshold, the critical depolarisation needed to fire an action potential.
It lies about fifteen millivolts above the resting membrane potential, taking the membrane from minus seventy millivolts up to roughly minus fifty-five millivolts.
Depolarisations that stay below threshold produce only small graded potentials, and these fade out.
By contrast, crossing threshold opens voltage-gated sodium channels in a regenerative, self-reinforcing way, and the full action potential follows.
Only depolarising stimuli can fire an action potential.
Hyperpolarising currents never do, however strong they are.
Subthreshold responses are graded.
The closer the stimulus gets to threshold, the larger the local potential becomes, for instance as it rises from eleven to eighty-nine per cent of threshold.
Even so, all of these responses fade out.
At one hundred per cent of threshold, the response jumps abruptly to a full action potential.
This is the experimental proof of the all or nothing law.

## All or nothing

That leads us to the all or nothing law itself.
If threshold is reached, the action potential always fires at the same full amplitude, peaking at about plus thirty millivolts.
If threshold is not reached, nothing happens at all.
This means stimulus strength does not change the size of the action potential.
Instead, stronger stimuli are coded as a higher firing frequency, not as bigger action potentials.

## Self propagating

Next, the action potential is self-propagating, meaning it is regenerated at full size at every point along the axon.
Local current from the active region depolarises the neighbouring membrane to threshold, which opens that membrane's sodium channels.
The signal therefore travels without decaying.
It also travels in one direction only, because the region just behind it is refractory.

## The three phases (the curve)

Now let's trace the shape of the action potential, which unfolds in three phases.
The first is depolarisation, also called the rising phase.
Once threshold is reached, the activation gates of the sodium channels open, and sodium rushes into the cell.
Sodium conductance rises by as much as five thousandfold.
Positive feedback then drives the membrane towards plus thirty millivolts, close to the sodium equilibrium potential.
The second phase is repolarisation, or the falling phase.
Here, the sodium inactivation gates close, and the voltage-gated potassium channels, which are slower to open, finally open.
Potassium leaves the cell, and the interior becomes negative again.
The third phase is hyperpolarisation, known as the undershoot.
Because potassium channels are slow to close, potassium keeps leaving the cell.
As a result, the membrane dips below the resting membrane potential, towards the potassium equilibrium potential of about minus eighty millivolts, before returning to rest.
This phase matches the relative refractory period.

## Voltage gated Na⁺ channel gates

Taking a closer look at the voltage-gated sodium channel, we find that each one has two gates.
The activation gate opens fast when the membrane depolarises to threshold.
The inactivation gate closes shortly afterwards, automatically, even while the depolarisation persists.
Together, the two gates give the channel a sequence of states.
At rest, it is closed but available, with the activation gate shut and the inactivation gate open.
At threshold, it is open, with both gates open, letting sodium flow in as the conductance rises five thousandfold.
At the peak, it is inactivated, meaning the inactivation gate is shut and no sodium flows.
Finally, it goes back to closed and available once repolarisation resets both gates.
This inactivated state is the molecular basis of the absolute refractory period.

## Voltage gated K⁺ channels and repolarisation

The voltage-gated potassium channels, by contrast, have a single slow activation gate.
It is opened by the same depolarisation, but with a delay.
Their late opening, combined with sodium channel inactivation, ends the upstroke.
Outward potassium current then repolarises the membrane.
And since these channels close slowly, the membrane transiently hyperpolarises.
As for the currents, the sodium current is brief and inward, with fast activation and fast inactivation.
The potassium current, meanwhile, is delayed, outward and longer lasting, matching repolarisation and the undershoot.
There are two channel state sequences worth memorising.
Step by step, the sodium channel goes closed, open, inactivating, inactivated, and closed again.
Over the same five steps, the potassium channel goes closed, closed, open, open, and closed.
The key point is timing.
The potassium channel opens with a clear delay relative to the sodium channel.
That delay is what allows depolarisation to develop before repolarisation begins.

## Refractory periods

That brings us to the refractory periods, of which there are two.
The absolute refractory period runs from the upstroke through most of repolarisation.
During this time, no stimulus, however strong, can trigger a second action potential.
That's because the sodium inactivation gates are shut, and they cannot reopen until the membrane repolarises.
Its function is to enforce one-way propagation and to cap the maximum firing rate.
The relative refractory period, on the other hand, occurs during hyperpolarisation.
A second action potential is possible here, but only with a stronger than normal stimulus.
The reason is that the membrane sits further from threshold and potassium is still leaving.
At the same time, some sodium channels have recovered.
Its duration is more variable than that of the absolute period.
Another way to see this is that threshold itself is not fixed.
During the spike, it rises off the scale, so no stimulus can reach it, and that is the absolute refractory period.
During hyperpolarisation, it gradually settles back towards normal, so only a stronger than normal stimulus can reach it, and that is the relative refractory period.
In other words, the refractory periods can be read directly from the threshold curve, not just from the membrane potential curve.

## Key numbers to memorise

To finish, here are the key numbers to memorise.
The resting membrane potential is minus seventy millivolts.
Threshold sits at minus fifty-five millivolts, which is fifteen millivolts above rest.
The peak reaches plus thirty millivolts.
Sodium conductance rises up to five thousandfold.
And the whole event lasts about one millisecond.
With those values in hand, our tour of the action potential is complete.
