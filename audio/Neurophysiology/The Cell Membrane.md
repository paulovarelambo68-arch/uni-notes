## The Cell Membrane

Let's start with the cell membrane, a thin, selectively permeable barrier that encloses every cell.
It's about seven and a half nanometres thick, and it separates the intracellular fluid, or cytoplasm, from the extracellular fluid.
In terms of structure, it's a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins, an arrangement known as the fluid mosaic model.
Within this model, the lipids form the matrix, while the proteins carry out most of the specific functions.
Its key physiological role is controlling what enters and leaves the cell.
This control is the basis of the membrane potential, which underlies all nerve signalling.
With that in mind, let's go through its main components one at a time.

## Phospholipid bilayer

To begin with, there's the phospholipid bilayer, which is the structural matrix of the membrane.
It's made of amphipathic molecules, so their hydrophilic heads face the intracellular or extracellular fluid, while their hydrophobic tails face inwards.
The bilayer is impermeable to ions and polar molecules.
This is what allows charge separation, in other words, the membrane potential.

## Cholesterol

Next comes cholesterol, which is interspersed between the phospholipids.
Its job is to modulate the fluidity and stability of the membrane.
Put simply, it prevents the membrane from becoming too rigid or too fluid.

## Integral (transmembrane) proteins

Now, let's turn to integral, or transmembrane, proteins, which span the entire bilayer.
They serve as channels, as carriers or pumps, as receptors, and as enzymes.
This means they mediate selective transport and signal transduction.

## Channel proteins

Then there are channel proteins, which form pores filled with water.
These pores allow passive ion diffusion, with ions moving down their electrochemical gradients.
For example, there are sodium, potassium, chloride and calcium channels.

## Gated ion channel

Building on that idea, a gated ion channel is a channel that opens or closes in response to a stimulus.
The stimulus might be voltage, a ligand, or something mechanical.
These channels are fundamental to generating action potentials in neurons.

## Peripheral proteins

That brings us to peripheral proteins, which are loosely attached to one surface of the membrane, usually the intracellular one.
They act as enzymes or as structural anchors, providing attachment for the cytoskeleton.
On top of that, they help to support signalling cascades.

## Glycoprotein

Moving on, a glycoprotein is a protein with a carbohydrate chain attached on the extracellular surface.
Glycoproteins function in cell recognition, as receptors, and in cell to cell adhesion.

## Glycolipid

Similarly, a glycolipid is a lipid with a carbohydrate chain on the extracellular side.
Glycolipids contribute to the glycocalyx, which is the coating on the cell surface.
Through this, they play a part in cell identity and recognition.

## Neurophysiology relevance in one line

Finally, here's why all of this matters for neurophysiology, boiled down to a single idea.
Put simply, the lipid bilayer provides the insulating barrier.
Meanwhile, the ion channels and pumps, which are integral proteins, create and exploit the ionic gradients that produce resting and action potentials.
That wraps up the cell membrane and its main components.
