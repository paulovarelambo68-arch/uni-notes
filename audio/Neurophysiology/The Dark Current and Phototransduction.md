## The Dark Current and Phototransduction

The dark current is the continuous influx of sodium ions into the outer segment of a photoreceptor.
In darkness, it keeps the cell depolarised at about minus forty millivolts.
This is possible because cyclic guanosine monophosphate, or cyclic G M P, holds the sodium channels open.
Phototransduction is the cascade by which light lowers cyclic G M P, closes these channels and hyperpolarises the cell.
That is how light is converted into an electrical signal.
We'll begin with the dark current, then follow the light cascade and the hyperpolarisation it produces, before gathering the key numbers and names.

## The dark current

Starting in darkness, then, the photoreceptor rests in a depolarised state.
Sodium flows into the outer segment through cyclic G M P gated sodium channels.
Some calcium enters as well, since the same channels admit it.
This sodium influx is the dark current itself.
It depolarises the cell and sets a membrane potential of about minus forty millivolts.
The channels stay open because cyclic G M P splints them open.
As long as cyclic G M P is bound, the inward current persists.
Working against it, potassium flows out of the inner segment through its own channels.
So the steady minus forty millivolts reflects the balance between sodium influx and potassium efflux.
As stressed in class, this resting potential is unusually high.
A typical neuron rests near minus seventy millivolts.
The photoreceptor, by contrast, already sits partly depolarised at minus forty millivolts before any stimulus arrives.

## The light cascade

Now, what happens when light arrives?
Light activates the photopigment.
Activated rhodopsin, known as metarhodopsin two, then starts the cascade.
Metarhodopsin two switches on the G protein transducin.
Transducin in turn activates cyclic G M P phosphodiesterase.
That is the enzyme to know by its full name, another point emphasised in class.
Two enzymes control cyclic G M P levels.
Guanylyl cyclase builds cyclic G M P from guanosine triphosphate, or G T P.
Cyclic G M P phosphodiesterase, on the other hand, breaks cyclic G M P down into five prime guanosine monophosphate, or five prime G M P.
The phosphodiesterase is active in the light, so illumination tips the balance towards breakdown and cyclic G M P levels fall.
With cyclic G M P gone, the splint is removed and the cyclic G M P gated ion channels close.
The dark current is cut off.

## Hyperpolarisation in the light

Once the channels close, the inward sodium current stops.
Meanwhile, the outward potassium current of the inner segment is maintained.
Positive charge keeps leaving the cell but no longer enters, which makes the interior more negative.
In other words, the cell hyperpolarises.
Light therefore produces the opposite of the depolarising receptor potential seen in most sensory receptors.

## Key numbers and names to memorise

That brings us to the numbers and names worth memorising.
The dark membrane potential is about minus forty millivolts, against about minus seventy millivolts in a typical neuron.
In the cascade, light comes first, followed by metarhodopsin two, then transducin, the G protein, and finally cyclic G M P phosphodiesterase.
After that, cyclic G M P falls, the cyclic G M P gated channels close, and the cell hyperpolarises.
As for the enzymes, guanylyl cyclase converts G T P to cyclic G M P.
Cyclic G M P phosphodiesterase converts cyclic G M P to five prime G M P, and it is the one active in the light.

## Summary

To sum up, most neurons signal by depolarising.
A stimulus drives the membrane towards threshold, and an action potential follows.
The photoreceptor works the other way round.
It already rests depolarised in the dark, held near minus forty millivolts by the dark current.
And when light arrives, it signals by hyperpolarising.
The reason is that the light cascade lowers cyclic G M P, the splinted sodium channels close, and the unopposed potassium efflux makes the interior more negative.
Put simply, it is the same ionic logic as an ordinary neuron, with the sign reversed.
That completes our tour of the dark current and phototransduction.
