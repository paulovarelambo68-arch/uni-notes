## The Eye and the Retinal Image

The eye is the organ of vision.
It gathers light and focuses it onto the retina, the neural layer lining the back of the globe.
The retina is where the image is formed, and it is also where processing begins.
The image projected onto the retina is inverted, and it covers both a binocular zone and a monocular zone.
In what follows, we'll go from the eye's anatomy, through focusing and its errors, to the retinal image and the back of the eye.

## Anatomy of the Eye

Our first stop is the path that light takes into the eye.
Light enters through the cornea, which is the transparent front surface.
From there, it crosses the anterior chamber and the posterior chamber.
Both are filled with aqueous humour, a clear watery fluid that nourishes the cornea and lens and maintains the pressure of the globe.
After that, light passes the pupil and the lens.
The lens is held in place by the suspensory ligament, which is attached to the ciliary body.
The main cavity of the eye is filled by the vitreous body, a transparent gel that lets light pass through to the back of the eye.
As for the wall of the globe, it has three layers.
These are the tough outer sclera, the middle choroid and the inner retina.
The fovea sits at the back pole.
The optic nerve exits at the blind spot, together with the retinal artery, wrapped in a nerve sheath.
Seen from the outside, the visible front of the eye shows the pupil and the iris through the cornea, along with the white sclera.
The eyelids and eyelashes frame the palpebral fissure, and the medial and lateral commissures sit at each corner.
The iris contracts and dilates to control how much light enters, and dilation lets in more light.
Its colour, however, has no functional purpose.
The choroid, meanwhile, is lightly pigmented with melanin, and it absorbs stray light.
This matters in albino people, who lack this pigment.
Finally, a rise in the pressure of the aqueous humour at the front of the eye is called glaucoma.
It happens when the fluid is produced faster than it drains, so the pressure inside the globe rises.
If that rise is sustained, it can damage the optic nerve and gradually erode vision.

## Accommodation

Next comes accommodation, the process by which the lens changes shape to focus light sharply on the retina.
It helps to compare two states.
In the unaccommodated state, the ciliary muscle is relaxed and the zonule fibres are pulled taut.
The lens is therefore held thin and flattened, which suits distant vision.
In the accommodated state, by contrast, the ciliary muscle contracts and the zonule fibres slacken.
The elastic lens rounds up into a thicker, more curved shape.
This increases its refractive power for near vision.
The key to understanding this is that the ciliary muscle is a ring.
When it is relaxed, the ring is wide, so it pulls the zonule fibres tight and stretches the lens flat.
When it contracts, the ring narrows and the fibres go slack.
As a result, the elastic lens springs back to its natural rounded shape.

## Focusing Light and Refractive Errors

Let's follow the light to where it ends up.
It is focused by the cornea and the lens onto the retina, at the back of the eyeball.
More specifically, it is directed to arrive at the fovea.
The cornea and lens bend incoming rays to a focal point.
But whether that point lands exactly on the retina depends on how far back the retina sits, that is, on the length of the eyeball.
When the two are matched, we call it emmetropia.
This is normal sight, or twenty twenty vision.
It gets that name because, on the standard chart, a healthy eye reads at twenty feet what should be readable at twenty feet.
Against this ideal, there are two refractive errors.
The first is myopia, or short-sightedness, in which the eyeball is longer than normal.
The rays come together before they reach the retina and then spread out again, which blurs distant objects.
The corrective lens therefore spreads the light before it enters, pushing the focal point back onto the retina.
The second is hypermetropia, or long-sightedness, in which the eyeball is shorter than normal.
Here, the rays have not finished converging by the time they reach the retina.
A convex lens converges the light earlier, which brings the focal point forward.
Presbyopia, lastly, is the age-related form of this loss of focusing ability.

## The Retinal Image

Turning to the image itself, the cornea and lens each bend incoming light rays inwards, towards a focal point.
That is why they are described as converging lenses, working on the same principle as a magnifying glass.
One consequence of bending light this way is that the retinal image comes out inverted.
It is flipped one hundred and eighty degrees, both vertically and horizontally.
Objects above you project onto the lower retina, and objects to your left project onto the right.
In other words, light arrives from the opposite side to what you perceive.
The brain has to learn to reinterpret this projection.
Babies take about six weeks to master it, helped by watching where their own hands are.
The visual field is also divided into zones.
The central binocular zone is covered by both eyes at once.
The monocular zones at the far edges, on the other hand, are seen by one eye only.
This is why the full extent of your peripheral vision to either side can only be appreciated with the other eye closed.

## The Fundus

Now for the fundus, the back of the eye as seen in a normal fundoscopic examination.
The pale optic disc is where the optic nerve leaves the eye.
At its centre is a small, paler depression called the optic cup, the point through which the nerve fibres and vessels actually pass.
From the disc, retinal arteries and veins radiate outwards across the surface.
Lateral to the disc lies the darker macula, the region responsible for detailed central vision.
At the centre of the macula sits the fovea, a tiny pit where photoreceptors are densest and acuity is sharpest.
Macular degeneration is the clinical correlate of damage to this region.
Because the optic disc carries no photoreceptors, it forms the blind spot.
There, the brain guesses and fills in the missing input, much like a car sitting in your blind spot.

## Summary

To sum up, vision begins as an optical problem.
The cornea and lens must project a focused image onto the fovea, and accommodation adjusts the lens to keep it sharp at different distances.
What the retina receives is an inverted projection with a built-in blind spot.
Perception, then, is the brain's reconstruction of that image.
That completes our tour of the eye, from the cornea at the front to the fundus at the back.
