## The Inner Ear and the Cochlea

The inner ear is a fluid-filled chamber that receives the vibrations gathered by the middle ear and converts them into neural signals.
Its auditory part is the cochlea, a coiled tube containing three fluid-filled compartments.
Inside it lies the Organ of Corti, the sensory epithelium of hearing.
From here, we'll trace the vibration from the oval window into the cochlea, name its three chambers, and then look closely at the Organ of Corti.

## From the Oval Window to the Cochlea

Let's begin with the fact that the inner ear is filled with fluid rather than air.
So sound arriving from the middle ear must set that fluid in motion.
The oval window transmits the vibrations to the vestibule and on to the cochlea, where they enter through the scala vestibuli.
Because the fluid cannot be compressed, the pressure has to be relieved at the far end.
At that far end, the scala tympani is connected to the round window, which lies between the inner and middle ear.
As the oval window is pushed in, the round window bulges outwards.

## The Three Chambers of the Cochlea

Now picture one turn of the coiled cochlea in cross section.
Its three chambers sit in layers, one on top of the other, and their names must be known by heart.
Uppermost is the scala vestibuli, which is filled with perilymph and is continuous with the vestibule.
In the middle is the scala media, filled with endolymph, which has an unusually high concentration of potassium.
Lowest of the three is the scala tympani, also filled with perilymph, and it ends at the round window.
These names are easy to memorise, because each chamber names its neighbour.
The vestibuli continues the vestibule, the tympani faces the tympanic cavity of the middle ear, and the media lies in the middle.
The fluids follow the same pattern of a pair and an exception.
The two outer scalas share perilymph, a fluid much like ordinary extracellular fluid and rich in sodium.
Only the media holds endolymph, and its high potassium is what makes this chamber so unusual.
As for the walls between the chambers, the basilar membrane separates the scala media from the scala tympani.
The second dividing wall is Reissner's membrane, a thin sheet that separates the scala media from the scala vestibuli above it.
On the outer wall of the scala media sits the stria vascularis, the tissue that maintains the unusual ionic composition of the endolymph.
At the inner core lies the spiral ganglion, which houses the cell bodies of the auditory nerve fibres.

## The Organ of Corti

That brings us to the Organ of Corti, the sensory structure of the cochlea.
It rests on the basilar membrane inside the scala media, and it carries the hair cells.
There is a single row of inner hair cells and, further out, three to four rows of outer hair cells, or five rows in some people.
The tunnel of Corti holds the two groups apart.
This tunnel is an arch braced by the rods of Corti, which are stiff, pillar-shaped supporting cells that give the organ its rigidity.
The basilar cells, meanwhile, are the remaining supporting cells, and they cradle the outer hair cells.
From the apex of each hair cell protrude the stereocilia, the bundle of fine, hair-like processes that gives the cell its name.
They reach towards the tectorial membrane, the gelatinous sheet that overlies the whole organ and rests against their tips.
Capping the top of the organ is the reticular lamina, a stiff, continuous plate.
Within it, the flat apical ends of the hair cells fit together with the flattened tops of the supporting cells, namely the rods of Corti and the basilar cells.
Tight junctions lock them all into a single sealed sheet.
The stereocilia poke out above this plate into the endolymph, while the cell bodies below it are bathed in a perilymph-like fluid.
In other words, the lamina keeps the two fluids apart.
Being rigid, it also makes the whole organ move as one piece.
Turning to the wiring, two sets of nerve fibres serve the organ.
The afferent axons of the spiral ganglion neurons contact the hair cells and gather to form the auditory nerve, which carries the signal to the brain.
The efferent axons, by contrast, arrive from the brainstem and bring modulatory input, mainly to the outer hair cells.

## Summary

To sum up, the inner ear passes vibration from the oval window through the vestibule and into the cochlea.
The cochlea is a fluid-filled tube of three chambers.
The scala vestibuli and the scala tympani, both filled with perilymph, flank the scala media, which is filled with endolymph and unusually high in potassium.
The Organ of Corti rides on the basilar membrane within the scala media.
Its inner and outer hair cells reach the tectorial membrane above and wire into afferent and efferent axons below.
And all the while, the round window relieves the pressure.
That's the inner ear and the cochlea, from the oval window right through to the Organ of Corti.
