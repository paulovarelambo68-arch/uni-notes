## The Lateral Geniculate Nucleus

The lateral geniculate nucleus, or L G N, is the thalamic relay of the visual pathway.
It receives ninety per cent of retinal axons, and it sends the signal on to the visual cortex.
The nucleus is organised into six layers of cell bodies.
These layers keep the inputs of the two eyes, and of the different ganglion cell types, strictly segregated.
We'll look first at the map the L G N holds, and then at its six layers and where they project.

## Retinotopic organisation

The first thing to know is that the L G N holds a retinotopic map.
That means neighbouring points on the retina project to neighbouring points in the nucleus.
So the spatial order of the visual field is preserved at this relay.
That said, the map is heavily distorted in favour of central vision.
Fifty per cent of the representation is the fovea.
This mirrors the dominance of the fovea in visual acuity.

## The six layers

Now for the structure itself.
The L G N is built from six layers of cell bodies, separated by intralaminar layers.
These intralaminar layers are also called koniocellular layers, and they take the input from K cells.
The six layers are numbered one to six, going from ventral to dorsal.
Each layer receives input from one eye only, and from one ganglion cell type only.
Because of that, the numbering tells you exactly which input sits where.
Layers one and two are the magnocellular layers, which receive input from M cells.
Layers three to six are the parvocellular layers, which receive input from P cells.
When it comes to the eyes, layers one, four and six receive the contralateral nasal hemiretina.
Layers two, three and five, by contrast, receive the ipsilateral temporal hemiretina.
As for where these layers send their output, each channel lands in its own place in the primary visual cortex.
The magnocellular layers, one and two, project to layer four C alpha.
The parvocellular layers, three to six, project to layer four C beta.
And the koniocellular layers project to the blobs of cortical layers two and three.
Each L G N channel therefore keeps its own cortical address.
In other words, the P, M and K streams remain segregated on arrival.

## Summary

To pull it all together, the lateral geniculate nucleus receives ninety per cent of retinal axons and relays the signal to the visual cortex.
It also keeps a retinotopic map in which half of the representation is the fovea.
Its six layers are numbered from ventral to dorsal.
Layers one and two are magnocellular and take input from M cells, while layers three to six are parvocellular and take input from P cells.
Layers one, four and six are driven by the contralateral nasal hemiretina, whereas layers two, three and five are driven by the ipsilateral temporal hemiretina.
Finally, K cells feed the koniocellular layers in between.
Picture the lateral geniculate nucleus, then, as a relay where each eye and each channel keeps firmly to its own layers.
