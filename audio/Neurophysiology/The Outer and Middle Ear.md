## The Outer and Middle Ear

The ear is the organ of hearing and balance, and it is divided into outer, middle and inner regions.
The outer and middle ear handle only the mechanical side of hearing.
They collect sound, protect the delicate parts, and deliver an amplified vibration to the inner ear, which is where transduction begins.
From here, we'll survey the ear as a whole, then look at the middle ear and its Eustachian tube, and finish with the auditory ossicles.

## Overview of the Ear

Taken as a whole, the ear falls into its three regions.
The outer ear consists of the visible pinna and the external auditory canal.
This canal funnels sound inwards to the tympanic membrane, also known as the eardrum.
That thin sheet marks the boundary with the middle ear.
Next comes the middle ear, which holds the three ossicles.
Further in lies the inner ear, containing the semicircular canals, the vestibule, the cochlea and the auditory nerve.
The round window sits at the base of the cochlea.
From the middle ear cavity, the Eustachian tube runs down towards the throat.
Back in the outer ear, the external auditory canal is lined with hairs and with wax-secreting glands.
This protective arrangement traps dust and debris before they can reach the tympanic membrane.

## The Middle Ear and the Eustachian Tube

Turning to the middle ear itself, we find an air-filled cavity sitting between the tympanic membrane and the inner ear.
Because it is filled with air, its pressure has to match the pressure outside.
Keeping the two matched is the job of the Eustachian tube.
This tube connects the cavity to the back of the nasal cavity, and it equalises pressure across the tympanic membrane.
Normally, the tube stays closed.
Chewing gum or changing the shape of the oral cavity pulls it open, though.
This lets air pass in or out, relieving the pressure difference across the eardrum.
Children are more prone to middle ear infection, and the reason lies in the tube.
Their shorter face leaves the tube at a flatter angle, and a flatter tube gets little help from gravity.
As a result, mucus drains poorly and pools in the cavity.
Ventilation on swallowing is also less effective.
That trapped fluid becomes a ready site for infection.
As the face lengthens with growth, the tube steepens, which is why the tendency largely resolves with age.

## The Auditory Ossicles

Inside the middle ear sit three auditory ossicles.
The first is the malleus, or hammer, followed by the incus, or anvil.
The third is the stapes, or stirrup.
Tiny synovial joints link them to one another.
Their task is to amplify vibrations and to transmit them from the tympanic membrane to the oval window.
The oval window is the point where the stapes meets the inner ear.
Let's trace the chain in order.
Sound waves travel down the external auditory canal and set the tympanic membrane vibrating.
The malleus, attached to the inner surface of the membrane, moves the incus and then the stapes.
In turn, the stapes pushes on the oval window, sending pressure waves through the cochlea.
In its natural position in the skull, the ear keeps this same layout.
The pinna and canal form the outer ear, and the ossicle chain is suspended in the air-filled middle ear cavity.
Deeper still, the coiled cochlea of the inner ear is embedded in bone.

## Summary

Pulling the threads together, the outer and middle ear solve a purely mechanical problem.
The canal gathers sound and protects the delicate parts.
The Eustachian tube keeps pressure balanced across the eardrum.
And the ossicle chain boosts the vibration before handing it to the oval window.
Converting that vibration into a neural signal is the work of the inner ear.
That is where the mechanical side of hearing comes to an end.
