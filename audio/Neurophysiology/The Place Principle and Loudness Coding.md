## The Place Principle and Loudness Coding

Sound has two perceptual dimensions, frequency and loudness, and the cochlea codes them by separate strategies.
Frequency is coded by the place principle.
Each frequency moves one particular stretch of the basilar membrane most strongly, so the position of the responding hair cells identifies the pitch.
Loudness, by contrast, is coded by how strongly and how widely the membrane responds, and by how hard the cochlea's own amplifier is driven.
Along the way, we'll cover the range the ear can hear, the mechanics behind the place principle, the travelling wave, and finally how loudness is coded.

## The hearing range and its loss with age

Starting with the range itself, a young ear hears from twenty to twenty thousand hertz.
With age, that range narrows to between fifty and five thousand hertz.
Yet the frequencies that actually matter for daily life span only a few hundred to a few thousand hertz.
They matter because this middle band carries speech.
Consequently, the older range still covers conversation, and the narrowing is far from incapacitating.
The loss itself reflects the progressive death of hair cells.

## The basis of the place principle

What makes the place principle work?
It rests on one mechanical fact, namely that the basilar membrane is not of equal thickness along its length.
Its thickness and rigidity decrease from the base towards the helicotrema, and as thickness decreases, stiffness decreases too.
In other words, the membrane is narrow and tight at the base, and wide and loose at the apex.
A structure built like this is frequency selective by its very construction.
The reason is that stiff tissue can follow rapid vibration, whereas loose tissue can follow only slow vibration.
As a result, high frequency sounds produce maximal motion at the base, and low frequency sounds produce it at the helicotrema.

## The travelling wave

Now, when the stapes sets the cochlear fluid moving, the basilar membrane does not vibrate all at once.
Instead, the disturbance runs along it from base to apex as a travelling wave.
How far the wave gets before it dies is decided by the stiffness gradient.
The wave grows as it leaves the base and reaches its maximum where the membrane's stiffness best matches the frequency.
Just beyond, it collapses, since there the membrane can no longer follow that frequency at all.
Take a high frequency wave first.
It meets membrane stiff enough to keep up with it right at the base, which is why it peaks there almost at once.
A low frequency wave, on the other hand, passes the stiff base without moving it much and travels on.
It peaks only where the membrane has grown loose enough to follow it, close to the helicotrema.
Between these extremes, every intermediate frequency finds its best matched region at its own point.
Once that point is reached, the peak stays fixed at that place for as long as the tone sounds.
This leads us to the place principle itself.
Each frequency of stimulation excites maximal motion at a specific position along the membrane, which is called its place.
The hair cells sitting at that place are driven hardest, and the brain reads pitch from whichever place is signalling most strongly.
Measurements of how far the membrane moves at each point along its length bear this out.
For a tone of one thousand six hundred hertz, the movement peaks close to the base.
At two hundred hertz, the peak lies further along, and at fifty hertz it sits near the apex.
Put simply, the lower the frequency, the further the wave travels before peaking.
As the wave advances, each point on the membrane reaches its own largest displacement over a whole cycle.
The curve that traces these largest displacements is called the envelope, and its peak is the place that encodes the frequency.
A xylophone offers a handy analogy.
Each of its bars resonates to one note, with one struck bar sounding four hundred and forty hertz.
In just the same way, each region of the membrane responds best to one frequency.
This means frequency selectivity belongs to the structure itself, rather than being computed later by the brain.
Mapped out along the membrane, the frequencies run in order, from twenty thousand hertz at the base to twenty hertz at the apex.
Four hundred and forty hertz falls between them.
With hair cells spread along the whole length, the entire audible range is laid out in order.
Each hair cell therefore reports the frequency of the place where it sits.
The same principle can be followed over time as well.
Because every wave enters at the base, a high frequency wave peaks almost at once and never reaches the apex.
A medium frequency wave peaks halfway along, while a low frequency wave travels nearly the full length before peaking.
Everything before the peak vibrates weakly, and everything beyond it stays still.
That way, each frequency drives only its own band of hair cells.

## Loudness coding

When it comes to loudness, three mechanisms sense it, and all of them grow with the strength of the vibration.
The first is the amplitude of response.
A louder sound displaces the basilar membrane further, so each responding hair cell has its stereocilia bent harder.
More potassium channels open and more potassium enters the cell, giving a larger depolarisation.
The receptor potential is graded, meaning its size tracks the strength of the stimulus.
The cell then releases more transmitter onto its afferent fibre, which fires faster.
The second mechanism is spatial summation.
A stronger vibration also drives a wider stretch of membrane past threshold, which means more hair cells respond.
Since each hair cell passes its signal to its own afferent fibres, recruiting more hair cells directly recruits more afferent fibres.
The brain reads their summed activity as a louder sound.
The third mechanism is the activation of the outer hair cells.
These cells amplify the membrane's motion continuously, and the harder a sound drives them, the larger the motion they feed back.
Their activation therefore compounds the first two mechanisms at once.
It enlarges each hair cell's response and widens the band of cells recruited.
Their boost is proportionally greatest for quiet sounds, whose vibration it raises up to a hundredfold.
This is what brings faint sounds above threshold at all.

## Summary

Pulling the threads together, the place principle rests on the membrane's gradient.
Thickness, rigidity and stiffness all fall from the base to the helicotrema.
That is why each frequency's travelling wave peaks at its own place, with high frequencies at the base and low frequencies at the helicotrema.
The hair cells at the peak report the pitch.
Onto this map, the young ear lays a range of twenty to twenty thousand hertz, which narrows with age to between fifty and five thousand hertz.
Loudness is coded separately.
It grows with the amplitude of each hair cell's response, and with the number of hair cells and afferent fibres recruited.
It also grows with the activation of the amplifying outer hair cells.
Those are the cochlea's two separate codes, one for pitch and one for loudness.
