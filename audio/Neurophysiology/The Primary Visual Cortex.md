## The Primary Visual Cortex

The primary visual cortex, known as V one, is visual area one, or Brodmann area seventeen.
It is the first area of the cortex to receive visual input.
Made up of six layers of cells, it takes its input from the lateral geniculate nucleus, or L G N, at layer four.
From many small pieces of information, it assembles the whole visual scene, much like a jigsaw.
In what follows, we'll cover how the six layers are laid out, the jigsaw idea, the inputs from the L G N, and the patches of cells called blobs.

## Layout of the six layers

To begin, the cortex is a sheet about three millimetres thick.
Its layers are counted from layer one at the surface down to layer six, just above the white matter.
The cells are also grouped into vertical cylinders that run through the whole sheet.
Each cylinder is a column, which is the stack of cells from all six layers at one spot.
Both descriptions hold at once, and a cell's job is set by its address.
Its layer sets what it does in the circuit, whereas its column sets which small piece of the scene it works on.
Within the sheet, axons and dendrites run vertically between the layers to connect them.
Each neuron also keeps most of its dendrites close to its own cell body.
Longer journeys use the white matter underneath, which is the myelinated cabling of the cortex.
Input arrives and output leaves through this white matter, and it is from there that the L G N axons ascend to reach layer four.

## The jigsaw idea

Now for the jigsaw idea itself.
The cortex assembles the whole visual scene from many small pieces of information, like a jigsaw.
No single piece of cortex deals with the scene as a whole.
Rather, each small columnar unit handles only a tiny fragment of it.
To return to the jigsaw, each piece on its own carries almost nothing of the picture.
The complete image appears only when every piece is in place.
In the same way, the perceived scene emerges only when the activity of all the small pieces is fitted together.
That is exactly what the columnar organisation of V one is built to do.
Assembly starts in V one, though it isn't finished there.
V one encodes the pieces and begins combining them within its columns, before passing the result on.
One route leads from V one through V two and V three to the middle temporal area, or M T.
A second route leads from V one through V two to V four.
The later areas add their own specialisations, so the complete scene is put together progressively across the visual areas.

## Inputs from the lateral geniculate nucleus

Turning to the inputs from the L G N, these enter in layer four.
Layer four has four sublayers, namely four A, four B, four C alpha and four C beta.
At this first stage, the two main cell streams stay anatomically separate.
Axons of M cells terminate on simple cells in four C alpha.
Axons of P cells, by contrast, terminate on simple cells in four C beta.
A third input runs alongside these two.
Axons from the intralaminar region of the L G N terminate in layers two and three, where they innervate the blobs.

## Blobs

That brings us to the blobs, which are small patches of cells in layers two and three of V one.
They receive the axons from the intralaminar region of the L G N.
This input carries colour information, especially from the short wavelength cones, or S cones, which are tied to blue light.
So the blobs are the first cortical stage of this colour pathway.
Seen from the side, each blob is a small peg that fills layers two and three, standing above the cells in layer four A.

## Summary

To sum up, V one is visual area one, Brodmann area seventeen.
It is a sheet about three millimetres thick with six layers, and it receives the L G N input in layer four.
It works like a jigsaw, assembling the whole visual scene from many small pieces of information handled by separate columnar units.
Within layer four, M cell axons end on simple cells in four C alpha, while P cell axons end on simple cells in four C beta.
Axons from the intralaminar region of the L G N end in layers two and three, where they innervate the blobs.
And with that, the first cortical pieces of the visual jigsaw are in place.
