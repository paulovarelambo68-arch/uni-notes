## The Sleep Cycle Across the Night and Across Life

Sleep unfolds in repeating cycles.
In each one, the brain descends from light sleep into deep slow wave sleep and back again.
Every pass ends with a period of rapid eye movement sleep, known as REM sleep.
Across life, this structure changes, with deep slow wave sleep giving way to lighter sleep and more frequent awakenings.
We'll follow one cycle through the night, see what marks out REM, and go through the brain waves of each stage and the hypnogram.
After that comes how the night is divided between the stages, and how the pattern shifts with age.

## The ninety minute cycle

Once we fall asleep, the brain first descends through the stages called N one and N two.
From there it sinks into the slow wave sleep of N three, and then it returns towards light sleep.
The full descent and return take about ninety minutes.
The first REM period begins about ninety minutes after falling asleep, once this first descent and return is complete.
From then on, REM recurs every sixty to ninety minutes, and each episode lasts about twenty minutes.

## The REM signature

Now for what makes REM stand out.
REM sleep breaks the rule that deeper sleep means slower waves.
Its electroencephalogram, or E E G, shows a faster, desynchronised rhythm of low amplitude, remarkably similar to the waking record.
Bear in mind, though, that this record is taken from the scalp, so it reflects only the cortex.
The hippocampus lies too deep to appear in it.
Recordings from the hippocampus itself show theta activity at four to eight hertz during REM.

## Reading the traces and the hypnogram

That brings us to the typical E E G trace of each stage, and to the hypnogram.
In the awake state, the E E G shows alpha waves.
N one shows smaller, slower theta waves.
N two is identified by a sleep spindle and a K complex.
Deeper still, N three shows the large, slow delta waves of deep sleep.
REM, in turn, returns to a fast, low amplitude rhythm.
As for the hypnogram, it is a graph that plots sleep stage against time across the whole night.
Over about eight hours, the trace steps down from wakefulness into N three and oscillates between depths.
Along the way it marks five REM periods in all, before the final awakening.

## Stage proportions in the young adult

Next, let's see how the young adult night is shared out between the stages.
N one takes about five per cent of it, and N two about forty-five per cent.
N three and REM take about twenty-five per cent each.
In other words, N two occupies the largest share, while N one is only a brief transition.
Across the night, sleep unfolds over about five cycles.
The descents into N three are longest in the first cycles.
REM episodes, by contrast, come later in the night and last longer each time.
This young adult pattern is the reference, and a personal sleep score or tracker should be compared against it.

## Sleep across life

Finally, let's turn to how sleep changes with age, comparing a young child with an elderly adult across the hours of the night.
The child falls quickly into long, uninterrupted plateaus of N three during the first hours.
The child also returns to REM at regular intervals, with almost no awakenings.
The elderly adult, on the other hand, spends little time in N three.
Their sleep rises repeatedly to wakefulness, so the night breaks into fragments, even though REM periods still appear.
The rule is simple.
As we age, we get less N three and more awakenings.

## Summary

To sum up, a night of sleep is built from cycles of about ninety minutes.
Each cycle runs from N one down to N three and back, before a REM episode of about twenty minutes.
During this episode, a desynchronised E E G, together with theta activity in the hippocampus, mimics wakefulness.
Slow wave sleep concentrates in the early cycles, while REM lengthens towards morning.
With age, N three shrinks and awakenings multiply.
And that is the shape of a night's sleep, from the first descent to the final awakening, and from childhood into old age.
