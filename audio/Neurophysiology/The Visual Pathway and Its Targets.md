## The Visual Pathway and Its Targets

The visual pathway is the route that ganglion cell axons follow from the retina into the brain.
Its targets are the structures where those axons terminate.
Its organisation follows a single rule, which is that each side of the brain is connected to the opposite half of the visual field.
The output of the pathway splits in two.
A large cortical route supports the conscious perception of vision.
Alongside it sits a small set of subcortical stations, meaning structures below the cortex, which drive automatic functions such as reflexes and daily rhythms.
We'll trace the fibres from the eye to their targets, and then see what happens when the pathway is damaged.

## The optic nerve and the optic chiasm

Our journey begins as the ganglion cell axons leave the eye, forming the optic nerve.
These axons meet at the optic chiasm, where a partial crossing takes place.
Fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross to the other side of the brain.
Fibres from the temporal half, on the other hand, stay on their own side.
Behind the chiasm, the fibres continue as the optic tract.
This sorting has a precise consequence.
Axons from the left half of each retina project in the left optic tract, carrying the representation of the right visual hemifield.
In the same way, the right optic tract carries the left hemifield.
Each tract therefore carries input from both eyes about the same hemifield.
As a result, the two retinal views of the same part of the world are brought together in one hemisphere, where they can be compared.
It is this arrangement that allows depth perception.

## Cortical and subcortical targets

Turning to the targets, the great majority of the output serves perception.
Ninety per cent of ganglion fibres synapse in the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus.
This nucleus relays the signal to the visual cortex, where the perception of vision takes place.
The remaining ten per cent of fibres terminate on subcortical targets, each with a distinct function.
The pretectum is responsible for the blinking response.
The superior colliculus deals with head and eye movements.
Finally, the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus looks after circadian rhythms.

## The pathway in the brain

Now let's put the whole route together.
The optic nerves run back from the eyes to the chiasm, where the nasal fibres cross.
Beyond the chiasm, the optic tracts sweep around the midbrain to reach the lateral geniculate nucleus on each side.
From there, the optic radiation fans out broadly through the white matter.
It ends in the striate cortex at the occipital pole, which is the first cortical station of vision.
Branching off this main route are the subcortical projections.
Small projections reach the hypothalamus for the regulation of circadian rhythms.
Others go to the pretectum, which provides reflex control of the pupil and lens.
This is the same reflex circuitry that lies behind the blinking response we met a moment ago.
A further branch reaches the superior colliculus, which orients the movements of the head and eyes.

## Visual field defects after lesions

That brings us to what happens when a lesion interrupts the pathway.
We'll consider three lesion sites, and in every case the logic follows from the crossing rule.
First, identify which fibres run at the lesion site.
Then remember that the nasal retina sees the temporal field, while the temporal retina sees the nasal field.
With those two facts in hand, the defect follows.
Let's begin with a lesion of one optic nerve.
It destroys every fibre coming from that retina before any crossing has occurred.
So the loss of vision is limited to the eye of origin.
If the left optic nerve is cut, for instance, the whole field of the left eye is lost, while the right eye sees normally.
It is a purely monocular blindness.
Next, take a lesion at the midline of the optic chiasm.
This destroys only the fibres that cross, which are those coming from the nasal half of each retina.
The uncrossed temporal fibres, meanwhile, are spared.
Because the nasal retina of each eye receives the temporal half of the visual field, both temporal hemifields are lost.
This defect is called bitemporal hemianopia.
The third site is one optic tract, where the fibres have already been sorted by the chiasm.
The left tract, for example, contains the uncrossed temporal fibres of the left eye together with the crossed nasal fibres of the right eye.
Both of these sets carry the right visual hemifield.
Cutting the left tract therefore blinds the right half of the visual field in both eyes.
This is known as a right homonymous hemianopia.
Past the tract, the pathway continues through the lateral geniculate body and the optic radiation, all the way to the visual cortex.
Here, the visual cortex means areas seventeen, eighteen and nineteen, that is, visual areas one to four, plus visual area five.
The same reasoning applies at every one of these stations.
That's because the sorting made at the chiasm, one hemifield per side, is kept unchanged up to the cortex.
Consequently, any lesion behind the chiasm produces a contralateral homonymous defect.
In other words, the opposite hemifield is lost in both eyes.

## Summary

Putting the pieces together, the chiasm sorts fibres so that each hemisphere receives the opposite hemifield.
Ninety per cent of fibres reach the lateral geniculate nucleus and the striate cortex for perception.
The other ten per cent feed the pretectum, the superior colliculus and the suprachiasmatic nucleus.
As for lesions, they read like the anatomy.
Nerve cuts blind one eye, and chiasm cuts remove both temporal fields.
Retrochiasmatic cuts, those behind the chiasm, erase the contralateral hemifield in both eyes.
And that completes our route, from the retina right through to the targets of the visual pathway.
