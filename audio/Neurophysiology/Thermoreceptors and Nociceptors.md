## Thermoreceptors and Nociceptors

Thermoreceptors and nociceptors are the free nerve endings of small diameter fibres.
These are either thinly myelinated A-delta fibres or unmyelinated C fibres.
Both kinds of receptor share a common property, which is that they are slowly adapting.
As a result, they keep signalling temperature or pain for as long as the stimulus persists.
Coming up are these free nerve endings, then the thermoreceptors and the nociceptors, and finally the ion channels that turn a stimulus into an electrical signal.

## 1. Free nerve endings

Unlike the encapsulated mechanoreceptors of touch, thermoreceptors and nociceptors are free nerve endings, with no specialised structure around them.
They also belong to the slow end of the fibre spectrum, running on small myelinated A-delta fibres or on unmyelinated C fibres.
Because they adapt slowly, their signal does not fade during a maintained stimulus.
That is exactly what you want from a system that warns about tissue danger.

## 2. Thermoreceptors

Now for the thermoreceptors, which split into two populations according to fibre type.
Cold receptors run on A-delta fibres, and they are more numerous than warm receptors.
Warm receptors, on the other hand, run on C fibres.
So the two innocuous temperature qualities are already separated at the level of the peripheral fibre.
Cold travels on the faster of the two slow fibre types, while warmth travels on the slowest.

## 3. Nociceptors

Nociceptors follow the same fibre division, and this explains the classic double sensation of pain after an injury.
A-delta fibres carry fast pain.
This is the immediate, sharp ouch you feel the moment you hurt yourself.
C fibres, by contrast, carry dull, throbbing pain.
This is the delayed, lingering ache that follows.
In other words, the same injury, signalled by two fibre types, gives two pain qualities.
These arrive one after the other, because the two fibres have different conduction velocities.

## 4. Molecular transducers: TRPV1, TRPM8 and PIEZO2

That brings us to the molecular transducers, and first to transduction itself.
Transduction is the conversion of a physical stimulus, here temperature or mechanical force, into an electrical signal in the sensory ending.
The channels responsible are membrane proteins whose pore opens in response to the stimulus itself, with no intermediary.
Ions flow through the open pore, and the ending depolarises.
The frequency of action potentials fired is then roughly proportional to the size of that depolarisation.
Since intensity is coded by firing frequency, weak stimuli produce a few action potentials per second.
Intense ones reach a few hundred per second at most, a ceiling set by the refractory period of the axon.
The exact figures, however, go beyond the slides.
Let's look at three of these channels.
The first belongs to the transient receptor potential, or T R P, family, and it is called T R P V one.
Its job is to detect noxious heat.
Its four subunits form a central cation-selective pore.
The channel is expressed in both A-delta and C nociceptive endings.
Heat opens it by displacing membrane lipids associated with the channel.
Its activation threshold, around forty-three degrees Celsius, matches the threshold at which heat becomes painful.
Beyond that, its roles extend to core body temperature, to inflammatory, neuropathic and visceral pain, and to protective reflexes.
The second channel, T R P M eight, is another member of the T R P family, and it detects cold.
Cold temperatures open it, giving cold receptors their sensitivity.
The third, Piezo two, handles touch and proprioception.
This mechanosensitive channel has an unusual three-subunit structure and large extracellular blades.
Tension in the surrounding membrane opens its pore.
In this way, it converts mechanical deformation into current in touch afferents and proprioceptors.
Its wider roles include mechanical pain, urination, respiration, blood pressure and skeletal remodelling.
Finally, the discovery of these receptors for temperature and touch earned David Julius and Ardem Patapoutian the twenty twenty-one Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

## Summary

Pulling it all together, thermoreceptors and nociceptors are the receptors themselves.
They are slowly adapting free nerve endings on A-delta or C fibres that detect temperature and pain.
The molecular transducers, by contrast, are not separate receptors.
Rather, they are the ion channels expressed within those endings, the machinery that opens in response to heat, cold or membrane tension.
That machinery turns the physical stimulus into an electrical signal.
T R P V one, T R P M eight and Piezo two are only some of these channels.
A wider family divides the whole range between them, with each channel gated at its own threshold.
For example, innocuous warmth is transduced by other channels, below the forty-three degrees Celsius at which T R P V one opens and heat becomes painful.
In short, the fibre is the detector, and the channel is what makes detection possible.
Which channel opens determines which quality you perceive.
And that completes thermoreceptors and nociceptors, from the free nerve endings down to the channels inside them.
