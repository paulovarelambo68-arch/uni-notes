
>Adaptive immunity relies on two kinds of ==lymphocyte==: B cells, which ==mature in the bone marrow== and ==make antibodies (humoral immunity)==, and T cells, which ==mature in the thymus== and provide ==cell-mediated immunity==. It follows ==three Rs: Recognise, Respond, Remember==. In ==clonal selection==, the antigen ==selects the rare pre-existing lymphocytes whose receptor fits== and these ==divide into a clone of effector and memory cells==, a process confined to ==T and B cells==.

### 1. The adaptive branch and its two cell types

As [[Immune System Overview – Functions and the Two Branches]] showed, adaptive, or acquired, immunity is the branch we are ==not born with==: it ==develops (adapts) throughout a lifetime==, is ==slower to respond than the innate system==, recognises antigens (the molecules its receptors bind) with ==high specificity==, improves with each contact and ==has a memory function==.

Its cells are the ==lymphocytes== ([[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]]), white blood cells of two kinds that mature, meaning become fully working cells, in different places and defend the body in different ways.

| Feature | B lymphocytes (B cells) | T lymphocytes (T cells) |
|---|---|---|
| Mature in | ==Bone marrow== | ==Thymus== |
| Role | ==Produce antibodies== | ==Cell-mediated immunity== |

These roles are the two arms of adaptive immunity. Antibodies, proteins that bind one particular antigen ([[B Cells and Antibodies]]), work dissolved in the body fluids, once called humours, hence ==humoral (antibody-mediated) immunity==. In ==cell-mediated immunity==, the T cells themselves act directly on other cells.

### 2. The three Rs

Whichever cell is involved, adaptive immunity follows the ==three Rs==.

- First, each lymphocyte must ==Recognise== its antigen: every cell has a ==specific receptor for a particular antigen==, and recognition is followed by ==clonal selection==.
- Then the lymphocytes ==Respond==, by ==cytokine secretion== (small signalling proteins, see [[Cytokines]]), ==antibody production== and ==direct cell killing by T cells==.
- Finally the system must ==Remember==, through long-lived ==memory cells==.

These three Rs are the scaffold of Immunology II: Recognise is explained below, Respond in [[T Cells and Antigen Presentation]] and [[B Cells and Antibodies]], and Remember in [[Immunological Memory – Primary and Secondary Responses]].

### 3. Clonal selection

Recognition rests on clonal selection, proposed by ==Burnet in 1957== (spelt Burnett on the slide). ==Each lymphocyte has receptors to bind a single antigen==, which defines its specificity. Since lymphocytes differ in specificity, the ==lymphocyte repertoire==, meaning the full set of different lymphocytes in the body, is ==extremely diverse==. So there are ==very few cells of any one specificity==, and each is ==highly specific==.

When one of these rare cells ‘finds’ its antigen, it ==binds and can become activated==, that is, switched on. ==The activated cell divides rapidly to make many copies, a clone== of identical cells sharing its receptor. This ==clonal expansion== provides the ==large number of antigen-specific lymphocytes== needed to keep pace with a multiplying microbe. The clone contains ==effector cells==, which do the job now, and ==memory cells==, which stay for the future.

The name stresses selection rather than instruction, because the antigen does not instruct a cell to make a matching receptor. ==The receptors exist before the antigen is ever met==, and the antigen ==selects, out of millions of pre-existing lymphocytes, the few whose receptor fits==, so only those expand.

Because it depends on these single-antigen receptors, ==clonal selection occurs for both T and B cells==, the only cells that carry them. Innate cells such as ==monocytes, neutrophils and basophils do not undergo it==, since all cells of a type carry identical non-specific receptors, leaving nothing to select.

### Summary

>Adaptive (acquired) immunity is slow but highly specific, improves with each contact and remembers. ==B cells mature in the bone marrow and make antibodies (humoral immunity)==, while ==T cells mature in the thymus and act directly on other cells (cell-mediated immunity)==. Responses follow the ==three Rs==: recognise through specific receptors, respond with cytokines, antibodies and killing by T cells, and remember through memory cells. In ==clonal selection (Burnet, 1957)== the antigen ==selects, from an extremely diverse repertoire, the rare pre-existing cells whose receptor fits==, and these ==divide rapidly into a clone of effector and memory cells==. This happens in ==T and B cells, never in innate cells== such as monocytes, neutrophils and basophils.
