
> ==Sinus arrhythmia== (==heart rate faster when breathing in, slower when breathing out==) is normal. ==AV-nodal block==, ectopic beats (==PVCs==) and ==re-entry== are faults, and re-entry can cause fatal ==ventricular fibrillation==, which a shock stops by ==depolarising every heart cell at once==. A raised ==ST segment== marks a heart attack.

### 1. A normal irregularity: sinus arrhythmia

The SA node is the heart's natural pacemaker, and the vagus nerve slows it by releasing acetylcholine ([[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate]]). In ==sinus arrhythmia== this braking follows the breath. During expiration, ==lung stretch receptors trigger a rise in vagal activity, which slows the heart==, and during inspiration ==vagal activity falls, so heart rate increases==. This irregular rhythm (arrhythmia) is ==perfectly normal==. On the ECG the beats bunch together on breathing in and spread out on breathing out.

### 2. AV-nodal block, three degrees

On the ECG, the P wave is the atria firing (depolarising) and the QRS complex is the ventricles firing. The PR interval is the delay between them, mostly spent in the AV node, the only electrical bridge between atria and ventricles ([[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System]]). In normal sinus rhythm ==every QRS follows a P wave== and ==the PR interval is 0.12 to 0.20 s==.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 21.46.44 2.png]]

Mild injury to the AV node causes ==first degree AV-nodal block==. The node still passes every signal, but slowly, so ==the PR interval is longer than 0.20 s== while every P wave keeps its QRS. In the figure, the blue PR bars are longer than the normal red ones.

More injury causes ==second degree AV-nodal block==. The node passes on ==only some of the signals from the atria== (supraventricular depolarisations), so ==some P waves have a QRS and others do not==. Each purple box holds ==three P waves but only two QRS complexes (3:2)==, and the purple-arrowed P wave is the dropped beat. ==Other ratios, such as 2:1, occur==.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 21.47.14.png]]

Complete block is ==third degree AV-nodal block==. The ==AV node conducts no signals==, so ==the atria and ventricles are electrically disconnected==. The atria keep their own pace, so ==P waves stay regular==. A ==secondary pacemaker==, a slower backup below the block, drives the ventricles, so ==QRS complexes are regular but slow==. The two rhythms ignore each other (purple bars for P waves, black bars for QRS), so the PR interval keeps changing.

To grade a strip:

1. Measure the PR interval.
2. Check that every P wave has a QRS.
3. Check whether P and QRS are related at all.
4. Name the degree: long PR is first, dropped beats second, no relation third.

### 3. Premature ventricular contractions

A premature ventricular contraction (PVC) is an ==ectopic beat==, meaning it starts in the wrong place. ==Beats normally start in the atria, at the SA node==, but during ==acute myocardial ischaemia== (poor blood flow to heart muscle) a beat can ==start in the ventricle== and disturb the rhythm.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 21.48.04 1.png]]

The boxed PVC ==has no P wave==, because ==it started in the ventricle==. It is ==premature==: the next beat was due at the green arrow. Its ==shape, amplitude and duration are abnormal==, because the signal ==skips the normal fast conducting pathways== and creeps slowly through ordinary muscle, so the complex is wide. After the PVC comes a long diastolic (filling) gap, the ==compensatory pause==, because the next signal from the SA node finds the ventricles still refractory and is ignored. The pause lets the ventricles ==fill more==, so the next beat is stronger ([[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling’s Law and Contractility]]).

### 4. Re-entry

In re-entry, another cause of extra beats, one signal loops back and fires the same tissue twice.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 21.48.51.png|400]]

In normal tissue (top drawing), a signal splits around a patch that cannot conduct. The two halves meet at point d and ==cancel each other out==, because each runs into muscle the other has just fired, which is refractory (unable to fire again yet, [[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period]]). Injured tissue (grey, bottom drawing) ==conducts only in the reverse direction==, like a one-way street. The lower half is stopped there, while the upper half goes round and ==comes back through the injured patch== (blue arrow). Crossing damaged tissue is slow, so when the signal reaches area G, G has recovered and ==fires again, too early==. This ectopic beat repeats if the signal keeps circling.

### 5. Ventricular fibrillation and defibrillation

Uncontrolled re-entry produces ==rapid, uncoordinated excitations== of the ventricles, called ==ventricular fibrillation (VF)==. The muscle quivers instead of pumping, so ==there is no cardiac output== and ==death follows within minutes==. VF is often the fatal result of ==myocardial ischaemia, anaesthetic overdose or electrocution==.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 21.49.57.png]]

A ==manual external defibrillator== delivers ==an electrical shock through pads or paddles placed on the chest==. The shock ==depolarises every heart muscle cell at once==, like a reset button. A looping signal can only run into recovered tissue, so with every cell refractory together, all the loops die out. As the cells recover, the fastest pacemaker, the ==SA node, takes over again== ([[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate]]).

### 6. The ischaemic marker on the trace

Ischaemia, poor coronary blood flow ([[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow]]), also shows on the ECG, which is made of waves (P, Q, R, S, T) with segments between them. The ==ST segment runs from the end of the S wave to the start of the T wave==. It is normally flat, because all ventricular cells are then equally activated and the ECG sees no difference. ==After a myocardial infarction (heart attack)==, damaged cells cannot stay activated, so ==the ST segment is elevated==.

### Summary

> ==Sinus arrhythmia== is normal: vagal braking rises during expiration, so ==heart rate falls when breathing out and rises when breathing in==. In AV-nodal block the signals from the atria are ==delayed (first degree, PR over 0.20 s)==, ==partly blocked (second degree, for example 3:2)== or ==fully blocked (third degree)==, which leaves regular P waves unrelated to slow, regular QRS complexes. A ==PVC== starts in the ventricle, so it has ==no P wave==, comes early, looks ==wide== and is followed by a ==compensatory pause== that boosts filling. ==Re-entry== through one-way injured tissue fires tissue again too early and can become ==VF, with no cardiac output==. A shock ==depolarises every cell at once==, so the loops die out and the SA node takes over, and ==ST elevation== marks a heart attack.
