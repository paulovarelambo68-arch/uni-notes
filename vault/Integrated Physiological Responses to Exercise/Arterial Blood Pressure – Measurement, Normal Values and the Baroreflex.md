
> With each beat ==the aorta stretches to store blood and recoils between beats==, so flow is continuous. ==Systolic pressure is read when the Korotkoff sounds appear and diastolic when they disappear==, and ==mean arterial pressure (MAP) = diastolic + 1/3 (systolic - diastolic)==, normally ==95 to 100 mmHg==. ==Systolic rises with age==, and ==baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch== hold MAP steady through ==the SA node, heart muscle, arterioles and veins==.

### 1. From intermittent ejection to continuous flow

Blood enters the aorta at ==up to 1.5 to 2.0 m/s (600 mL/s)==, but averaged over the whole beat it moves at only ==0.20 m/s==. ==About two-thirds of the stroke volume (SV) is ejected in the first third of systole==. The vessels downstream cannot take blood that fast, so ==the aorta stretches to hold 70 to 80% of the SV==, and only ==20 to 30% flows straight on==. In diastole ==the stretched aorta recoils==. This pushes the stored blood into the next stretch of artery, which expands and then recoils in turn. In this way ==elastic recoil turns stop-start ejection into continuous, pulsing flow==. This is called the windkessel, or air-chamber, effect.

### 2. Measuring arterial pressure

Arterial pressure is measured with a sphygmomanometer and a stethoscope. The sphygmomanometer is ==an inflatable cuff on the upper arm attached to a manometer== (pressure gauge), and ==the stethoscope is placed over the brachial artery== below the cuff. The measurement has four steps.

1. ==The cuff is inflated above systolic pressure==. This squashes the artery shut, so flow and the pulse below the cuff stop.
2. The cuff is let down to just below systolic. Now the peak of each beat forces the artery open briefly, and the turbulent (swirling) spurt of blood makes a tapping ==Korotkoff sound==. ==Systolic pressure is the cuff pressure at which sounds are first heard==.
3. Once the cuff is below diastolic, the artery stays open throughout the beat, and flow turns smooth and silent. ==Diastolic pressure is the cuff pressure at which the sounds disappear==. The sounds become muffled a few mmHg before they disappear.
4. In some healthy people the sounds persist well below true diastolic pressure, so their diastolic pressure cannot be measured accurately.

Readings are given in ==mmHg because mercury is 13.5 times denser than water or blood==, which keeps the column short. They are ==taken at heart level==, with the upper arm by the side. Venous pressure is low enough to measure directly. In intensive care, a saline-filled tube in a large vein reads the ==central venous pressure (CVP)==, which is the pressure where the great veins enter the heart. It is normally ==6 to 11 mmHg==.

### 3. Systolic, diastolic, pulse pressure and the mean

The two readings are the peak and trough of each pressure wave. ==Systolic is the highest pressure, reached during ejection==, and ==diastolic is the lowest, reached at the end of diastole==. The difference between them is the ==pulse pressure (systolic - diastolic)==, so a reading of 125/80 has a pulse pressure of 45 mmHg. This swing fades as blood moves into smaller vessels, until ==arterioles and capillaries have an almost steady pressure== ([[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles]]). Inside the ventricle, pressure falls almost to 0 mmHg in diastole. Yet ==arterial pressure stays near 80==, because ==the closed aortic valve traps the blood in the arteries==, where the recoiling walls keep squeezing it.

Averaged over a beat, the pressure wave gives the ==mean arterial pressure (MAP)==, which is ==the pressure that drives blood round the body==. From a reading, ==MAP = diastolic + 1/3 pulse pressure==. This is ==not the simple average, because at rest diastole lasts about two-thirds of each beat==, so pressure spends longer near its low value. For example, 125/80 gives 80 + 45/3 = ==95 mmHg==, not 102.5.

### 4. Normal values and age

In resting adults, the normal values are ==diastolic 70 to 84, systolic 110 to 139 and MAP 95 to 100 mmHg==. Pressure creeps up with age. A typical reading is ==110/70 mmHg at 20 and 140/80 at 60==, which is just past the upper limit of normal.

The figure plots brachial artery pressure (mmHg) against age (years). Men are shown as solid lines and women as dashed lines. ==Systolic climbs throughout life==, ==diastolic rises until about 50 and then falls==, and ==the mean rises moderately==. The mean rises because ==TPR rises with age==. The gap between systolic and diastolic depends on ==compliance==, which is how easily the aorta stretches to take each SV, and with age ==the aorta stiffens==. A stiffer aorta has two effects. First, each beat pushes pressure higher, which raises systolic. Second, less stored blood remains to hold pressure up between beats, so diastolic falls. After about 50, this second effect outweighs the rise in TPR, which is why diastolic starts to fall. That ==fall may reduce coronary blood flow==, because the left coronary artery fills mainly in diastole ([[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow]]). Over 24 hours, ==pressure dips at night, and this dip is often missing in hypertension== ([[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment]]).

### 5. The baroreceptor reflex

Since CO = SV x HR,  ==MAP = SV x HR x TPR==. The body can therefore keep pressure steady by adjusting any of these three factors. Rearranged, the equation gives ==HR = MAP / (SV x TPR)==. ==Short-term control needs information on arterial pressure and blood volume==. ==Pressure is sensed by baroreceptors==, which are nerve endings that fire when the artery wall stretches.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 16.44.40.png|437]]

The red circles mark the two sites of the baroreceptors. ==The carotid sinus== is a slight widening where the common carotid artery forks in the neck, and it signals through the ==glossopharyngeal (IX) nerve==. ==The aortic arch== signals through the ==vagus (X) nerve==. Both send their signals to the ==cardiovascular centre in the medulla==. The SA node, the heart's pacemaker ([[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate]]), senses nothing. It only receives the reflex's commands.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 16.45.02.png|406]]

The figure shows three arterial pressure traces: lower, normal and elevated. Under each trace, every vertical line is one carotid sinus nerve impulse. ==The higher the pressure, the faster the firing==, and the impulses come in bursts on each upstroke of pressure.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 16.45.32.png|440]]

The cardiovascular centre (at the top of the figure) receives this signal. Through ==sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves==, it drives the ==four effector organs shown in the boxes: SA node, cardiac muscle, arterioles and veins==. The arrows show how each effector acts on pressure through HR, SV or TPR. Together they form a ==negative-feedback loop==, which pushes any change in pressure back towards normal. ==The arterioles of the lungs are not effectors==, because they are not part of TPR.

When MAP rises, the baroreceptors fire faster, and the centre ==raises parasympathetic and lowers sympathetic activity==. This acts on each effector:
- At the heart, ==the SA node slows, lowering HR==.
- ==Contractility falls==, so ==SV falls==. Contractility is the strength of contraction at a given filling ([[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling's Law and Contractility]]).
- ==The arterioles relax (vasodilation), lowering TPR==.
- The veins, which hold most of the blood, also relax and hold more of it. As a result, ==less blood returns to the heart== (venous return falls). The ventricle then fills less, so its ==end-diastolic volume (EDV)== falls, and it ejects less.

==Lower HR and SV reduce CO==, and with TPR also lower, MAP falls.

When MAP falls, for example after a haemorrhage or on standing up, the loop runs in reverse. ==HR and contractility rise==, ==arterioles constrict== to raise TPR, and ==veins constrict== to return more blood to the heart.

Standing up shows how this works. When you stand, the column of blood below the heart ==adds about 50 mmHg to pressures in the thigh==. So ==a femoral artery reads about 170/130 when the brachial artery reads 120/80==. Blood ==pools in the easily stretched leg veins==, which lowers ==venous return and CO==. The reflex responds, and it is helped by ==valves in the veins and the skeletal-muscle pump==, in which contracting leg muscles squeeze blood upward. When you are standing, the carotid sinus, which guards the brain's blood supply, sits above the heart and reads a lower pressure.

Lying down suddenly ==reverses all this==. The legs lose their extra 50 mmHg, and ==venous return and CO rise==. The carotid sinus is now level with the heart, so it ==senses a higher pressure==. The reflex therefore ==slows the heart and dilates vessels==, and pressure at heart level barely changes.

The reflex handles only quick changes. ==If pressure stays high for a few days, the baroreceptors reset== to the new level and stop opposing it. Something else must therefore set pressure in the long run, and that job falls to ==the kidneys, which control blood volume==. When pressure is high, the kidneys ==excrete more salt and water (pressure diuresis)==. Blood volume then falls, and so do EDV, SV, CO and MAP. When pressure is low, the kidneys retain salt and water, and the chain runs the other way. Pressure settles where ==salt and water output equals intake== ([[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment]]).

### Summary

> ==The aorta stores 70 to 80% of each stroke volume and recoils in diastole==, turning stop-start ejection into continuous flow. With a cuff, ==systolic is the pressure at which the Korotkoff sounds first appear and diastolic the pressure at which they disappear==. ==Pulse pressure is their difference==, and ==MAP = diastolic + 1/3 pulse pressure== because diastole lasts two-thirds of the resting beat. Normal resting values are ==110 to 139 over 70 to 84 mmHg, with a MAP of 95 to 100==. With age, ==systolic rises as the aorta stiffens, while diastolic falls after about 50==. ==Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch== report to the medulla, which answers a rise in MAP by ==slowing the SA node, reducing contractility and dilating arterioles and veins==, and answers a fall by doing the reverse. Lying down suddenly raises venous return and the pressure at the carotid sinus, so ==the reflex slows the heart and dilates vessels== and pressure stays almost level, while ==long-term control belongs to the kidneys==.
