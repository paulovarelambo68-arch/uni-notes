
> Gap junctions make the ventricle's muscle cells act as one electrical unit, a ==functional syncytium==. At rest each cell sits at ==about -90 mV==, close to the ==potassium equilibrium potential (-94 mV)==. When it fires, ==sodium entering== makes a fast spike, ==calcium entering== holds a ==plateau at 0 to -20 mV== and ==potassium leaving== ends it. Because the sodium channels are locked shut during the plateau, the cell cannot fire again for ==about 200 ms==, so heart muscle ==cannot be tetanised==.

### 1. A functional syncytium

For the heart to pump, its muscle cells must contract together, so the electrical signal that triggers contraction, the ==action potential==, has to reach every cell. The cells of the myocardium (heart muscle) are interwoven, may branch at either end, and are joined where they meet by ==intercalated discs==. These contain ==gap junctions==, tiny tunnels that let current flow straight from one cell into the next. The cells therefore behave electrically like one giant cell, a ==functional syncytium==, so ==a stimulus arising anywhere in the ventricle leads to complete contraction of both chambers (all-or-none contraction)==. The route the wave takes is set by the conducting system ([[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System]]).

### 2. The resting membrane

Every cell has a voltage across its membrane, the ==membrane potential==, measured inside relative to outside in millivolts (mV). In the contractile cells of the atria and ventricles (the heart's chambers), it rests at ==about -90 mV==, meaning the ==inside is negative==. A rise towards zero or above is called ==depolarisation==, and a fall back towards -90 mV is ==repolarisation==.

This voltage depends on three positive ions. Potassium (K+) is far more concentrated inside the cell (about 140 against 4 mmol/L), while sodium (Na+) and calcium (Ca2+) are more concentrated outside. Ions can only cross the membrane through ==membrane channels==. How easily an ion crosses is its ==conductance (G)==, defined as ==G = 1/R==, where R is the membrane's resistance to that ion. When the conductance for potassium, sodium or calcium (GK, GNa or GCa) rises, that ion flows across, and this flow of charge is a current, written iK, iNa or iCa.

Because all three ions are positive, one rule tells you what a current does. ==K+ leaving makes the inside more negative==, while ==Na+ or Ca2+ entering makes it more positive==. So an inward current depolarises the cell, and an outward current repolarises it.

At rest, ==permeability to Ca2+ is very low== and ==permeability to K+ is high==, because many potassium channels are open. This resting potassium current is called ==iK1==. ==Permeability to Na+ is about 10% of that to K+==, which lets a small ==inward background current (ib)== trickle in.

So the resting potential is ==mainly due to two things: the high K+ concentration inside the cell and the high permeability to K+==. Potassium leaks out, taking positive charge with it, and the inside turns negative, which pulls K+ back. At one voltage the outward push of the concentration difference and the inward pull of the charge balance exactly, and K+ stops moving overall. That voltage is the ion's ==equilibrium potential (E)==, and for potassium ==EK is -94 mV==. The resting potential ==sits close to EK== because the resting membrane passes mostly K+, and the small sodium leak (ib) keeps it a little less negative, at -90 mV. In working heart muscle this resting potential is ==stable==, staying flat until excitation arrives, unlike the voltage of pacemaker cells, which drifts upwards between beats ([[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate]]).

### 3. The five phases

When excitation arrives through the gap junctions, the cell fires its action potential. This is divided into ==five phases==, numbered so that the upstroke is phase 0 and rest is phase 4.

- ==Phase 4== is the resting membrane potential, the flat line between beats, held by iK1 and ib.
- ==Phase 0== is the upstroke, a sudden jump to a positive peak.
- ==Phase 1== is early repolarisation, a small dip just after the peak.
- ==Phase 2== is the plateau, a long, almost flat stretch near 0 mV.
- ==Phase 3== is late repolarisation, the fall back to rest.

The cell leaves rest when incoming excitation lifts the voltage to the ==threshold of about -65 mV==. There, ==voltage-sensitive fast sodium channels open very quickly==, so the membrane's ==permeability to sodium suddenly rises== and Na+ floods in (==iNa==), causing the depolarisation. Within ==1 to 2 ms== the cell loses its negative charge and ==overshoots to about +40 mV==. It stops short of the sodium equilibrium potential because ==potassium is still flowing out== at the same time.

The peak is brief, for two reasons. The fast sodium channels are ==self-inactivating==, meaning they shut themselves a moment after opening, so GNa falls. Then, within a couple of milliseconds, a short-lived potassium outflow called the ==transient outward current (ito)== ==repolarises the membrane by a few millivolts==, which is phase 1.

Instead of falling back to rest, the voltage then holds the ==plateau==, phase 2, a ==feature unique to cardiac muscle==, just below the peak at ==about 0 to -20 mV==. It is produced by ==a small but sustained inward Ca2+ current== through ==voltage-operated L-type calcium channels==, L standing for long-lasting, which is why the current is labelled iCa-L. These channels ==begin to open as the upstroke passes about -30 to -35 mV==, but ==slowly compared with the fast sodium channels==, so their current takes over only after the spike. The ==inward Ca2+ current almost balances the outward K+ current==, so the voltage stays nearly flat.

Late in the plateau, ==the calcium channels begin to inactivate==, and part of the inward current is then carried by ==sodium entering through the sodium-calcium (Na-Ca) exchanger==. This carrier ==swaps three Na+ in for one Ca2+ out==, bringing in three positive charges and removing two. The result is a ==net inward current==, which sustains the late plateau while clearing calcium from the cell.

In phase 3, as the calcium channels close, ==potassium conductance (GK) increases==. K+ leaves the cell as ==iK==, and the membrane ==repolarises back to rest==.

### 4. Why the plateau is long

The long plateau matters for two reasons. First, it keeps ==the cell refractory, meaning it cannot be excited again, for 200 to 400 ms==, while ==its contraction lasts only 200 to 250 ms==. A new contraction cannot start until the last is over, so ==contractions can never fuse==. Such fusion, called ==tetanus==, happens in skeletal muscle when rapid stimuli start each contraction before the previous one has relaxed. A heart in tetanus could never relax to refill with blood, which ==would be fatal==.

Second, ==the plateau not only starts contraction but also sets its strength==, because the calcium entering during it is what triggers contraction, so ==more calcium entry gives a stronger contraction== ([[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation]]).

### 5. Refractory periods and the sodium-channel gates

The refractory time has two parts. During the plateau, while the voltage is still near 0 mV, ==the heart is completely inexcitable==, so no stimulus, however strong, can trigger a new action potential. This is the ==absolute refractory period, about 200 ms==. Excitability then ==returns gradually during repolarisation, once the voltage is back to about -40 mV==. This gradual recovery is the ==relative refractory period, about 50 ms==, during which only a ==stronger stimulus than normal== works and the response is ==smaller==.

In the figure, the red box covers the upstroke and plateau of a normal action potential, marking the absolute refractory period, and the short double arrow under the late downslope marks the relative refractory period. The extra traces on the right are second action potentials triggered at different moments. The earlier they start, the smaller and slower they are, and only one triggered after full repolarisation is normal.

The cause is the fast sodium channel, which has ==two gates== in a row, like two doors, so sodium passes only when both are open. The ==m gate (activation gate)== opens when the membrane depolarises. The ==h gate (inactivation gate)== shuts shortly afterwards and ==does not reopen until the membrane repolarises==. 

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 13.30.52.png|424]]

The figure follows one channel round this cycle, with + and - showing which side of the membrane is positive. At rest (bottom) the channel is ==closed but excitable==, with the m gate shut and the h gate open. During the upstroke (top left) ==both gates are open== and sodium flows in. During the plateau (top right) the h gate has swung shut, so the channel is ==inactivated==. It becomes excitable again only after repolarisation.

This explains both refractory periods. During the plateau the h gates are shut, so ==the sodium channels are inactivated== and no stimulus can open them. Once the membrane repolarises past about -40 mV, some h gates reopen. A normal stimulus cannot open enough channels to overcome the outward K+ current, but ==a stronger stimulus can==, and because fewer channels take part, ==the upstroke is smaller and slower==. The same recovery rule is why an electrical wave that loops back on itself (re-entry) can only re-excite muscle that has recovered ([[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation]]).

### Summary

> Gap junctions make heart muscle a ==functional syncytium== that contracts ==all-or-none==. High potassium permeability holds the resting cell at ==about -90 mV==, near ==EK (-94 mV)==. From the ==threshold of -65 mV==, fast sodium channels drive the upstroke to ==+40 mV==, ito trims the peak, ==L-type calcium current balances potassium outflow on the plateau==, and rising potassium conductance repolarises the cell. Because the ==h gates keep the sodium channels inactivated== until repolarisation, the cell is ==absolutely refractory for about 200 ms== and ==relatively refractory for about 50 ms==, so the heart ==cannot be tetanised==, and plateau calcium sets the strength of each beat.
