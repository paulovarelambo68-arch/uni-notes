
>Repeated training makes the immune system and neuroendocrine response adapt, and ==the result depends on intensity and recovery==. Moderate training with adequate recovery ==lowers baseline inflammation and improves immune regulation==, whereas chronic high-intensity training ==suppresses several immune functions and raises inflammatory cytokines==. Athletes are not immunocompromised, as ==they typically suffer only upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs)==, but ==training before full recovery may lead to chronic immunosuppression==.

### 1. From repeated bouts to adaptation

Chronic exercise means training repeated over months and years, not the single acute bout of the previous notes. Each bout mobilises immune cells into the blood and redistributes them to the tissues ([[Mechanisms of Exercise-Induced Immune Changes]]), and its repetition ==leads to adaptations in the immune system and the neuroendocrine (stress-hormone) response==, just as repeated training adapts muscle and heart.

### 2. Chronic moderate exercise with adequate recovery

At moderate intensity with adequate recovery, the adaptation leaves ==blood leukocyte (white blood cell) number unchanged==, so the benefit lies in how immune cells work. ==Immune cell trafficking becomes more efficient==, meaning cells move between blood and tissues more readily, and ==innate and adaptive regulation improves==.

Part of the better regulation comes from ==increased T cells, including Tregs==, the regulatory T cells that suppress immune responses ([[T Cells and Antigen Presentation]]), because more brakes keep responses from overshooting. Cytokines, the immune system's signalling proteins ([[Cytokines]]), are rebalanced too, as ==baseline pro-inflammatory cytokines decrease and anti-inflammatory cytokines increase==, which ==reduces chronic low-grade inflammation==, a mild, persistent, body-wide inflammation. The result is ==lower baseline (resting) inflammation, seen as a lower level of C-reactive protein (CRP)==, the liver protein that marks inflammation ([[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP]]).

Training also brings ==slower age-related immune decline, that is less immunosenescence==, the gradual weakening of immune function with age, and ==maintained or increased salivary IgA==, the antibody abundant in saliva ([[B Cells and Antibodies]]).

A sample question asks which option is NOT associated with such training: the odd one out is ==higher baseline pro-inflammatory cytokines==, because the opposite is true.

### 3. Chronic high-intensity exercise

Chronic high-intensity training reverses much of this. The catecholamines (adrenaline and noradrenaline) and cortisol that mobilise immune cells in a single bout ([[Mechanisms of Exercise-Induced Immune Changes]]) ==become suppressive when hard training keeps them always switched on==. This ==chronic catecholamine and cortisol exposure== lowers the activity of natural killer (NK) cells, the innate killers of virus-infected cells ([[Complement and Natural Killer Cells]]). Neutrophils, the main phagocytes ([[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis]]), show impaired phagocytosis and ==oxidative burst==, the burst of toxic reactive oxygen species a neutrophil produces to kill what it has swallowed. Monocytes and macrophages develop ==desensitised signalling==, meaning their receptors stop responding to signals they see all the time, and persistent muscle and tissue stress keeps IL-6 (interleukin-6) and TNF-α (tumour necrosis factor alpha) raised.

| Effect | Mechanism | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| ↓ Natural killer (NK) cell activity | Chronic catecholamine and cortisol exposure | Reduced early antiviral defence |
| ↓ Neutrophil function | Impaired phagocytosis, oxidative burst | Slower bacterial clearance |
| ↓ Monocyte/macrophage responsiveness | Desensitised signalling | Reduced pathogen recognition |
| ↑ Inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) | Persistent muscle/tissue stress | Low-grade systemic inflammation |

Under high intensity, the IL-6 in the last row ==tends to be pro-inflammatory and can add to inflammation, because it uses a different signalling mechanism==, as [[IL-6 in Exercise]] explains.

### 4. Are athletes immunocompromised?

So are athletes immunocompromised, with a seriously weakened immune defence? Not in the usual sense, because ==athletes do not present with the serious illnesses normally associated with immunodeficiency==, only with ==URTIs== ([[Exercise and Infection Risk – J-Shaped Curve and Open Window]]). However, ==repeated patterns of training before full recovery may eventually lead to chronic immunosuppression==, a lasting dampening of immunity built from ==small changes in several parameters== rather than one big defect. It is therefore ==crucial to balance exercise intensity with rest, nutrition and recovery strategies==.

### 5. The whole picture

These threads meet in the lecture's closing table, a one-page answer to exercise immunology essays.

| Type of exercise | Effect on immunity |
|---|---|
| Acute moderate exercise | Typically enhances immune surveillance: increased NK cell activity, mobilisation of T cells, transient rise in IL-6 and IL-15, anti-inflammatory effects |
| Acute, very intense or prolonged exercise | Can create the "open window" of temporary immune suppression (3–72 hours): lymphocytopenia, reduced NK cell cytotoxicity, higher cortisol and catecholamines |
| Chronic moderate exercise | Lower baseline inflammation, improved immune regulation |
| Chronic frequent intense exercise | Risk of cumulative immunosuppression if recovery is insufficient: persistent inflammation, elevated stress hormones, reduced antibody responses, higher susceptibility to infections (especially upper respiratory tract infections) |

The table reads along two axes, ==acute (one bout) versus chronic (training)== and ==moderate versus intense==. Both acute rows rest on the hormones and cell movements of [[Mechanisms of Exercise-Induced Immune Changes]]. In the moderate one, immune surveillance means patrolling for threats, the anti-inflammatory IL-6 is explained in [[IL-6 in Exercise]], and ==IL-15== is another muscle-released cytokine, which supports NK and T cells. The intense one is the open window of [[Exercise and Infection Risk – J-Shaped Curve and Open Window]], with ==lymphocytopenia==, a fall in circulating lymphocytes, and reduced NK cell cytotoxicity, or killing ability. The chronic rows are sections 2 to 4 above, with reduced antibody responses pointing to [[B Cells and Antibodies]]. Read together, ==moderate exercise helps at both timescales==, whereas ==intense exercise suppresses immunity briefly after one bout, and cumulatively without recovery==.

### Summary

>Repeated mobilisation of immune cells makes the immune system and neuroendocrine response adapt. With ==moderate intensity and adequate recovery==, leukocyte numbers are unchanged but trafficking and regulation improve, ==T cells including Tregs increase==, ==pro-inflammatory cytokines and CRP fall== while anti-inflammatory cytokines rise, immunosenescence slows and salivary IgA is maintained. With ==chronic high intensity==, ==NK cell, neutrophil and monocyte/macrophage function fall==, and ==IL-6 and TNF-α rise, causing low-grade systemic inflammation==, because hormonal and tissue stress never switch off. Athletes are not immunocompromised, suffering ==only URTIs==, but training before full recovery can add up to chronic immunosuppression. In short, acute moderate exercise ==enhances immune surveillance==, acute intense exercise ==opens a 3 to 72 hour window of suppression==, chronic moderate exercise ==improves regulation== and chronic intense exercise without recovery ==risks cumulative immunosuppression==.
