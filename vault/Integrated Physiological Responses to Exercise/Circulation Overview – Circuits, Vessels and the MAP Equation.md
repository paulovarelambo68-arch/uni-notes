
> The heart pumps ==4 to 6 L of blood== through ==two circuits in series== and into systemic ==beds in parallel==, which keep resistance low and allow separate control of each organ's flow. Mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance (==MAP = CO x TPR==). Flow is equal at every level, so blood is slowest in the capillaries, whose combined area is largest, while the distensible veins hold ==two-thirds of the blood==. In heavy exercise most of the output is redirected to working muscle.

### 1. What the system is for

The cardiovascular system exists ==to supply the tissues with O2 and nutrients and to remove CO2 and H+ ions==, carried in blood by ==bulk flow== (the whole fluid moving down a pressure difference), because diffusion is quick only over the micrometre distances in lungs and tissues. It comprises the heart, arteries, arterioles (small muscular arteries), capillaries (thin-walled exchange vessels), venules and veins, and holds ==4 to 6 L of blood==.

### 2. Two circuits in series

This blood follows two circuits. In the pulmonary circulation the ==right ventricle== pumps deoxygenated blood into the ==pulmonary arteries==, the pulmonary capillaries pick up O2 and off-load CO2, and ==oxygenated== blood returns to the left atrium in the ==pulmonary veins==. In the systemic circulation the ==left ventricle== pumps it into the ==aorta== and its branches, the tissue capillaries off-load O2 and pick up CO2, and the systemic veins return deoxygenated blood to the right atrium.

These two circuits, pulmonary and systemic, are ==in series==, meaning one after the other rather than side by side, because ==each ends where the other begins==: the pulmonary circuit delivers blood to the left heart, which starts the systemic circuit, and the systemic circuit returns it to the right heart, which starts the pulmonary one. All the blood therefore passes through both in turn, which means that ==both ventricles must pump the same volume per minute==, though at very different pressures ([[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds]]), because if one pumped more, blood would pile up in the circuit it feeds. A separate lung circuit makes sense, because ==the lungs do not compete with other organs for flow==, and because they are close to the heart with ==very low vascular resistance==, pumping blood through them takes little energy. Blood in the pulmonary circuit arrives deoxygenated and is there for gas exchange, so the airway walls, which do no gas exchange, are fed like any other tissue: ==the bronchi receive oxygenated blood from the systemic circulation== through the bronchial arteries, which is why they also appear among the systemic beds in section 3.

### 3. Vascular beds in parallel

The systemic circuit is organised differently: the left ventricle drives blood at high pressure into beds ==arranged (mostly) in parallel==, namely brain, head and arms, bronchi, heart (coronary), the ==splanchnic circulation== (liver, GI tract, spleen), kidneys, and trunk and legs, so that ==only part of the cardiac output passes through any one tissue at a time==.

The figure shows this layout. Around the heart and lungs in the middle, a red arterial column (right) carries blood out from the aorta, and a blue venous column (left) brings it back to the heart. Each organ (purple arrows) is a separate branch running across from red to blue, like a rung of a ladder. Blood leaving the aorta takes just one rung and returns to the heart, rather than passing through the brain, then the kidneys, then the legs. That is what ==in parallel== means: the organs sit side by side, ==each fed directly from the arteries==. "Mostly" covers a few exceptions, such as blood from the gut and spleen, which flows on through the portal vein into the liver, so it crosses two organs in a row.

This layout has two advantages. First, ==total resistance is much lower in parallel than in series==. When blood must cross obstacles one after another (in series), their resistances add up, like toll booths on a single road: ==R = R1 + R2 + ...== (the total is the sum of the individual resistances). When paths sit side by side (in parallel), blood splits between them, so each extra path makes flow easier, like extra lanes on a motorway: here the ease of flow, called conductance (1/R), adds up, ==1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...==. Two organs that each have resistance R thus give 2R in series but only R/2 in parallel, four times less. Second, every organ gets the same arterial pressure through its own arterioles, small muscular arteries that act like taps, so ==each organ's flow can be turned up or down on its own== without affecting the others, as [[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles]] explains, along with how pressure falls along the circulation.

### 4. The three features and the one equation

Whatever the arrangement, a circulation has three features, flow (==cardiac output, CO==), pressure (==mean arterial pressure, MAP==) and resistance (==total peripheral resistance, TPR==), linked by ==MAP = CO x TPR==, the hydrostatic equivalent of Ohm's law ==V = i x R== (voltage = current x resistance) and, the professor stresses, the most important formula in the cardiovascular system.

The first, ==cardiac output, is the volume ejected by either ventricle in 1 min==, and ==CO = SV x HR==, stroke volume (SV) being the litres ejected per beat and heart rate (HR) the beats per minute. It is ==5 to 6 L/min at rest== and ==30 to 35 L/min in severe exercise==.

The second, MAP, is the arterial pressure averaged over a cycle, estimated from a cuff reading as diastolic plus a third of the pulse pressure (systolic minus diastolic), as [[Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex]] explains. Blood flows from high to low pressure, so what drives it round the body is the pressure at the start of the systemic circuit (MAP) minus the pressure at its end, in the right atrium (==right atrial pressure, RAP==). Since ==RAP is about 0 mmHg==, the driving pressure is simply MAP, which is why ==MAP alone appears in the equation==.

The third, resistance, is how hard it is to push blood through the vessels. By Ohm's law, pressure difference = flow x resistance (==ΔP = Q x R==), so ==R = ΔP / Q==, and for the whole systemic circulation ==TPR = MAP / CO==. Replacing CO with SV x HR gives ==MAP = SV x HR x TPR== (pressure rises with each of the three), which can be solved for any one term: ==CO = MAP / TPR==, ==SV = MAP / (HR x TPR)== and ==HR = MAP / (TPR x SV)==.

### 5. Vessel numbers, cross-sectional area and velocity

This flow passes through a tree of vessels: ==one aorta of about 2.5 cm== divides into ==about 160 million arterioles== and ==5 billion capillaries of 8 µm==, which recollect into ever fewer, wider veins ending in ==two venae cavae of about 3.2 cm==, and on average ==the venous side has more and larger vessels== than the arterial side. Total cross-sectional area therefore ==rises from the aorta to the arterioles==, reaches ==about 1000 cm² at the capillaries==, and is ==far higher in venules and veins than in arterioles and arteries==.

This matters because ==volume flow is the same in every section== (the same volume crosses the aorta and the capillaries each minute), and flow (Q) equals velocity (v) times area (A), so ==v = Q / A==: ==velocity is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area==. Because the heart pumps in bursts, blood in the aorta shoots forward at up to ==150 cm/s== during each contraction and almost stops (==close to 0==) between beats, so its speed is given as an average: ==about 20 cm/s in the aorta and arteries==. It then drops to ==0.05 cm/s in the capillaries==, ==allowing time for diffusion across the capillary wall==, and rises again to ==about 15 cm/s in the vena cava==.

### 6. Where the blood is

The body's 4 to 6 L of blood is not shared evenly between the vessels at any point in time. Approximate textbook values say: ==about two-thirds in the systemic veins and venules==, ==about an eighth in the systemic arteries==, ==only about a twentieth in the capillaries== (their total area is huge, but each capillary is very short), ==about a tenth in the pulmonary circuit== and the rest in the heart. The venous side has more and larger vessels precisely to be this store: numerous, wide, thin-walled veins are distensible, making them ==capacitance vessels==, a low-pressure reservoir whose blood ==sympathetic venoconstriction== (nerve-driven narrowing of the veins) can push back towards the heart. Venous pressure is below capillary pressure, and that gradient returns the blood.

### 7. How the cardiac output is shared out

Because the organs are in parallel, the cardiac output is shared according to need. In heavy exercise, sympathetic nerves narrow kidney and splanchnic arterioles (==sympathetic vasoconstriction==), and working muscle takes most of the output.

| Organ | Share at rest (McArdle) | Heavy exercise |
|---|---|---|
| Splanchnic (liver, GI tract, spleen) | 25% | falls sharply |
| Kidneys | 20% (1.1 L/min) | falls to 0.25 L/min |
| Brain | 15% | essentially unchanged |
| Skeletal muscle | 15–20% | rises to 80–85% |
| Skin | 6% | rises, for heat loss |
| Heart | 4–5% | rises four- to fivefold |

==Renal blood flow far exceeds the kidneys' own metabolic needs== because its main job is to bring plasma to be filtered into urine, so the kidney cells extract only a small fraction of its O2. Per the lecturer, ==brain flow does not rise in exercise==, although McArdle reports a 25 to 30% rise. Coronary and skin flow are in [[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow]].

### Summary

> Blood (==4 to 6 L==) crosses ==pulmonary and systemic circuits in series==, so both ventricles pump the same volume per minute, while systemic ==beds in parallel== keep resistance low because ==conductances add==. ==MAP = CO x TPR==, with ==CO = SV x HR== and RAP about 0 mmHg, so ==HR = MAP / (TPR x SV)==. As ==v = Q / A==, blood slows from 20 to ==0.05 cm/s== in the capillaries (==1000 cm²==) and speeds up to 15 cm/s in the vena cava, while the veins hold ==two-thirds of the blood==. At rest the kidneys' fifth of the output ==far exceeds their metabolic need==, while in exercise muscle takes ==80 to 85%==, renal and splanchnic flow fall and brain flow does not rise.
