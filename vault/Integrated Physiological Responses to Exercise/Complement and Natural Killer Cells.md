
> Besides phagocytosis, the innate branch has two more weapons: complement and natural killer (NK) cells. ==Complement is a system of blood plasma proteins that a pathogen switches on==. It can be started by ==three pathways that all meet at one central protein, C3==, and it then acts in ==three ways: opsonisation, inflammation and cell lysis==. ==NK cells recognise the body's own virus-infected or cancerous cells and kill them==. The two share a memory hook, because ==both kill by punching holes==, complement in microbes and NK cells in sick body cells.

### 1. What complement is

Besides its cells, the innate branch uses complement, a set of ==about 30 plasma proteins==, made mostly by the liver. They ==normally circulate in an inactive state== and become activated at sites of infection, in the presence of a pathogen.

Activation runs as a cascade, in which each activated protein activates the next, mostly by splitting it, so that a small trigger is greatly amplified. Once activated, they ==trigger a series of inflammatory events==, and ==C3 is the central component==.

### 2. Three ways in, one hub, three ways out

C3 is the most plentiful complement protein in the plasma. Three routes, called pathways, can start the cascade, and all lead to C3.

- The ==classical pathway== starts when antibodies, proteins made by B cells (see [[B Cells and Antibodies]]), stick to a microbe.
- The ==lectin pathway== starts when a lectin, a plasma protein, sticks to sugar patterns on a microbe's surface.
- The ==alternative pathway== starts spontaneously, because a little C3 breaks down all the time in the blood, and fragments that land on a microbe stick to it and set off the cascade.

In the diagram, the three pathway arrows point into the red circle, showing that ==all three converge on C3==. C3 is then split into a small piece, C3a (orange), and a large piece, C3b (yellow). ==C3a floats away and drives inflammation==. ==C3b sticks to the microbe's surface== and does two jobs. First, it carries out ==opsonisation==, which means coating the microbe so that phagocytes grab it more easily (see [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis]]). Second, as its downward arrow shows, it keeps the cascade going into the final stage, the ==terminal pathway==, where the last complement proteins join up into a pore in the microbe's membrane. This pore is the ==membrane attack complex (MAC)==. So there are three ways in, one hub and three ways out: inflammation, opsonisation and the MAC.

### 3. The three functions

Complement has three functions, which match the three ways out above: ==opsonisation, inflammation and cell lysis==.

The first function, ==opsonisation, may be the most important==. Activated complement proteins such as ==C3b and C4b==, the large pieces of C3 and C4, bind to the pathogen's surface. Phagocytes have receptors that grip these proteins, so a coated pathogen becomes ==more amenable to phagocytosis==. Such proteins are called ==opsonins==, because they mark a target for eating.

The second function, inflammation, is driven by the small pieces ==C3a, C4a and C5a==. They are ==mediators of inflammation==, meaning chemical signals that set it off. They bind to receptors on ==neutrophils, macrophages, mast cells, basophils== (see [[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]]) ==and endothelial cells==, the cells that line blood vessels. As a result, phagocytes are drawn to the infection, mast cells and basophils release more inflammatory chemicals, and the vessel wall becomes leaky. Inflammation as a whole is covered in [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP]].

The third function, ==cell lysis==, means bursting the pathogen, which is the job of the membrane attack complex.

The figure shows the MAC in the pathogen's cell membrane, the blue lipid bilayer, with the lilac intracellular space below. ==C5b== (dark blue), the large piece of C5, is joined by ==C6== (red), ==C7== (green) and ==C8== (yellow). Then ==several C9 molecules== (orange) form a ring that passes through the membrane, leaving an open channel into the cell. Water and ions rush in through it, which ==disrupts the pathogen's fluid balance== until it swells and bursts ==(it lyses)==.

### 4. Natural killer cells

Complement cannot reach a virus hiding inside a body cell, which is where natural killer (NK) cells come in. These are ==lymphocytes (10 to 15% of blood lymphocytes)== yet belong to the ==innate branch== (see [[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]]) because they act at once, without earlier exposure.

NK cells recognise whether a cell has been ==invaded by a virus or has become cancerous==, because such cells have ==lost or modified surface structures==. Healthy cells display self markers that act as a brake on NK cells, whereas infected or cancerous cells often lose them, releasing the brake, and may gain stress molecules that switch NK cells on.

The NK cell then kills its target by releasing two proteins. ==Perforin punches holes in the cell membrane==, and ==granzyme enters through the perforin pore and induces apoptosis==, the programmed cell-death pathway.

This completes the memory hook, since the MAC punches holes in microbes and perforin in infected or cancerous body cells. 

==Cytotoxic T cells use the same perforin and granzyme==, but only against cells bearing the one antigen, or foreign molecule, they are specific for (see [[T Cells and Antigen Presentation]]). Exercise also changes NK cell activity, as the exercise notes will show.

### Summary

> Complement is ==about 30 circulating plasma proteins activated in the presence of a pathogen==, whose ==classical, lectin and alternative pathways converge on C3==. C3 splits into C3a, which drives inflammation, and C3b, which coats the microbe and leads into the terminal pathway. Complement's functions are ==opsonisation by C3b and C4b, possibly the most important==, inflammation via C3a, C4a and C5a, and ==lysis by the membrane attack complex (C5b to C9)==, which disrupts fluid balance. ==NK cells, innate lymphocytes (10 to 15% of blood lymphocytes)==, recognise virus-infected or cancerous cells by their lost or modified surface structures and kill them with ==perforin, which punches holes like the MAC, and granzyme, which induces apoptosis==.
