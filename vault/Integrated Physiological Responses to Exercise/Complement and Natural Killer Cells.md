
> Complement and natural killer (NK) cells complete the innate branch's armoury, and both can kill by punching holes. Complement is ==about 30 circulating plasma proteins activated in the presence of a pathogen==, whose ==three pathways converge on C3== and whose functions are ==opsonisation, inflammation and lysis by the membrane attack complex==. ==NK cells, innate lymphocytes (10 to 15% of blood lymphocytes)==, kill virus-infected or cancerous cells with ==perforin and granzyme==.

### 1. What complement is

Besides its cells, the innate branch uses complement, a set of ==about 30 plasma proteins==, meaning proteins dissolved in the plasma and made mostly by the liver. They ==normally circulate in an inactive state== and become activated at sites of infection, so complement proteins are ==circulating plasma proteins that are activated in the presence of a pathogen==.

Activation runs as a cascade, in which each activated protein activates the next, mostly by splitting it, so that a small trigger is greatly amplified. Once activated, they ==trigger a series of inflammatory events==, and ==C3 is the central component==.

### 2. Three ways in, one hub, three ways out

Three routes, called pathways, can start this cascade.

- The ==classical pathway== starts when antibodies, proteins made by B cells, bind to a microbe (see [[B Cells and Antibodies]]), which lets the adaptive branch use complement.
- The ==lectin pathway== starts when a lectin, a sugar-binding plasma protein, recognises sugar patterns on a microbe's surface.
- The ==alternative pathway== starts spontaneously on microbial surfaces.

The three pathway arrows enter the red circle, showing that ==all three converge on C3==, which is split into a smaller fragment, labelled a, and a larger one, labelled b, as are the complement proteins C4 and C5. The message is three ways in, one hub and three ways out.

### 3. The three functions

Of these three outcomes, ==opsonisation may be the most important function==. Activated complement proteins such as ==C3b and C4b bind to the pathogen's surface and make it more amenable to phagocytosis==, because phagocytes carry receptors for them, which makes C3b and C4b ==opsonins==, coatings that mark something to be eaten.

The small fragments ==C3a, C4a and C5a== are instead ==mediators of inflammation==, chemical signals that drive it. They bind to receptors on ==neutrophils, macrophages, mast cells and basophils== (see [[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]]) and on ==endothelial cells==, which line blood vessels, so that phagocytes arrive, mast cells and basophils release further mediators and the vessel wall loosens. This inflammatory role returns in [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP]].

The third function, ==cell lysis==, is the work of the membrane attack complex, the first of the note's two hole-punchers.

The figure shows the MAC in the target's cell membrane, the blue lipid bilayer above the lilac intracellular space. It is built from ==C5b, C6, C7, C8 and a ring of several C9 molecules==, drawn in dark blue, red, green, yellow and orange, and the ring spans the bilayer, opening a channel into the cell. Water and ions rush through this pore, which ==disrupts the pathogen's fluid balance== until it swells by osmosis and ==lyses==, or bursts.

### 4. Natural killer cells

Complement cannot reach a virus hiding inside a body cell, which is where natural killer (NK) cells come in. These are ==lymphocytes, 10 to 15% of blood lymphocytes==, yet belong to the ==innate branch== (see [[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]]) because they act at once, without earlier exposure.

NK cells recognise whether a cell has been ==invaded by a virus or has become cancerous==, because such cells have ==lost or modified surface structures==. Healthy cells display self markers that act as a brake on NK cells, whereas infected or cancerous cells often lose them, releasing the brake, and may gain stress molecules that switch NK cells on.

The NK cell then kills its target by releasing two proteins. ==Perforin punches holes in the cell membrane==, and ==granzyme enters through the perforin pore and induces apoptosis==, the programmed cell-death pathway, which is how NK cells tackle hidden pathogens.

This completes the memory hook, since the MAC punches holes in microbes and perforin in infected or cancerous body cells. ==Cytotoxic T cells use the same perforin and granzyme==, but only against cells bearing the one antigen, or foreign molecule, they are specific for (see [[T Cells and Antigen Presentation]]). Exercise also changes NK cell activity, as the exercise notes show.

### Summary

> Complement is ==about 30 circulating plasma proteins activated in the presence of a pathogen==, whose ==classical, lectin and alternative pathways converge on C3==. C3 splits into C3a, which drives inflammation, and C3b, which coats the microbe and leads into the terminal pathway. Complement's functions are ==opsonisation by C3b and C4b, possibly the most important==, inflammation via C3a, C4a and C5a, and ==lysis by the membrane attack complex (C5b to C9)==, which disrupts fluid balance. ==NK cells, innate lymphocytes (10 to 15% of blood lymphocytes)==, recognise virus-infected or cancerous cells by their lost or modified surface structures and kill them with ==perforin, which punches holes like the MAC, and granzyme, which induces apoptosis==.
