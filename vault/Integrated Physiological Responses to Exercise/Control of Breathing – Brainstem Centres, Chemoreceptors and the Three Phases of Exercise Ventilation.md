
> Breathing runs on its own, yet it speeds up the moment you start to move and slows again when you stop. This note explains the brain centres that set the rhythm and the sensors that report what the blood needs. It then explains why exercise calls for extra signals from the nerves, which is what lets breathing keep pace with the muscles.

### 1. Why breathing needs a controller

Breathing is adjusted all the time to match the body's metabolism. The aim is that ==the oxygen and carbon dioxide pressures in the alveoli, and so in arterial blood, stay nearly constant== from rest to hard exercise. A thermostat does the same job for a room, turning the heating up or down to hold one temperature however cold it is outside. The breathing controller turns ventilation up or down to hold the alveolar gases steady however hard the muscles work.

### 2. The rhythm generators in the medulla

The control centres sit in the brainstem, the lowest part of the brain, just above the spinal cord. Two parts of it matter, ==the medulla and the pons==. The medulla is the bottom section and the pons sits just above it. ==The medulla directly controls automatic breathing==, the breathing you do without thinking. It takes input from the pons, from chemical sensors in the blood and brain, and from higher brain centres when breathing is voluntary.

Within the medulla, two groups of neurons, share the work. The ==dorsal respiratory group (DRG)== fires rhythmic bursts of signals to the diaphragm and the inspiratory rib muscles, and so sets the resting rhythm. It is the pacemaker of quiet breathing.

The ==ventral respiratory group (VRG)== holds both inspiratory and expiratory neurons but is mainly concerned with expiration. It stays quiet at rest. In forceful breathing it becomes active and drives the abdominal wall, the intercostal muscles between the ribs and the accessory muscles of the neck and chest.

The cycle comes from these neurons switching on and off. The inspiratory neurons fire and air flows in. Then ==they stop firing, partly because they limit themselves and partly because the expiratory neurons inhibit them==, and exhalation begins.

At rest exhalation is passive, because the stretched lung and chest spring back. In exercise the expiratory muscles push the air out, which costs energy. As expiration goes on, the inhibition fades and the inspiratory neurons fire again.

### 3. The fine-tuners in the pons

The pons adjusts the rhythm through two centres that pull in opposite directions. The ==pneumotaxic centre== times the switch from breathing in to breathing out. It ==prevents over-inflation of the lungs==, promotes expiration and restrains the apneustic centre.

The ==apneustic centre== acts on the DRG to prolong and deepen inspiration. It stretches out the ramp of signals to the diaphragm, so the diaphragm keeps contracting for longer. A ramp is a signal that grows steadily over time. The balance between the two centres sets how deep each breath is.

### 4. The sensors: chemoreceptors

The medulla needs information to act on, and at rest ==the chemical state of the blood largely regulates breathing==. This is humoral control, where ==humoral means carried in the blood==. The sensors are the ==chemoreceptors, structures that stimulate breathing when carbon dioxide, acidity or temperature rise and when oxygen or blood pressure fall==. They come in two sets, central and peripheral. Of all these signals, ==the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood is the most important breathing stimulus at rest==.

The ==central chemoreceptors sit in the medulla itself==, bathed by the fluid around the brain, the cerebrospinal fluid. Carbon dioxide crosses easily from the blood into this fluid and reacts with water to make carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions. The central receptors respond to those hydrogen ions, so they sense carbon dioxide through the acid it makes. This is the same reaction that carries carbon dioxide in blood, explained in [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate]]. These receptors, and the respiratory centre they feed, ==respond to a rise in carbon dioxide but not to a fall in oxygen==.

The peripheral chemoreceptors sit in the arteries, beside the pressure sensors of [[Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex]] but sensing chemistry rather than pressure. The ==carotid bodies== lie at the fork of the carotid artery in the neck, so they monitor arterial blood just before it reaches the brain. The ==aortic bodies== lie on the aortic arch, the curve of the great artery leaving the heart. A ==fall in arterial oxygen raises ventilation only through these peripheral receptors==. They protect against thin air and matter most in lung disease and at altitude, as [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training]] explains.

![[Pulm2_slide25_peripheral_chemoreceptors.png|272]]

When arterial carbon dioxide rises, ==80 per cent of the extra ventilation comes from the central receptors and 20 per cent from the peripheral ones==. The peripheral response is weaker but ==about five times faster==, because the carotid bodies sit directly in the arterial bloodstream. Their signals reach the medulla along ==the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves==. The medulla raises ventilation, more carbon dioxide is breathed out and arterial carbon dioxide falls again. This is negative feedback: the response removes the signal that caused it, so the level is held steady.

### 5. Why chemistry alone cannot explain exercise breathing

At rest, chemical feedback is enough to explain breathing. ==A fall in blood pH signals retained carbon dioxide and carbonic acid==, and the inspiratory neurons work harder to blow the carbon dioxide off. But this feedback cannot explain the large rise in breathing during exercise, for three reasons.

First, there is no oxygen signal, because ==alveolar oxygen does not fall as exercise gets harder==. In intense exercise the huge volumes breathed even ==push alveolar oxygen above 100 mmHg==, which speeds oxygen loading in the lung. Second, there is no carbon dioxide signal either, because ==alveolar oxygen and carbon dioxide pressures stay near resting values across a wide range of intensities==. The receptors see nearly the same blood as at rest, yet ventilation is many times higher.

Third, in light and moderate exercise ==ventilation rises in step with oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production==. The match is so close that something must be anticipating the need rather than correcting an error afterwards.

### 6. The nervous drives

The missing signals come from the nervous system, in two forms. The first is ==cortical influence==. Outflow from the motor cortex, which commands the muscles, also stimulates the respiratory neurons in the medulla, and so does the anticipation of exercise. Together they ==start the abrupt rise in breathing on the starting line==, before any chemistry has changed, a drive also called central command. The second is ==peripheral influence==, sensory signals from the joints, tendons and muscles as they move, which keep adjusting ventilation throughout exercise.

Core temperature is not one of the drives. Except in extreme overheating, ==a rise in core temperature has little effect on breathing in exercise==. The reason is timing: ventilation rises at the start of exercise and falls in recovery far too quickly to follow a temperature that changes over minutes.

The same speed rules out chemistry, since blood takes time to carry carbon dioxide to the receptors, whereas a nerve signal arrives within a second. Temperature does act strongly on the oxygen dissociation curve, which describes how readily haemoglobin lets go of oxygen. That, though, is an effect on the blood, not on breathing.

### 7. Putting it together: the three phases

==No single factor controls breathing in exercise==. The nervous drives and the chemical feedback act together, and their timing shows in three phases. ==Phase I is the abrupt rise at the start==, driven by the cortex and by signals from the moving limbs.

==Phase II is the gradual climb to a steady level==, driven by central command, by the medullary neurons themselves, and by feedback from chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors. ==Phase III is the fine tuning of that level== by feedback, as temperature, carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions slowly rise.

### 8. Ventilation against work: the ventilatory threshold

How much ventilation rises depends on how hard the work is. At moderate steady workloads ==ventilation rises in a straight line with oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production==. The extra air comes ==mainly from deeper breaths==, and at higher steady workloads mainly from faster breathing. The blood still has enough time in the lung capillaries to equalise fully with alveolar air, which is why the arterial gases stay steady.

Above a certain intensity the straight line breaks. ==The ventilatory threshold is the point in a graded exercise test where ventilation starts to rise faster than oxygen uptake==. A graded test steps the workload up every few minutes until exhaustion.

The threshold appears because the muscles are now making lactate by anaerobic metabolism, the energy pathway that runs without oxygen. Bicarbonate in the blood buffers this lactic acid, soaking up its hydrogen ions, and ==the buffering releases extra carbon dioxide that must be breathed off==. That is why ventilation climbs faster than oxygen uptake. The lactate threshold is explained in [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold]] and the buffering in [[Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney]].

### 9. Training and heat

Two further situations show the controller at work. After endurance training ==ventilation is lower at the same work rate==. The likely reason is that a trained person makes less lactate at a given load. There is then less acid and carbon dioxide to feed back to the chemoreceptors, and so less drive to breathe.

Heat does the opposite. In ==prolonged submaximal exercise in hot, humid conditions, ventilation drifts upward== while arterial carbon dioxide barely changes. The extra breathing is therefore not a carbon dioxide signal but ==the rising core temperature acting on the central chemoreceptors==. This is the one setting where temperature matters. It cannot explain the quick rise at the start of exercise, but after half an hour of heating it adds a slow drift.

### Summary

> The ==medulla makes the breathing rhythm==, with the dorsal respiratory group driving inspiration at rest and the ventral group adding forceful expiration, while the ==pons shapes each breath== through its pneumotaxic and apneustic centres. At rest the blood's chemistry runs the show and ==arterial carbon dioxide pressure is the most important stimulus==, sensed as hydrogen ions by the ==central chemoreceptors in the medulla==. The peripheral carotid and aortic bodies give only ==20 per cent of the carbon dioxide response but respond five times faster==, and they are the ==only sensors of low oxygen==. In exercise the alveolar gases stay near resting values while ventilation soars, so ==nerves from the motor cortex and the moving limbs drive breathing== and chemistry fine-tunes it. This gives ==three phases==: an abrupt nervous rise, a gradual climb to a plateau, and fine tuning by feedback. Ventilation rises in a straight line with oxygen uptake until the ==ventilatory threshold==, where ==bicarbonate buffering of lactate releases extra carbon dioxide==, while training lowers ventilation at a given load and heat makes it drift upward.
