
> Heart muscle contracts when ==calcium binds troponin C==, which moves tropomyosin out of the way so that myosin can grab actin and pull. On every beat a little calcium enters through ==L-type calcium channels (25%)== and triggers a much bigger release from the cell's store, the ==sarcoplasmic reticulum (75%)==, which is called ==calcium-induced calcium release==. To relax, the cell clears the calcium away, mostly by pumping it back into the store with ==SERCA (about 70%).== Sympathetic stimulation, through ==beta1 receptors, cAMP and protein kinase A==, makes each beat stronger and quicker to relax, while ==hypoxia== weakens the beat because the cell runs short of ATP.

### 1. The contractile proteins

A heart muscle cell is packed with thin threads of actin and thick threads of myosin, lying side by side. Small heads on the myosin, called cross-bridges, grab the actin, pull it a short way, let go and grab again, like hands pulling on a rope. This draws together the Z lines that anchor the ends of the actin, so the cell shortens and produces force (tension). The threads slide past each other without getting shorter themselves, which is why this is called the ==sliding filament mechanism==.

Calcium decides when the heads can grab. At rest a thin protein called tropomyosin lies along the actin and covers the ==myosin-binding sites on actin==. Attached to it is a small group of proteins, the troponin complex, and its part called ==troponin C== senses calcium. When calcium binds troponin C, tropomyosin changes shape and shifts aside, so the sites are uncovered and the heads can grab and pull. When calcium leaves, the sites are covered again and the muscle relaxes. Calcium does not just switch contraction on; the amount of calcium sets how strong it is. In a normal heartbeat there is only enough calcium to fill some of the troponin C molecules, so only some of the binding sites on actin are uncovered and only some myosin heads can pull at the same time. The force of a contraction depends on how many heads are pulling together, just as a rope is pulled harder when more people grab it. So ==more calcium means more troponin C filled, more binding sites uncovered, more myosin heads pulling at once and a stronger contraction==. Because troponin C is far from full in a normal beat, the heart has plenty of room to strengthen its beat this way.

This settles a common exam trap: ==myosin binds actin, not tropomyosin==, which only covers the binding sites. Three other statements about cardiac contraction are true. It is ==all-or-none==, because the heart's cells are connected electrically, so all of them contract on every beat (see [[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period]]). It ==increases active tension==, which just means the force the muscle makes by contracting. And it is ==strengthened by more calcium entering from outside the cell==, as section 2 explains.

Since every cell already contracts on every beat, the heart cannot make a beat stronger by using more cells, the way your arm muscles bring in extra fibres to lift something heavier. It has two other ways. It can be filled and stretched more before it contracts, which is Starling's law, or it can contract more strongly from the same stretch, which is a rise in ==contractility==. This note explains the calcium machinery behind contractility, and the note [[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling’s Law and Contractility]]  explains both ideas in full.

### 2. The calcium cycle during systole

In systole, the contraction phase of the heartbeat, the action potential has to get calcium onto troponin C. This link between the electrical signal and contraction is called ==excitation-contraction coupling==, and it uses calcium from two sources: the fluid outside the cell, and a store inside the cell called the ==sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)==.

On every beat it runs in this order:

1. The action potential spreads along the cell membrane and down the ==T tubules==, tunnels of membrane that carry it deep into the cell, and depolarises the membrane to threshold.
2. ==L-type calcium channels== (L for long-lasting) open and calcium flows in, but this is ==not enough to cause contraction: only about 25%== of what is needed.
3. This calcium acts as a trigger: it opens the SR's release channels, the ==ryanodine receptors==, which sit right next to the T tubules. This contact point is the ==diad==, where each L-type channel faces about ten ryanodine receptors, so a little calcium opens many of them.
4. The SR releases the ==other 75%==, and the calcium level in the cell rises ==10- to 100-fold==.
5. Calcium binds troponin C, tropomyosin moves, actin and myosin interact, and the muscle contracts.

Steps 2 to 4 are called ==calcium-induced calcium release (CICR)==: a small calcium entry unlocks a much larger store, like a small key opening a big door. Release then stops, perhaps partly because the high calcium level shuts the ryanodine receptors.

Because the trigger calcium enters during the plateau of the action potential (see [[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period]]), the key rule is that ==more calcium entering during the plateau means more release from the SR and a stronger contraction==.

### 3. Relaxation: three ways to remove calcium

To relax, so that the heart can refill with blood, the calcium must come off troponin C. At the end of contraction calcium stops entering and the SR is no longer stimulated to release it, so the calcium already in the cell must be removed quickly. There are three ways:

- Most of it, ==about 70% in humans==, is pumped back into the SR by ==SERCA==, the SR's calcium pump, which runs on ATP (its full name is sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase).
- Most of the rest is pushed out of the cell by the ==sodium-calcium (Na-Ca) exchanger== in the outer membrane.
- A small amount is pumped out by the ==cell membrane calcium pump (Ca-ATPase)==, which also runs on ATP.

SERCA has a brake, a small protein called ==phospholamban (PLN)==, which ==slows calcium uptake into the SR while it is dephosphorylated==, meaning while it carries no phosphate tag. Section 4 shows what happens when a tag is added.

The Na-Ca exchanger works differently, because it uses no ATP and is instead ==powered by the sodium gradient==. There is far more sodium outside the cell than inside, so sodium flows in through the exchanger, three ions at a time, and each time it pushes one calcium ion out. The gradient is kept up by the Na-K pump, which uses ATP to pump sodium out of the cell. So if the Na-K pump is blocked, sodium builds up inside, the exchanger slows and calcium stays in the cell, which is how digoxin works (section 5).

### 4. Sympathetic stimulation: more force, faster, and faster relaxation

The sympathetic nervous system, which readies the body for action, speeds up both halves of this cycle, as a single contraction shows.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-03 at 20.20.42.png|516]]

The graph shows the force of one contraction of a strip of heart muscle (in grams, vertical axis) over time (horizontal axis), starting at the stimulus S. The muscle is held at a fixed length (isometric) and starts from the same stretch both times (the same preload). The solid line is the normal contraction (control) and the dashed line is the contraction after adrenaline (beta-adrenergic stimulation). The dashed curve shows the slide's three effects:

- It peaks higher, at about 10 g instead of 6 g, which is ==an increase in active tension==.
- It rises more steeply, which is ==an increase in the rate of tension development==.
- It falls more steeply and ends sooner, which is ==an increase in the rate of relaxation==.

Because the starting stretch was the same, the stronger beat is not Starling's law at work but a true rise in contractility.

The next figure, which the professor would asks medical students to memorise, shows how adrenaline does this, and one rule explains it: ==protein kinase means phosphorylation==. A protein kinase is an enzyme that sticks a phosphate tag, taken from ATP, onto other proteins, and the tag switches those proteins on or off. Every effect in the cascade is one of these tags.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-03 at 20.21.48.png]]

As in Figure 3.13, the outside of the cell is at the top and the grey band is the cell membrane. The numbers in brackets below are the numbers printed on the figure, and the cascade starts in the top-left box:

1. A catecholamine (noradrenaline from sympathetic nerves, or adrenaline from the adrenal glands) binds the ==beta1 receptor== on the cell membrane.
2. The receptor switches on adenylate cyclase, a membrane enzyme that ==turns ATP into cyclic AMP (cAMP)==, a messenger inside the cell.
3. ==cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA)==, labelled cAMP-PK (5).
4. ==PKA phosphorylates the L-type calcium channel==, shown by the red arrow (6) to the channel (2), so the channel lets in more calcium each time it opens.
5. With each action potential (1), more trigger calcium therefore enters, more is released from the SR (3) and more reaches troponin C and the threads (4), along the magenta arrows.
6. This extra calcium increases the force of contraction, which is the ==positive inotropic effect== (inotropic means affecting the force of contraction).

The lower part of the figure shows PKA's two other targets, phospholamban (7), which sits on SERCA (8), and troponin I (9). Along the membrane on the right are the Na-Ca exchanger (10), the membrane calcium pump (11) and the Na-K pump (12), where the cardiac glycosides, the drug family that includes digoxin, act.

Those two other targets speed up relaxation. Phosphorylating phospholamban ==reduces its brake on SERCA==, so calcium is pumped back into the SR faster and the muscle relaxes faster, which is the ==lusitropic effect== (lusitropic means affecting relaxation). It also ==loads the SR with more calcium for the next contractions==, so the next beats are stronger and build force faster, which is what the slide means by saying that phospholamban phosphorylation promotes the rate of contraction. Phosphorylating ==troponin I==, the inhibitory part of the troponin complex, makes troponin C hold calcium less tightly, which the slide calls ==inhibition of calcium binding to troponin C==. Calcium still binds, so the muscle still contracts, but it lets go sooner, so ==relaxation is faster==.

The same cAMP and PKA pathway also raises the heart rate by acting on the heart's pacemaker, the SA node (see [[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate]]).

### 5. Inotropic agents and the mechanism of hypoxia

Every agent on the inotropic slide acts on this calcium machinery. Agents that raise contractility have a ==positive inotropic effect== and agents that lower it a ==negative inotropic effect==. The table also includes two changes that exams list beside them, a faster heart rate and a larger venous return.

| Change | Effect on contractility | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Noradrenaline and adrenaline | Positive | More calcium enters and more is stored in the SR (section 4) |
| Raised extracellular calcium | Positive | More calcium outside, so more enters with each action potential |
| Digoxin | Positive | It ==blocks the Na-K pump==, so sodium builds up inside, the Na-Ca exchanger slows and more calcium stays in the cell |
| Faster heart rate | Positive | Calcium enters more often than it can be removed, so the SR loads up and each beat gets a little stronger, the ==staircase (treppe) effect== |
| Larger venous return (more blood filling the heart) | None | The beat is stronger only because the muscle is stretched more, which is ==Starling's law, not contractility== |
| Hypoxia (too little oxygen) | Negative | Explained below |
| Calcium-channel blockers | Negative | Less calcium enters through the L-type channels |
| Beta-blockers | Negative | They block the beta1 receptor, so the cascade of section 4 cannot start |

The professor asks how hypoxia lowers contractility, and the answer comes down to energy. Heart muscle makes its ATP using oxygen and ==can make only a little ATP without it==, so a hypoxic cell soon runs short. Everything that uses ATP slows down: the myosin heads cycle more slowly, and SERCA refills the SR less, so relaxation is sluggish and less calcium is ready for the next beat. Waste also builds up, because making ATP without oxygen produces acid (H+) and the breakdown of energy stores releases phosphate. ==H+ competes with calcium for troponin C== and ==phosphate weakens the pull of each myosin head==, so ==the muscle makes less force from the same calcium and the same stretch==. That is exactly what a fall in contractility means, and it is why hypoxia is the negative inotrope among the options in the table.

### Summary

> Heart muscle shortens by the sliding filament mechanism, and ==calcium binding troponin C== is the switch that lets myosin grab actin. On each beat a little calcium entering through ==L-type channels (25%)== triggers a large release from the ==SR (75%)==, called ==calcium-induced calcium release==, so more calcium entering during the plateau gives a stronger beat. To relax, ==SERCA pumps about 70% back into the SR==, held back by ==phospholamban==, while the Na-Ca exchanger and the membrane calcium pump remove the rest. Sympathetic stimulation works through the ==beta1 receptor, cAMP and protein kinase A==, which phosphorylates the L-type channel (a stronger beat, the ==positive inotropic effect==), phospholamban (faster relaxation, the ==lusitropic effect==) and troponin I (calcium lets go sooner). Adrenaline, raised calcium, digoxin and a faster heart rate raise contractility, a larger venous return strengthens the beat only through ==Starling's law==, and ==hypoxia== lowers contractility because ATP runs short and ==acid and phosphate blunt the response to calcium==.
