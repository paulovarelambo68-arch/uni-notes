# Immunology (Dr Louise Horrigan)

## In haematopoiesis, which of the following cells arises from the common myeloid progenitor?

- [ ] Natural killer cell
- [ ] Memory B cell
- [x] Eosinophil
- [ ] Helper T cell

> The common myeloid progenitor produces erythrocytes, platelets and the white cells called monocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils. The common lymphoid progenitor produces the lymphocytes: B cells, which end as plasma and memory cells, T cells, which end as helper, cytotoxic and regulatory T cells, and natural killer cells, which are lymphocytes by family although innate by branch. See [[Cells and tissues of the immune system#3. Haematopoiesis one stem cell, two families]].

## Which of the following cytokines activates macrophages to kill the microbes they engulf?

- [ ] IL-10
- [x] IFN-γ
- [ ] TGF-β
- [ ] IL-1ra

> IFN-γ is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that activates macrophages to kill what they engulf. IL-10 does the opposite and switches macrophages off, TGF-β holds back lymphocytes and macrophages, and IL-1ra blocks the receptor for IL-1, all three being anti-inflammatory. See [[Cytokines#2. Pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines]].

## Which of the following does NOT start a pathway of complement activation?

- [ ] Antibodies bound to the surface of a microbe.
- [ ] A lectin bound to sugar patterns on a microbe.
- [ ] Spontaneous breakdown of small amounts of C3.
- [x] Assembly of C5b to C9 in the microbe's membrane.

> C5b to C9 assemble into the membrane attack complex in the terminal pathway, the final stage of the cascade, so they end it rather than start it. The classical pathway starts when antibodies stick to a microbe, the lectin pathway when a lectin sticks to sugar patterns on its surface, and the alternative pathway spontaneously, because a little C3 breaks down all the time and fragments that land on a microbe set off the cascade. See [[Complement and Natural Killer Cells#2. Three ways in, one hub, three ways out]].

## Humoral immunity is defence mediated by

- [ ] T cells acting directly on other cells.
- [ ] hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline.
- [x] antibodies dissolved in the body fluids.
- [ ] phagocytes engulfing microbes in tissues.

> B cells make antibodies, which work dissolved in the body fluids, once called humours, hence humoral or antibody-mediated immunity. T cells acting directly on other cells is the other arm of adaptive immunity, cell-mediated immunity. See [[Adaptive Immunity – Principles and Clonal Selection#1. The adaptive branch and its two cell types]].

## In antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), which cell grips the antibodies coating a target cell and kills it?

- [ ] Cytotoxic T cell
- [ ] Mast cell
- [ ] Plasma cell
- [x] Natural killer cell

> In ADCC, antibodies coat a target such as an infected cell, and a natural killer cell grips their Fc stems with its Fc receptors and kills it, which is one of the ways antibodies target cells and microbes for destruction. A cytotoxic T cell also kills infected cells, but only after being presented with its one antigen, which it recognises with its own T cell receptor rather than through bound antibody. See [[B Cells and Antibodies#3. What antibodies do]].

## During class switching from IgM to IgG, the B cell

- [x] keeps its specificity but changes its heavy chain constant region.
- [ ] changes its variable region so that it binds a different antigen.
- [ ] changes its light chains while keeping its heavy chains the same.
- [ ] joins five Y units into a pentamer held together by a joining chain.

> In class switching the B cell keeps its antigen specificity but changes the constant region of its heavy chain, the part that sets the isotype, which is why the secondary response is mainly IgG rather than IgM. Changing the variable region would change which antigen it binds, and a pentamer held together by a joining chain is the structure of IgM, whereas IgG is a monomer. See [[Immunological Memory – Primary and Secondary Responses#3. The secondary response]].

## In acute inflammation, swelling is caused by

- [x] fluid leaking out of more permeable vessels.
- [ ] neutrophils dividing within the injured tissue.
- [ ] prostaglandins acting on local nerve endings.
- [ ] vasoconstriction of the local blood vessels.

> Inflammatory mediators make the local vessels more permeable, so fluid and plasma proteins leak out into the tissue and cause the swelling. Prostaglandins acting on nerve endings cause pain, and the heat and redness come from increased blood flow through dilated, not constricted, vessels. See [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP#2. The acute inflammatory event, step by step]].

## Compared with lean visceral fat, the visceral fat of an obese person shows a shift in macrophages

- [ ] from M2 to M1, with more IL-10 released.
- [ ] from M1 to M2, with more IL-10 released.
- [ ] from M1 to M2, with more TNF-α released.
- [x] from M2 to M1, with more TNF-α released.

> Obesity turns visceral fat into a source of chronic low-grade inflammation, as the anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages of lean fat, which with regulatory T cells and eosinophils release IL-10, IL-4 and IL-13, give way to pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, which with other inflammatory cells release IL-6, TNF-α and IFN-γ. A shift towards M2 with more IL-10 describes lean, insulin-sensitive fat, the opposite of the obese picture. See [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP#4. Chronic inflammation]].

## In lymphocyte trafficking, diapedesis is the step in which the cell

- [ ] rolls along the wall as CD44 catches loosely on hyaluronan.
- [x] squeezes between two endothelial cells into the tissue.
- [ ] latches firmly onto the wall, then stops and flattens.
- [ ] is washed off the vessel wall into the circulating blood.

> A lymphocyte leaves the blood in three steps: rolling, as CD44 catches loosely on hyaluronan, then firm adhesion, as further adhesion molecules latch on and the cell stops and flattens, then diapedesis, meaning leaping through, as it squeezes between two endothelial cells into the tissue. Being washed off the vessel wall into the blood is demargination, which moves cells into the circulation rather than out of it. See [[Mechanisms of Exercise-Induced Immune Changes#4. Where the cells come from and how they cross the wall]].

## Chronic high-intensity exercise without adequate recovery reduces neutrophil function. Which of the following describes this change?

- [x] Impaired phagocytosis and oxidative burst, slowing bacterial clearance.
- [ ] Desensitised receptor signalling, reducing the recognition of pathogens.
- [ ] Increased NETosis, trapping more bacteria in the upper airways.
- [ ] Reduced cytotoxicity, weakening the early defence against viruses.

> Under chronic high-intensity training, neutrophils show impaired phagocytosis and oxidative burst, the burst of toxic reactive oxygen species they use to kill what they swallow, so bacteria are cleared more slowly. Desensitised signalling, which reduces pathogen recognition, describes monocytes and macrophages, and weaker early antiviral defence follows the fall in NK cell activity. See [[Chronic Exercise and Immune Function – Moderate versus High Intensity#3. Chronic high-intensity exercise]].

# Cardiovascular System (Prof. Tony Wheatley)

## Two vascular beds, each with a resistance of R, are arranged in parallel. Their combined resistance is:

- [ ] 2R
- [ ] R
- [x] R/2
- [ ] R/4

> In parallel the conductances add, so 1/R total = 1/R + 1/R = 2/R, giving R/2. The same two beds in series would give 2R, four times more, which is why the parallel arrangement keeps total resistance low. See [[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation#3. Vascular beds in parallel]].

## In relation to the atrial pressure trace of the cardiac cycle, which statement is correct:

- [x] The a wave is produced by atrial contraction
- [ ] The c wave is produced by the atrium filling in systole
- [ ] The v wave is produced by the AV valve bulging back
- [ ] The a wave occurs during isovolumetric relaxation

> The a wave is atrial contraction, the last event of diastole. The c wave is the closing AV valve bulging back into the atrium, and the v wave is the atrium filling against the closed AV valve in systole, so the c and v statements swap the two causes. See [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#7. Heart sounds and the ECG on the same timeline]].

## Which of the following is the best index of myocardial contractility:

- [ ] Stroke volume
- [ ] End-diastolic volume
- [ ] Peak aortic systolic pressure
- [x] Left ventricular dP/dt max

> dP/dt max is the fastest rise of ventricular pressure during isovolumetric contraction, when the valves are shut and fibre length is fixed, so it reflects contractile strength largely independent of preload and afterload. Stroke volume also rises with EDV (Starling's law), so it is not a pure index of contractility. See [[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling’s Law and Contractility#5. Measuring contractility]].

## A resting heart rate of 60–70 beats/min, below the SA node's own rate of about 100 beats/min, is due to:

- [ ] Continuous sympathetic tone to the SA node
- [x] Continuous vagal tone to the SA node
- [ ] The AV node taking over as pacemaker
- [ ] Circulating adrenaline acting on β1 receptors

> The vagus brakes the SA node constantly (vagal tone), which is why a denervated, transplanted heart rests near 100 beats/min. Sympathetic tone and circulating adrenaline would raise the rate, not lower it. See [[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate#3. Autonomic control of heart rate]].

## On the LV pressure–volume loop, at which point does the dicrotic notch (incisura) appear in the aortic pressure trace?

![[PV_loop_lettered_A-F.png|400]]

- [ ] A
- [ ] D
- [ ] E
- [x] F

> The incisura is the brief dip in aortic pressure as back-flow snaps the aortic valve shut, which on the loop is F, the end of ejection. D is where the aortic valve opens, and A is where the mitral valve opens. See [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#5. Closing the loop]].

## Digoxin has a positive inotropic effect because it:

- [ ] Stimulates β1 receptors, raising cAMP
- [ ] Opens L-type Ca2+ channels directly
- [x] Blocks the Na-K pump, so less Ca2+ is extruded
- [ ] Speeds Ca2+ uptake into the SR by SERCA

> Blocking the Na-K pump lets Na+ build up inside the cell, which slows the Na-Ca exchanger, so more Ca2+ stays in the cell and contraction strengthens. β1 receptors and cAMP are how noradrenaline and adrenaline act, not digoxin. See [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation#5. Inotropic agents and the mechanism of hypoxia]].

## When travelling at 0° in the frontal plane, a ventricular depolarisation wave causes a large positive deflection in which lead?

- [x] Lead I
- [ ] Lead III
- [ ] aVF
- [ ] aVR

> Lead I runs from RA (negative) to LA (positive) at 0°, so a wave at 0° runs straight along it and gives its tallest positive deflection. aVF (+90°) lies at right angles and is flat, while lead III (+120°) and aVR (−150°) are more than 90° away and show negative deflections. See [[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System#5. The twelve leads limb leads, the hexaxial system and chest leads]].

## An ECG strip shows regular P waves, but every third P wave is not followed by a QRS complex. This is most likely:

- [ ] Normal sinus rhythm
- [x] Second degree AV-nodal block
- [ ] First degree AV-nodal block
- [ ] Third degree AV-nodal block

> In second degree block the AV node passes only some atrial impulses, so some P waves have a QRS and others do not, here in a 3:2 ratio. First degree block conducts every P wave, only more slowly, while in third degree block the P waves are unrelated to slow, regular QRS complexes. See [[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation#2. AV-nodal block, three degrees]].

## Because the myocardium extracts 70–80% of the O2 from its blood, a rise in its O2 demand is met mainly by:

- [ ] Raising O2 extraction to nearly 100%
- [ ] Direct sympathetic dilation of coronary arteries
- [x] Increasing coronary blood flow up to fourfold
- [ ] Raising arterial haemoglobin saturation

> With extraction already near its limit, the heart can get more O2 only by increasing flow, which metabolic vasodilation (adenosine and hypoxia) raises up to fourfold. Sympathetic nerves do not dilate the coronary arteries directly; they only increase the heart's work. See [[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow#1. Coronary supply]].

## In essential hypertension, the renal output (pressure diuresis) curve:

- [ ] Shifts left, so salt is excreted at a lower pressure
- [x] Shifts right, so a higher pressure is needed to excrete normal salt
- [ ] Is unchanged, the pressure being held up by the baroreflex
- [ ] Becomes flat, so salt output no longer depends on pressure

> Remodelled renal vessels reduce pressure diuresis and shift the curve right, so a normal salt intake is balanced only at about 140 mmHg: the hypertension is needed to excrete sodium and water normally. The baroreflex cannot be responsible, because its receptors reset to a sustained pressure within days. See [[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment#6. What causes essential hypertension]].

# Respiratory System (Dr Daniel O'Toole)

## Alveolar ventilation (VA) =

- [ ] Breathing rate x Tidal volume
- [ ] Minute ventilation + Dead-space ventilation
- [x] (Tidal volume - Dead space) x Breathing rate
- [ ] Tidal volume / Pulmonary blood flow

> Only the part of each breath beyond the dead space reaches the alveoli: at rest (500 - 150) mL x 12 breaths/min gives about 4.2 L/min. Breathing rate x tidal volume is minute ventilation (VE), which equals VA plus dead-space ventilation. See [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt#4. Dead space and alveolar ventilation]].

## During a heavy lift, the Valsalva manoeuvre is thought to help by

- [x] bracing the chest and improving leverage
- [ ] increasing venous return to the heart
- [ ] raising stroke volume by Starling's law
- [ ] lowering the pressure inside the abdomen

> Closing the glottis while the expiratory muscles contract turns the chest into a rigid, pressurised column, which is thought to improve leverage. The high pressure actually squashes the chest veins, cutting venous return and stroke volume, and raises abdominal pressure enough to risk a hernia. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#8. Straining against a closed throat the Valsalva manoeuvre]].

## The PO2 of blood is set by the

- [ ] total oxygen content of the blood
- [ ] oxygen bound to haemoglobin
- [ ] percentage saturation of haemoglobin
- [x] oxygen dissolved in the plasma

> Oxygen bound to haemoglobin exerts no pressure, so the dissolved oxygen alone sets the PO2 that drives diffusion, even though it is only about 1.5 per cent of the blood's oxygen. Bound oxygen makes up most of the content, but it is "hidden" from the PO2. See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#1. Why Dissolved Oxygen Cannot Supply the Body]].

## A thickened alveolar membrane may cause few problems at rest but limits exercise because, in exercise,

- [ ] alveolar PO2 falls well below 100 mmHg
- [x] blood spends less time in the lung capillary
- [ ] haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen rises
- [ ] fewer pulmonary capillaries are open to the alveoli

> A thicker membrane lengthens the time needed to reach equilibrium. At rest blood stays about 0.75 s, which leaves time to spare, but in exercise it may stay only about 0.25 s, so it leaves the lung short of oxygen. Capillaries are actually recruited in exercise rather than closed. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#4. When the exchange is impaired thick membranes, lost surface and too little time]].

## Ventilation falls as quickly as it rose when exercise stops, which indicates

- [ ] a fall in core temperature
- [x] neural rather than chemical control
- [ ] a rise in arterial PO2 at the carotid bodies
- [ ] a rapid fall in blood lactate

> Breathing changes far too fast at the start and end of exercise to follow blood chemistry or temperature, both of which take time to change. This marks a nervous drive from the motor cortex and the moving limbs. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#7. Putting it together the three phases]].

## Endurance performance is predicted better than by VO2max by the

- [ ] maximum voluntary ventilation
- [ ] vital capacity
- [x] lactate threshold
- [ ] resting VO2

> Long races are run below VO2max, and training can raise the lactate threshold without raising VO2max, so two people with the same VO2max can perform very differently. Lung function measures such as vital capacity do not even separate elite endurance athletes from healthy people. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#4. How much of the ceiling you can use the lactate threshold]].

## The ventilatory buffer lowers H+ concentration by

- [x] lowering blood CO2 by breathing more
- [ ] secreting H+ into the urine with ammonia
- [ ] binding H+ to deoxyhaemoglobin
- [ ] retaining CO2 to form more carbonic acid

> Raised H+ or CO2 stimulates the chemoreceptors, alveolar ventilation rises, and blood CO2 falls, which pulls H+ + HCO3- towards CO2 + H2O. This removes acid within minutes rather than just hiding it. Retaining CO2 is the response to alkalosis, and secreting H+ into the urine is the slower renal buffer. See [[Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney#3. The bicarbonate buffer and how breathing removes acid]].

## At altitude, steady cycling at the same power output feels harder because its oxygen cost

- [ ] rises as the muscles work less efficiently
- [ ] falls while lactate production rises
- [ ] is met mainly by anaerobic energy
- [x] is a larger share of a reduced VO2max

> The muscles do the same work, so the task still costs about 2 L/min of O2, but VO2max has fallen. That cost is therefore about 50 per cent of maximum at sea level and about 70 per cent at 4,300 m. The oxygen cost itself does not change with altitude. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#6. Exercising up there]].

## Compared with peripheral macrophages, alveolar macrophages

- [ ] are recruited from blood monocytes in infection
- [ ] act only once adaptive immunity has begun
- [x] are residents of the air spaces
- [ ] cannot polarise into M1 or M2 forms

> Alveolar macrophages are residents of the air spaces and are the first cells to meet an inhaled microbe. Peripheral macrophages are recruits that begin as blood monocytes and mature in the tissue during infection. Either kind can polarise into M1 or M2. See [[Lung Immune Cells – Toll-like Receptors, Macrophages and Neutrophils#4. The macrophage two forms]].

## Airway macrophages in asthma

- [x] make IL-10 mRNA but too little IL-10 protein
- [ ] show increased phagocytosis of microbes
- [ ] clear dying cells more efficiently
- [ ] release less TNF-alpha and IL-6

> Asthmatic macrophages make more IL-10 messenger RNA but less IL-10 protein, so the signal that switches inflammation off is missing. They also show reduced phagocytosis and efferocytosis, and they release more TNF-alpha, IL-1 beta, IL-6 and IL-8, not less. See [[Lung Disease and Exercise – ARDS, Asthma and COVID#2. When the airways narrow asthma]].

# Training and Exercise Response (Dr Nicole Burns)

## During detraining, which of the following best explains the rise in heart rate at a given submaximal workload?

- [ ] A fall in maximal heart rate
- [x] A fall in stroke volume
- [ ] A rise in parasympathetic tone
- [ ] A rise in plasma volume

> Detraining lowers plasma volume and therefore stroke volume, so the heart must beat faster to maintain cardiac output (HR × SV) at a given submaximal workload. Plasma volume falls rather than rises, and neither a rise in parasympathetic tone nor a fall in maximal heart rate would raise the submaximal heart rate. See Training & Detraining, slide 32 (Detraining (Reversal of Adaptations)).

## With aerobic training, which muscle fibres improve their oxidative (aerobic) potential?

- [ ] Slow-twitch fibres only
- [ ] Fast-twitch fibres only
- [ ] Slow-twitch, at the expense of fast-twitch
- [x] Both slow- and fast-twitch fibres

> The lecture states that all fibres maximise their existing aerobic potential, and the summary figure shows the oxidative potential of fast-twitch fibres rising with training. Endurance athletes do have larger slow-twitch than fast-twitch fibres, but the fast-twitch fibres still gain oxidative potential rather than losing it. See Training & Detraining, slide 13 (Muscle Fiber Type and Size).

## Which of the following best describes the effect of endurance training on blood pressure, according to the lecture?

- [x] A fall in hypertensive people, via improved vascular function
- [ ] A fall in hypertensive people, via a reduced blood volume
- [ ] A fall in all people, via a lower maximal cardiac output
- [ ] A fall in athletes only, via a lower resting heart rate

> The table of cardiovascular adaptations lists a fall in blood pressure in hypertensive individuals, brought about by improved vascular function and with cardioprotective effects. Blood volume and maximal cardiac output both increase with endurance training, so neither explains the fall. See Training & Detraining, slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training).

## The increase in pulmonary diffusion capacity with endurance training is attributed to which of the following?

- [ ] Improved respiratory muscle endurance
- [ ] Training of the diaphragm and intercostals
- [x] Capillary–alveolar adaptations
- [ ] A higher breathing frequency

> The respiratory adaptations table attributes the greater diffusion capacity to capillary–alveolar adaptations, which improve O₂ exchange. Improved respiratory muscle endurance and training of the diaphragm and intercostals explain better ventilation efficiency and delayed respiratory fatigue. Breathing frequency falls with training rather than rises. See Training & Detraining, slide 16 (Respiratory adaptations to endurance training).

## Compared with before training, which of the following best describes the blood lactate response to exercise of increasing intensity after endurance training?

- [ ] Lactate starts to accumulate at a lower VO₂
- [x] Lactate starts to accumulate at a higher VO₂
- [ ] Lactate at rest is substantially higher
- [ ] Lactate rises steadily from the start of exercise

> In the lecture's figure, blood lactate after training stays near resting levels until a much higher oxygen uptake than before training. Exercise therefore continues longer before the onset of blood lactate accumulation (OBLA). Resting lactate is similar before and after training; what shifts is the point at which lactate starts to accumulate. See Training & Detraining, slide 17 (Blood Lactate Concentration Adaptations).

## As aerobic fitness improves, which of the following is needed to keep training at the desired exercise heart rate?

- [x] A periodic increase in the exercise level
- [ ] A periodic decrease in the exercise level
- [ ] A lower target heart rate for training
- [ ] A lower training frequency each week

> Training lowers the heart rate at a given submaximal workload, so the exercise level must be raised periodically to reach the desired exercise heart rate. Lowering the exercise level would drop the heart rate further, below the training-sensitive zone. See Training & Detraining, slide 22 (Training Intensity: Train at HRmax Percentage).

## Which of the following best describes the control of ventilation during exercise?

- [ ] Chemical feedback alone, via the central chemoreceptors
- [ ] Neural drive alone, via motor command
- [ ] Neural drive plus O₂ sensed by the central chemoreceptors
- [x] Both immediate neural drive and chemical feedback

> The lecture concludes that during exercise, ventilation reflects both immediate neural (feed-forward) drive and chemical feedback, with brainstem drive changing breathing depth and frequency. Neither input acts alone. The central chemoreceptors respond mainly to CO₂-related changes in CNS H⁺, not to O₂. See Integrated Response, slide 8 (What tells the brain to increase ventilation?).

## Which of the following is listed in the lecture as a clinical use of VO₂max testing?

- [ ] Imaging blockages in the coronary arteries
- [ ] Measuring bone mineral density in older adults
- [x] Identifying the cause of unexplained dyspnoea
- [ ] Diagnosing the cause of an acute infection

> Clinically, VO₂max testing is a diagnostic procedure used, among other things, to identify the cause of unexplained dyspnoea, to evaluate heart and lung status and response to therapy, and to plan reconditioning programmes. It assesses the cardiorespiratory response to exercise and does not image the coronary arteries. See Integrated Response, slide 23 (VO₂max- clinically).

## At the same submaximal workload, which of the following best describes the RER of an endurance-trained athlete compared with an untrained person?

- [x] Lower, as more fat is oxidised
- [ ] Higher, as more fat is oxidised
- [ ] Higher, as more carbohydrate is oxidised
- [ ] Lower, as more carbohydrate is oxidised

> After endurance training, more of the energy at a given submaximal intensity comes from lipid and less from carbohydrate. Because RER (VCO₂/VO₂) is about 0.7 for fat and 1.0 for carbohydrate, this shift gives the trained athlete a lower RER, whereas greater carbohydrate use would raise it. See Training & Detraining, slide 12 (Metabolic Adaptations).

## According to the lecture, how can VO₂max be determined?

- [ ] Only by a maximal test to exhaustion
- [x] Either submaximally or maximally
- [ ] Only from the resting heart rate
- [ ] Only from blood lactate samples

> The lecture states that VO₂max can be determined either submaximally or maximally. VO₂max is defined as the largest O₂ uptake during strenuous exercise to exhaustion, but a maximal test is not the only way to determine it. See Integrated Response, slide 26 (VO₂ max).
