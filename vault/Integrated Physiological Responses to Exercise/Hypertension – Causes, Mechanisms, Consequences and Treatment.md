
>Hypertension is ==a chronic, usually progressive rise in arterial pressure==, diagnosed when repeated resting readings exceed ==140/90 mmHg under 50 years of age, or 160/95 mmHg over 50==; ==about 10% of cases are secondary==, with a findable cause, and ==90% are essential==. Whatever starts it, ==a raised resistance in the small arteries holds the pressure up==, and the kidney comes to need that pressure to excrete the day's salt. Untreated it silently damages the heart, brain and kidneys, so treatment aims at ==135–145/85 mmHg==.

### 1. What counts as hypertension

Clinical hypertension is ==a chronic, usually progressive, raised arterial pressure==, and the dividing line between normal and raised is difficult to choose. Most physicians diagnose it when ==repeated resting measurements exceed 140/90 mmHg under 50 years of age, or 160/95 mmHg over 50==; just below that is not safe either, because ==a high-normal 130–139/85–89 mmHg doubles the ten-year risk of cardiovascular disease==. ==Secondary hypertension, about 10% of cases, has an identifiable pathological cause==, while ==essential (primary) hypertension, the other 90%, has no obvious organic cause==.

### 2. Secondary hypertension

The most common secondary cause is ==pre-eclamptic toxaemia==, the hypertension of pregnancy, which affects ==7 to 10% of pregnancies== and ==remits on delivery==. Three others are uncommon. ==Hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome)== is an excess of aldosterone, the hormone that tells the kidney to keep sodium, so ==its hypertension comes from excessive salt and water retention==. ==Renal artery stenosis==, a narrowed artery to one kidney, ==raises pressure by activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system==. A ==phaeochromocytoma is a catecholamine-secreting tumour==, whose adrenaline and noradrenaline ==raise pressure through α-adrenoceptors==, the receptors that constrict small arteries.

### 3. How extra fluid becomes high pressure

How does keeping salt and water raise the pressure?

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 16.47.54.png|511]]

Follow the red arrows down. Salt holds water with it, so ==retaining sodium expands the fluid outside the cells, the plasma and the blood volume==. The fuller circulation drives more blood back to the heart, so the ventricle fills more and ejects a larger ==stroke volume==, the volume pumped per beat, by Starling's law ([[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling's Law and Contractility]]). Stroke volume times heart rate is ==cardiac output==, and ==MAP = CO x TPR==: mean arterial pressure equals cardiac output times total peripheral resistance, the resistance of all the small arteries together. So ==a larger output raises MAP==.

The side branch adds the rest. Each organ keeps its own blood flow steady, so the extra flow makes ==its arterioles constrict== ([[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles]]), and constriction in many organs at once ==raises TPR and MAP further==. ==Hypertension started by an increase in fluid volume is maintained by an increase in total peripheral resistance.==

### 4. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system

A narrowed renal artery is modelled in rodents by clipping one renal artery and leaving the other kidney alone.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 16.48.24.png|293]]

In the figure the clip, marked X, narrows one renal artery, so less blood reaches that kidney. The kidney cannot tell this from a real fall in blood pressure, so it responds as if pressure were low: its ==juxtaglomerular cells==, flow sensors in the walls of its small arteries, ==release the enzyme renin into the blood==. ==Renin cuts angiotensinogen==, an inactive protein made by the liver, ==into angiotensin I==, and then ==angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)==, found mainly in the lungs, ==cuts angiotensin I into angiotensin II==, the active hormone. 

Angiotensin II raises pressure in two ways ([[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles]]). Quickly, as a ==powerful vasoconstrictor==, it narrows small arteries throughout the body, which raises TPR and therefore MAP. More slowly, it makes the ==adrenal cortex==, the outer layer of the adrenal glands above the kidneys, ==secrete aldosterone==, which tells the kidney tubules to ==reabsorb more sodium==. Water follows the sodium, so ==blood volume rises and MAP climbs further== through the chain in section 3. This is why ==captopril==, a drug for high blood pressure, works: it ==blocks ACE==, so less angiotensin II and less aldosterone are made, the small arteries relax, the kidneys keep less salt and water, and blood pressure falls (section 9).

### 5. Essential hypertension: benign, malignant, young and old

Most patients, though, have essential hypertension, which takes one of two courses. ==Benign essential hypertension is the commonest==: ==slowly progressive, almost symptomless==, and ==usually found at a routine check-up==. The rarer ==malignant hypertension is rapidly progressive==, quickly causing ==cardiac failure with oedema, renal damage, and cerebral damage with papilloedema and retinopathy==, a swollen optic disc and damaged retinal vessels.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 16.48.52.png|329]]

The pattern also changes with age. The four panels run against oxygen uptake, which rises as exercise gets harder; solid lines are patients aged 17 to 29, dashed lines patients aged 60 to 69. At rest, on the left, ==both groups have a high MAP but reach it differently==: the ==young have a raised heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output==, while ==in the old a raised peripheral resistance maintains it==. In exercise the ==young raise stroke volume and heart rate, drop resistance and raise MAP moderately==, whereas the ==old raise stroke volume and heart rate less, drop resistance less, and MAP rises markedly==, because stiff, narrowed arterioles cannot dilate freely.

### 6. What causes essential hypertension

Two theories explain this. The first is neurogenic: ==an over-active sympathetic nervous system==. In the young it drives the heart, through the ==β1-adrenoceptors== that speed and strengthen it, giving ==tachycardia (a fast heart rate), a raised stroke volume and a raised cardiac output==; in the old it drives the vessels, as ==α-adrenoceptor-mediated peripheral vasoconstriction== with ==down-regulation of the β2-adrenoceptors through which adrenaline dilates them==. ==Vasodilators reverse that constriction at first==, but ==persistent constriction thickens and permanently narrows the walls of small arteries and arterioles==, so ==the raised resistance no longer answers vasodilators==; high pressure can also ==destroy some of those vessels, a loss called rarefaction, which raises resistance further==.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 16.49.15.png|636]]

The figure sorts the causes into two boxes. On the left are ==inherited predispositions: sympathetic over-reactivity, an exaggerated pressure response from abnormal ion movement across cell membranes, weak vasodilator (depressor) systems, and the metabolic syndrome of insulin resistance, obesity and abnormal blood fats==, to which ==family history== belongs. On the right are ==repeated environmental influences: psychological stress, a high salt (NaCl) intake, physical inactivity with a high calorie intake, chronic alcohol intake and smoking tobacco==. Both act through exaggerated sympathetic 'defence' reactions, which constrict the kidney's vessels and fix the hypertension by remodelling the resistance vessels. ==Cardiac hypertrophy is in neither box: it is a consequence of hypertension, not a cause.==

The second theory is faulty sodium handling, which explains why the pressure stays raised. ==The most important mechanism for long-term pressure control is the renal-body fluid system==: the kidney adjusts the body's fluid volume until output matches intake, and ==its equilibrium point is the arterial pressure at which water and sodium intake equals output==.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-05 at 16.49.46.png|549]]

These curves plot salt intake and output, in multiples of a normal daily intake, against arterial pressure. The normal curve rises almost vertically near 100 mmHg, because ==a high arterial pressure produces a high output of salt and water, which is pressure diuresis==. A normal intake (the lower horizontal line) is therefore balanced at D, above the pressure marked C, and a high intake at E. ==In hypertensives, salt-sensitive and salt-insensitive alike, the curve shifts right== (blue arrows), because ==renal vessels remodelled as the pressure develops chronically reduce pressure diuresis==, so those same intakes are balanced only at A, about 140 mmHg, and at B: ==the hypertension is required to excrete sodium and water normally==. This is why the baroreflex cannot cure it ([[Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex]]): below that equilibrium point the kidney retains fluid until the pressure returns.

### 7. What hypertension does

What makes it dangerous is that ==hypertension is virtually symptomless== and ==rarely presents with nosebleeds or headaches==, contrary to popular belief. Untreated, it ==damages the heart, brain and kidneys==, causing ==cardiac hypertrophy, hypertensive heart failure, atheromatous coronary artery disease== (fatty plaques in the heart's arteries), ==strokes, hypertensive retinopathy and chronic renal failure==. The outlook is grave: ==about 50% of untreated patients eventually develop heart failure, 25% renal failure and 25% cerebral complications==, so it must be ==treated at the symptomless stage==.

### 8. Ventricular hypertrophy

The first consequence follows directly. ==Cardiac hypertrophy occurs in response to the increased afterload==, the pressure the ventricle must push against to eject ([[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling's Law and Contractility]]), so ==the heart does more work and its walls grow thicker==. ==The wall thickens without any increase in chamber size==, growing inwards, which is ==concentric hypertrophy==; in endurance athletes the thickening instead ==comes with a larger chamber==, called eccentric hypertrophy. The hypertensive wall ==becomes stiffer, losing distensibility==, so ==relaxation is impaired and diastolic filling is reduced==.

That wall is also short of oxygen. It is built of ==cardiac myocytes (muscle cells) and extracellular matrix==, and ==the myocytes grow more in diameter than in length== while ==the capillaries stay unchanged in number and diameter==, so they lie ==further apart==, the ==ratio of capillary surface area to myocyte volume falls== and ==each cell gets less oxygen==. On the electrocardiogram the thickened left ventricle gives ==tall R waves in V5, V6 and aVL and deep S waves in V1 and V2==, as more activity travels towards the left-facing leads ([[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System]]).

### 9. Treatment

Treatment therefore starts early, and ==aims to reduce systolic pressure to 135–145 mmHg and diastolic pressure to 85 mmHg==, which ==greatly reduces mortality and morbidity==. Four avenues do it, each attacking one side of MAP = CO x TPR.

| Avenue | What it lowers | Drug class | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reduce TPR | TPR | ==Calcium-channel blockers== and ==α1-adrenoceptor blockers== (peripheral vasodilators) | ==nifedipine==, ==prazosin== |
| Reduce the extracellular fluid volume, raised in hypertension | CO, through blood volume | ==Diuretics== | — |
| Inhibit angiotensin-converting enzyme | Both: less angiotensin II (tone), less aldosterone (volume) | ==ACE inhibitors== | ==captopril== |
| Reduce cardiac output | CO | ==β-adrenoceptor blockers== | ==propranolol, atenolol== |

The targets of the first and last rows are described in [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation]] and [[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate]].

### Summary

> Hypertension means ==repeated resting pressures above 140/90 mmHg under 50, or 160/95 mmHg over 50==; ==10% is secondary==, with a findable cause such as pre-eclampsia, Conn's syndrome, renal artery stenosis or a phaeochromocytoma, and ==90% is essential==. Retained salt and water raise blood volume, stroke volume, cardiac output and pressure, after which ==organs autoregulate by constricting, so a raised TPR maintains the hypertension==; with a narrowed renal artery the same job is done by a ==continuously activated renin-angiotensin system== acting through angiotensin II. Essential hypertension is usually ==benign, slow and symptomless==, driven in the young by a ==high cardiac output== and in the old by a ==high peripheral resistance==, which vessel-wall thickening and ==rarefaction== make permanent. The kidney then holds the pressure there, because its ==renal output curve is shifted right, so a high pressure is needed to excrete a normal salt load==. Untreated, ==50% develop heart failure, 25% renal failure and 25% cerebral complications==, while the pressure-loaded ventricle undergoes ==concentric hypertrophy==: a thicker, stiffer wall, an unchanged chamber and ==capillaries too far apart to supply the enlarged cells==. Treatment to ==135–145/85 mmHg== uses ==vasodilators, diuretics, ACE inhibitors and β-blockers==.
