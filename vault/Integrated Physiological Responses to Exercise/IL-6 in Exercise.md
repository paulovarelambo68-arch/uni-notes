
>IL-6 has ==two sources==: ==immune cells in infection or injury==, where it is usually ==pro-inflammatory==, and ==contracting skeletal muscle==, which makes it a ==myokine==. In exercise it rises ==up to 100-fold after a marathon==, more than any other cytokine, and ==lactate drives its release at high intensity==. Short muscle pulses are ==metabolic and anti-inflammatory== and ==mobilise NK cells, neutrophils and lymphocytes==, whereas ==chronically elevated IL-6== is ==harmful==, which is the ==IL-6 paradox==.

### 1. Two sources, two faces

IL-6, or interleukin-6, is one of the [[Cytokines]] and ==a well-established mechanism of exercise-induced immunomodulation==, meaning a main route by which exercise alters immunity. ==Several immune cells== produce it, particularly in response to ==infection or injury==, and there it is ==usually thought of as a pro-inflammatory cytokine==.

Exercise adds a second source, because ==contracting skeletal muscle== produces IL-6 in high levels, which makes it a ==myokine, a cytokine made and released by muscle==. The IL-6 gene is ==silent in resting muscle but rapidly activated in exercise==, and the size of the increase depends on ==exercise intensity, duration, the muscle mass involved and endurance==, meaning training status.

Intensity matters because ==in high-intensity exercise, lactate causes an increase in IL-6 secretion==. Lactate generated during strenuous cycling tracked closely with an ==approximately 5-fold increase in IL-6==, and in adults with obesity doing a single bout of cycling at ==80% of VO₂peak== (their highest oxygen uptake in a test, so hard work), ==a roughly 1.3-fold rise in IL-6 correlated with a roughly 3.8-fold rise in lactate==. Whether lactate drives IL-6 release in lower-intensity exercise is ==unknown==.

### 2. How much, and in what order

The muscle source is so large that ==IL-6 increases up to 100-fold following a marathon==, which makes it the cytokine with ==the most significant rise in plasma during prolonged, intense endurance exercise==.

![[Screenshot 2026-10-23 at 14.07.28.png|361]]

In this figure (Pedersen and Toft, 2000) the x axis is time, with the double arrow marking the strenuous exercise, and the y axis is the relative rise in plasma concentration. ==IL-6 rises first and towers over the rest==, peaking at about the end of exercise, and ==IL-1ra peaks after it==, IL-1ra being the IL-1 receptor antagonist, which blocks the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1. The chemokines IL-8 and MIP-1β (cytokines that attract white cells) rise modestly, while TNF-α (tumour necrosis factor alpha), its soluble receptor sTNF-αR, which mops it up, and IL-1 stay lowest. So, unlike an infection, exercise produces IL-6 ==without a large wave of the pro-inflammatory pair TNF-α and IL-1==, and ==the IL-6 peak is followed by anti-inflammatory signals==.

### 3. Metabolic effects in acute exercise

In acute exercise, meaning a single bout, this muscle IL-6 has ==anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects==. Metabolically, it ==enhances glucose uptake by muscle==, ==enhances fat oxidation to increase fat's availability as an energy source== and so ==acts as a metabolic regulator==.

![[Screenshot 2026-10-23 at 14.08.06.png|283]]

In this figure (Petersen and Pedersen, 2005) contraction switches on IL-6 mRNA in the red muscle fibre on the left, and the blue spheres are the released IL-6. Their arrows run to adipose tissue (yellow) for ==lipolysis, the breakdown of stored fat==, to the liver for ==glycogen breakdown to glucose==, back into the muscle for fat oxidation, and into the blood vessel, where small arrows show TNF falling and IL-1ra, IL-10 and soluble TNF receptor rising. The message is that one muscle signal ==releases fuel from fat and liver== while ==tilting the blood towards an anti-inflammatory state==.

Blood glucose benefits too, because acute elevations in IL-6, and training, ==stimulate secretion of GLP-1== (glucagon-like peptide-1, a gut hormone that boosts insulin release) ==from intestinal cells and pancreatic β-cells==, ==improving insulin secretion and glycaemic control== (blood glucose control). All these metabolic effects can ==indirectly support immune cell energy needs==.

### 4. Immune effects of exercise-induced IL-6

IL-6 also acts on immune cells, first by moving them into the blood, the mobilisation described in [[Mechanisms of Exercise-Induced Immune Changes]]. It ==mobilises NK cells==, the natural killer cells of [[Complement and Natural Killer Cells]] that kill infected and tumour cells, which gives it ==anti-tumour activity==, and in preclinical (mouse) models, ==IL-6-dependent NK recruitment into tumours== supports anti-tumour immunity in ==B16 melanoma and 4T1 breast cancer==. It also stimulates the ==mobilisation of neutrophils and lymphocytes== into the circulation, which ==supports immune surveillance==, patrolling for pathogens and abnormal cells.

The second effect, seen in the metabolic figure's blood vessel, is ==anti-inflammatory in acute exercise==, because IL-6 ==stimulates production of IL-10 and IL-1ra==, which are anti-inflammatory cytokines, and ==decreases production of the pro-inflammatory TNF-α==.

The third effect reaches the liver, where ==IL-6 triggers production of acute-phase proteins==, plasma proteins that surge after infection or injury, ==such as C-reactive protein (CRP)==, the inflammation marker of [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP]]. This is ==useful for the minor damage caused by exercise==.

### 5. The IL-6 paradox

These benefits come from short pulses, and the reversal when IL-6 stays high is the ==IL-6 paradox==. ==Chronically elevated circulating IL-6== is associated with ==cardiometabolic disease, heightened autoimmunity and inflammation, cancer progression and increased all-cause mortality==, meaning death from any cause. Such chronic disease involves ==sustained release from inflamed tissues, including visceral fat==, the abdominal fat inflamed in obesity ([[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP]]).

The same can happen with relentless hard training. IL-6 produced under ==chronic high-intensity exercise tends to be pro-inflammatory== and can contribute to inflammation, because it ==uses a different signalling mechanism==. Put simply, brief muscle pulses of IL-6 signal in a way that does not switch on TNF-α and IL-1, whereas IL-6 that stays high, released by immune cells and inflamed tissue, signals in a way that amplifies inflammation.

The rule to remember is that ==short-lived, muscle-derived IL-6 pulses== in exercise are ==anti-inflammatory and metabolic==, whereas ==chronic IL-6 elevation== from inflamed tissue or relentless high-intensity training is ==harmful==. The long-term consequences for athletes are in [[Chronic Exercise and Immune Function – Moderate versus High Intensity]].

### Summary

>IL-6, usually a ==pro-inflammatory cytokine of infection and injury==, is also a ==myokine==: its gene switches on in exercising muscle, and its release rises with ==intensity, duration, muscle mass and endurance== and, at high intensity, ==with lactate==. It rises ==up to 100-fold after a marathon== and is ==followed by IL-1ra== rather than TNF-α and IL-1. In acute exercise it boosts ==glucose uptake, fat oxidation, lipolysis, liver glycogen breakdown and GLP-1==, ==mobilises NK cells, neutrophils and lymphocytes==, ==raises IL-10 and IL-1ra, lowers TNF-α== and ==triggers acute-phase proteins such as CRP==. In the ==IL-6 paradox==, ==chronic elevation== from inflamed tissue such as visceral fat, or from chronic high-intensity training with its ==different signalling mechanism==, is ==pro-inflammatory== and linked to ==cardiometabolic disease, autoimmunity, cancer progression and all-cause mortality==.
