
> When a microbe invades, innate cells first recognise it ==non-specifically==, because their receptors bind ==structures shared by whole groups of microbes==. ==Neutrophils and macrophages==, the most important phagocytes, then destroy it by phagocytosis, whose three steps, ==recognition, ingestion and digestion==, unfold as seven phases. Afterwards neutrophils commonly die tidily by ==apoptosis==, while some release their DNA as ==neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)== that catch pathogens.

### 1. How innate cells know an enemy

Pathogen recognition by innate cells is ==non-specific== ([[Immune System Overview – Functions and the Two Branches]]). To recognise microbes, innate cells express ==pathogen recognition receptors (PRRs)==, also called pattern recognition receptors, such as the ==Toll-like receptors (TLRs)==. PRRs bind ==pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)==, ==structures that whole groups of microbes share== but our own cells do not make. The classic example is ==lipopolysaccharide (LPS)==, a fat joined to a long sugar chain, which is ==present on all Gram-negative bacteria==.

The term Gram-negative refers to the Gram stain, a dye test that sorts bacteria by wall structure, since Gram-positive bacteria keep its purple dye and Gram-negative bacteria lose it. The molecules of each wall are exactly the patterns that PRRs read.

Because these molecules are shared, one receptor serves against many microbes. In the second figure each yellow innate cell carries a TLR, drawn as a stalk topped by a dashed hook, and the same hook grips three microbes that look nothing alike, because ==identical TLRs recognise a common structure on different microbes==. That is what non-specific means, and since the receptors are inherited ready-made, nothing has to be learned, so the innate system needs no memory. It also explains why innate cells such as neutrophils are ==present and functional from birth and respond immediately, but do not develop or adapt over a lifetime==, as lymphocytes do.

### 2. Phagocytosis: who does it and the three steps

Once recognised, a microbe can be destroyed by phagocytosis, literally 'cell eating', in which a cell engulfs it whole and digests it. ==Neutrophils and macrophages are the most important phagocytes== ([[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]]), and ==following infection they can leave the blood and travel to the site of infection==, macrophages arriving as monocytes that mature in the tissue.

In either cell, phagocytosis involves ==recognition, ingestion and digestion==, whose initials spell RID. Recognition uses the PRRs above and also ==opsonins==, molecules that coat a microbe to mark it for eating, which complement proteins provide ([[Complement and Natural Killer Cells]]).

### 3. The seven phases

The figure breaks those three steps into seven phases:

![[Screenshot 2026-10-22 at 20.48.50 1.png]]

The cut-open phagocyte shows the microbe (or other particle) as a brown oval and digestive enzymes as blue dots, and black arrows trace its path through the numbered phases. The process begins with ==chemotaxis and adherence (1)==. Chemotaxis is movement up a chemical gradient, which means that the phagocyte crawls towards rising concentrations of attractants, such as microbial products and some [[Cytokines]], until the microbe adheres to it, and this contact is the recognition step. In ==ingestion (2)== the cell wraps pseudopods ('false feet', arm-like extensions of the cell) around the microbe until they fuse, sealing it inside a ==phagosome (3)==, a membrane-bound vesicle also called a phagocytic vesicle. 

Digestion follows. The phagosome fuses with a ==lysosome==, a vesicle full of digestive enzymes, to form a ==phagolysosome (4)==, where the enzymes ==digest the microbe (5)==. Anything indigestible is left in a ==residual body (6)==, which ==discharges the waste (7)== at the cell surface. The lesson is how the three steps map onto the seven phases, with recognition at phase 1, ingestion at 2 and 3, and digestion and clean-up at 4 to 7.

### 4. What happens to neutrophils afterwards: NETs

The neutrophil's own fate completes the story. ==Following phagocytosis, neutrophils have a short lifespan and commonly undergo apoptosis==, a programmed, tidy cell death in which the cell packages itself up for macrophages to clear without spilling its contents. This quiet removal ==contributes to the resolution of inflammation==, meaning that the local inflammatory reaction settles once the microbes are cleared.

Alternatively, some neutrophils undergo ==NETosis==. Their ==nuclear chromatin==, the DNA of the nucleus with the proteins it is wound around, ==is released into the extracellular space==, where it forms a matrix, or mesh, known as ==neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)==. NETs ==trap pathogens==, which may then be phagocytosed by other neutrophils and macrophages, and the neutrophil ==may or may not die== after NETosis.

![[Screenshot 2026-10-22 at 20.49.15 1.png|385]]

In this colour-enhanced micrograph the neutrophil is yellow, the web spreading from it is its released chromatin and the orange rods are trapped bacteria, which is a NET at work.

Through this trapping ==NETosis can contribute to pathogen killing==, but ==excessive NETosis contributes to thrombosis, autoimmunity and cancer progression==, thrombosis being clotting inside blood vessels and autoimmunity an immune attack on the body's own tissues.

### Summary

> Innate recognition is ==non-specific==, because ==PRRs such as TLRs bind PAMPs== shared by whole groups of microbes, like ==LPS on all Gram-negative bacteria==, so identical TLRs recognise a common structure on different microbes and no memory is needed. ==Neutrophils and macrophages==, the most important phagocytes, leave the blood and destroy microbes by ==recognition, ingestion and digestion==, which run as seven phases from chemotaxis and adherence to the discharge of waste, with digestion inside a ==phagolysosome==. Afterwards neutrophils commonly undergo ==apoptosis, which helps inflammation resolve==, or ==NETosis==, releasing their chromatin as NETs that trap pathogens. NETosis helps kill pathogens, but in excess it contributes to ==thrombosis, autoimmunity and cancer progression==.
