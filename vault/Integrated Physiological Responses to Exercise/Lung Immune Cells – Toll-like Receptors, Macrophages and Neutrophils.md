
> Every breath pulls microbes into the lungs, and the barriers of the airways stop most of them. The cells described here deal with the rest. There are three characters: the sensors that notice a microbe, the macrophage that fights or repairs, and the neutrophil that kills and dies.

### 1. Who guards the lung

Three characters guard the lung, met here in the order they act. Pattern recognition receptors, protein sensors on and inside immune cells, notice a microbe and raise the alarm. The alveolar macrophage, a large eating cell that lives in the air sacs, answers the alarm. It deals with whatever got past the barriers of [[The Lung's First Line of Defence – Nose, Cilia, Mucus and Antimicrobial Peptides]]. The neutrophil then pours in from the blood, kills with chemicals and dies. The cells themselves, their origins and their general jobs are described in [[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]].

### 2. How innate cells recognise a microbe

Before any cell can act, it must tell a microbe apart from the body's own tissue. The theory explaining how was ==proposed by Janeway in 1989==. Innate cells carry ==pattern recognition receptors (PRRs)==, the microbe-spotting proteins introduced in [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis]]. These receptors are ==encoded in the germline==, meaning their genes are inherited ready-made, so everyone is born with the same set rather than learning it.

There are ==about 100 different PRRs==, each with a fixed specificity: it recognises one kind of pattern and never varies. Examples are the ==Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and the NOD-like receptors==. The first sit on the cell surface or inside the cell, the second only inside.

Both recognise ==pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)==, structures shared by whole classes of microbes. Examples are peptidoglycan from bacterial walls and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from the outer wall of some bacteria. More than a thousand are known, and they make good targets for two reasons. They are usually ==essential to the microbe's survival==, so it cannot drop them. And they are ==never found on the body's own cells==, so the alarm never fires at healthy tissue.

### 3. Toll-like receptors: structure and signal

The Toll-like receptors are the best-studied sensors, and their build explains how the alarm works. A Toll-like receptor has ==three parts==. Outside the cell is a horseshoe made of ==18 to 25 copies of a leucine-rich repeat==, a short protein motif rich in the amino acid leucine. The inner curve of the horseshoe grips the microbial pattern. A single helix crosses the membrane, and inside the cell hangs the ==TIR domain==, named after the Toll and interleukin-1 receptors, which both carry it.

![[Pulm5_slide09_tlr_structure.png]]

The drawing shows a pair of Toll-like receptors, labelled TLR1-TLR2 heterodimer, in the cell membrane. Above it the two leucine-rich repeat (LRR) motifs curve like horseshoes around the bound molecule, labelled tri-acylated lipopeptides, a fatty pattern from bacterial surfaces. Two transmembrane helices cross the membrane and the two intracellular TIR domains hang below. The horseshoes closing around the pattern is the moment the alarm goes off, because closing brings the two TIR domains together. Only the pair can start the signal, like two keys that must be turned at once.

When a pattern binds, ==two receptors usually pair up (dimerise)==, and their TIR domains recruit adaptor proteins, mainly MyD88 and TRIF. These start a cascade of proteins activating other proteins, with two results. One is phagocytosis, the swallowing of the microbe. The other is the activation of transcription factors, proteins that enter the nucleus and switch particular genes on, chief among them ==NF-kB==.

The genes switched on make cytokines, the immune system's messenger molecules, covered in [[Cytokines]]. They also make co-stimulatory molecules, the second signals needed to activate a T cell, covered in [[T Cells and Antigen Presentation]]. So one receptor gripping one pattern ends in a cell that eats, calls for help and can wake the adaptive system.

![[Pulm5_slide10_tlr_family_and_ligands.png]]

This cell keeps its sensors in two places. On the plasma membrane sit TLR-1:TLR-2, TLR-2, TLR-4, TLR-5 and TLR-2:TLR-6, matched to bacterial lipopeptides, peptidoglycan, LPS and flagellin, the protein of the bacterial tail. Inside the cell, in the membrane of the endosome where swallowed material is broken down, sit TLR-3, TLR-7, TLR-8 and TLR-9. These recognise the double-stranded RNA, single-stranded RNA and CpG DNA that a virus exposes only once it is inside. About ten sensors therefore cover bacteria outside and viruses inside.

TLR4 is the worked example for the lung. It is found on monocytes, the blood cells that become macrophages, and in many tissues. ==TLR4 recognises lipopolysaccharide==, the LPS in the outer wall of Gram-negative bacteria, a wall type explained in [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis]]. Among these bacteria, ==Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumoniae== cause pneumonia, so TLR4 is a front-line sensor of lung infection.

TLR4 also recognises ==damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)== such as HMGB1 and S100A8. These molecules stay inside healthy cells and spill out when the body's own cells are injured, so the same receptor answers injury as well as infection. A bruised lung with no microbe in it still raises an inflammatory alarm. That helps clear the damage, but it can also keep inflammation going when there is nothing to kill.

### 4. The macrophage: two forms

The first cell to answer the alarm is usually a macrophage, and the lung has two kinds. ==Alveolar macrophages== are residents that live in the air spaces and are the first cells to meet an inhaled microbe. ==Peripheral macrophages== are recruits. They begin as blood monocytes, which leave the vessels during an infection and mature into macrophages in the tissue, a lineage drawn in [[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]].

The right panel, labelled Resident e.g. AM, shows the same journey ending in the interstitial and alveolar macrophages that already live in the lung. The lung sits in the middle, so its defence is a mix of residents and recruits.

Either kind can ==polarise==, which means settle into one of two working states depending on the signals around it. Think of a soldier and a repair crew.

The soldier is the ==M1 macrophage, which is broadly pro-inflammatory==. It is switched on by ==interferon-gamma, LPS, TNF-alpha and GM-CSF==, signals that mean a microbe is present. It then makes the pro-inflammatory cytokines ==IL-1 beta, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha and interferons==, which call in and rouse other cells. It kills by making ==nitric oxide through the enzyme iNOS== and reactive oxygen species, aggressive forms of oxygen, and it phagocytoses more than a resting cell. Its surface markers ==CD80, CD86 and HLA-DR== are the molecules that present antigen and activate T cells, so it also hands the fight to the adaptive system.

The repair crew is the ==M2 macrophage, which is broadly anti-inflammatory==, with several sub-types. It is switched on by ==IL-4, IL-13, IL-10, TGF-beta, the steroid hormones called glucocorticoids, and adenosine==, signals that mean the fight is ending or must be calmed. It makes ==IL-10, TGF-beta and VEGF==. The first two damp inflammation, and VEGF is a growth factor that grows new blood vessels into healing tissue. It phagocytoses even more than M1, because clearing dead cells is part of repair, and its markers are ==CD163 and CD206==.

The same two states appeared in fat tissue in [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP]]. A healthy response ==starts with M1 to kill and ends with M2 to repair==.

### 5. The neutrophil

When the macrophage's cytokines reach the blood, the third character arrives. The ==neutrophil== is a granulocyte, a white cell packed with granules of killing proteins, from the cast in [[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]]. It is the most common white blood cell, ==up to 70 per cent of all white cells==. A microlitre of blood holds ==2,500 to 7,000 of them==, and the total count is a critical indicator of health. Neutrophils circulate in an inactive state, ==live only hours to days==, and are constantly replaced from the bone marrow.

Injury switches them on in three steps. First the neutrophil finds the site. Damaged tissue and activated macrophages release chemical signals that spread outward as a ==chemotactic gradient==, a trail that grows stronger towards its source. Surface G-protein-coupled receptors sense the trail and rearrange the cell's actin skeleton, its internal scaffold. The cell then crawls up the gradient and out of the blood vessel.

Second, at the site, it becomes fully active. It ==releases its granule proteins and phagocytoses== the microbes. It also throws out ==neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)==, webs of its own DNA that snare bacteria, described in [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis]].

Third, it dies. Within hours it ==enters apoptosis==, the tidy programmed death from the same note, which limits the damage its chemicals would otherwise do to healthy tissue. Macrophages in the ==liver, bone marrow and spleen== clear the dead cells.

### 6. The oxidative burst

The neutrophil's main weapon is the ==oxidative burst, also called the respiratory burst==, a sudden production of reactive oxygen species. The second name has nothing to do with breathing: it comes from the sharp rise in the cell's oxygen use when the burst starts. The burst happens in the phagosome, the pouch that closes around a swallowed microbe. The phagosome then fuses with lysosomes, the cell's bags of digestive enzymes, and turns acidic. The enzyme ==NADPH oxidase (NOX2)== sits in the phagosome membrane and makes superoxide, the first reactive oxygen species.

In words, two oxygen molecules take electrons from the carrier NADPH and become two superoxide ions. As a formula, ==2 O2 + NADPH -> 2 O2- + NADP+ + H+==, where O2 is oxygen, NADPH the electron donor, O2- superoxide, NADP+ the spent donor and H+ a hydrogen ion. Superoxide is then converted to ==hydrogen peroxide==. The neutrophil also turns hydrogen peroxide and chloride into ==hypochlorous acid==, the active ingredient of household bleach. These chemicals kill by oxidising the microbe, stripping electrons from its proteins, fats and DNA until it falls apart, like bleach on a worktop.

==The body's own tissue resists them better than the pathogen does==, which is why such a crude weapon can be used. Even so, the burst is double-edged. Neutrophils also release reactive oxygen species outside the cell. They ==need this to cut a path through the extracellular matrix==, the protein mesh between cells, but it damages nearby tissue.

The burst is used as a ==test of neutrophil health==, and it is the function that chronic hard training impairs, as [[Chronic Exercise and Immune Function – Moderate versus High Intensity]] explains. Nor is it perfect: some bacteria, ==Staphylococcus aureus== among them, survive inside the phagosome and escape.

### 7. When neutrophils run out

Because neutrophils die so quickly, a long infection can exhaust the supply. In the ==late phase of pneumonia== they are used up faster than the bone marrow can replace them. The result is ==neutropenia==, too few neutrophils in the blood. With its front-line killers gone, the lung is open to a ==second infection about a week after the illness began==.

The risk is treated with ==preventive (prophylactic) antibiotics and drugs that stimulate the neutrophil's precursor cells== in the marrow. The same neutropenia follows ==acute radiation sickness==, from radiotherapy or a nuclear event. Radiation kills dividing cells, and the marrow cells that make neutrophils never stop dividing.

### Summary

> The lung's cellular defence starts with ==pattern recognition receptors==, about 100 inherited sensors that recognise ==pathogen-associated molecular patterns== shared by classes of microbes and absent from the body's own cells. A ==Toll-like receptor== has a leucine-rich horseshoe outside, a helix through the membrane and a ==TIR domain== inside, and two receptors pairing around a pattern activate ==NF-kB==, which switches on cytokine genes. ==TLR4== recognises ==LPS== from Gram-negative bacteria and also ==DAMPs== from injured cells, so it answers injury as well as infection. Macrophages ==polarise== into ==M1==, the pro-inflammatory killer switched on by interferon-gamma and LPS, making IL-1 beta, IL-6, TNF-alpha, nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species, or ==M2==, the anti-inflammatory repairer switched on by IL-4, IL-13 and IL-10, making IL-10, TGF-beta and VEGF. The ==neutrophil==, the most common white cell at ==2,500 to 7,000 per microlitre==, follows a ==chemotactic gradient== to the site, degranulates, phagocytoses, throws NETs and dies by ==apoptosis== within hours. Its ==oxidative burst== uses ==NADPH oxidase== to make superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and ==hypochlorous acid==, which kill the microbe but also damage tissue. In late pneumonia the marrow cannot keep up, ==neutropenia== follows, and the ==second infection about a week in== is prevented with antibiotics and marrow stimulation.
