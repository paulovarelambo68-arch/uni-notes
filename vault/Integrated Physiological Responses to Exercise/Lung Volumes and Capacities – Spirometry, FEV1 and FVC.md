
> The chest works as a pump, and this note measures that pump. It covers how much air moves with each breath, how much never leaves the lungs, and how fast air can be blown out. Those measurements tell healthy lungs from diseased ones and show what exercise does to each breath.

### 1. Measuring a breath: the spirogram

A spirometer is an instrument that records the volume of air breathed in and out against time, and the trace it draws is a ==spirogram==. Every value below is a guideline, because lung volumes vary with body size, sex and age.

The normal breath comes first. ==Tidal volume (VT)== is the air moved in or out in one normal breath, about 500 mL. The extra air that can still be breathed in after a normal breath in is the ==inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)==, about 3.0 L. The extra air that can still be pushed out after a normal breath out is the ==expiratory reserve volume (ERV)==, about 1.2 L.

Adding the three gives the biggest breath a person can take, the ==forced vital capacity (FVC), about 4.7 L==. In symbols, ==FVC = VT + IRV + ERV==.

### 2. The air that never leaves: residual volume

Even the hardest breath out leaves air in the lungs, and that air is the ==residual volume (RLV)==, the bracket RV on the trace. It averages ==0.8 to 1.2 L in healthy young women== and 0.9 to 1.4 L in young men. A spirometer cannot measure it, because that air never comes out of the mouth.

Residual volume rises with age, because elastic tissue is lost and replaced by stiffer scar-like tissue, a change called ==fibrosis==. The lung then springs back less at the end of a breath out, so more air stays behind.

This trapped air has a job. Because the alveoli never empty, blood passing them between breaths always finds air to exchange gases with, like a bath that is never fully drained. So residual volume ==keeps gas exchange going between breaths==, and blood oxygen and carbon dioxide stay steady instead of swinging with each breath.

Adding residual volume to the biggest breath gives the whole lung, the ==total lung capacity (TLC)==, about 5.9 L. In symbols, ==TLC = RLV + FVC==.

Exercise also changes residual volume, in a way that is easy to get backwards. During exercise it dips slightly, but for a while ==after a bout it rises==, for two reasons.

The first reason is that ==small airways deep in the lung close early==. These tiny tubes have no stiff wall of their own. They stay open only because the stretched lung tissue around them pulls on them. At the end of a full breath out the lung is small, so that pull is weak, and the squeeze from the chest can flatten them like a soft straw pinched shut. After exercise they flatten sooner than usual, so air in the alveoli behind them is trapped before it can leave.

The second reason is that ==extra blood stays in the vessels in and around the lungs== for a while after exercise. That blood takes up space inside the chest. The lungs therefore cannot be squeezed down as far as usual, so a little more air is left behind at the end of the breath out.

### 3. How fast, not just how much: dynamic volumes and FEV1

The volumes so far are static: they say how much air the lungs hold, not how fast it moves. Moving air over time is dynamic, and this ==dynamic capacity depends on both the size of the biggest breath and the speed of breathing==. Speed is tested with a timed forced breath out.

The person fills the lungs and blows out as hard and fast as possible. The air out in the first second is the ==forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1)==. Dividing it by FVC gives the fraction of the vital capacity emptied in one second, ==FEV1/FVC==, which indicates the lung's capacity for airflow. A high ratio needs strong expiratory muscles to push and open airways to let air through, so it ==reflects expiratory power and airway resistance==.

In healthy people the ratio is about ==85 per cent==. A ratio of ==70 per cent or less indicates airway obstruction==, meaning narrowed airways slow the air out. Four diseases do this:
- ==COPD== (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) is long-term narrowing and damage of the airways, mostly from smoking, covered in [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold]].
- ==Asthma== is reversible narrowing of inflamed airways, covered in [[Lung Disease and Exercise – ARDS, Asthma and COVID]].
- ==Bronchiectasis== is permanently widened airways that fill with mucus.
- ==Cystic fibrosis== is a genetic disease whose thick, sticky mucus blocks the airways.

When air leaves slowly, the lungs cannot empty before the next breath is due, so air is trapped, the ==lungs over-inflate== and oxygenation suffers.

### 4. Narrow airways or stiff lungs: obstructive versus restrictive disease

The ratio is read together with the forced vital capacity, because lung disease comes in two broad kinds. ==Obstructive disease== is a problem of breathing out: narrowed airways let air leave slowly. ==Restrictive disease== is a problem of breathing in: stiff lungs cannot fill, so they hold less. Stiffness means low compliance, the ease with which the lung stretches, explained in [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre]].

Obstruction is like squeezing a full bottle through a narrow neck, which empties slowly. Restriction is like squeezing a small, rigid bottle, which empties fast but never held much.

Both diseased lungs share the same small FVC, which is why the ratio tells them apart. Obstruction shows as ==a low ratio==, whatever the FVC. Restriction shows as ==a low FVC with a normal or high ratio==, because what little air there is leaves fast.

### 5. What one bout of exercise does to each volume

The volumes measured at rest all shift once exercise begins, and the table lists each shift. It also includes the breathing rate and the minute ventilation, the total air breathed in one minute, explained in [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt]].

| Volume | Resting value | Change in acute exercise |
|---|---|---|
| Tidal volume (VT) | 500 mL per breath | Increases up to four times |
| Breathing frequency | 12 to 15 breaths per minute | May rise to 60 breaths per minute |
| Minute ventilation (VE) | 6 L/min | Up to 175 L/min in large, aerobically trained athletes |
| Inspiratory reserve volume | 3,000 mL | Decreases |
| Expiratory reserve volume | 1,200 mL | Decreases |
| Vital capacity | 4,700 mL | Slight decrease |
| Residual volume | 1,200 mL | Slight decrease |
| Total lung capacity | 5,900 mL | Slight decrease |

The pattern is simple. Each breath grows by eating into both reserves, so both shrink, and tidal volume swells to as much as ==four times its resting size==. It takes more from the inspiratory reserve, because there is far more room to grow upwards, 3.0 L, than downwards, 1.2 L. The capacities barely change, because the size of the lungs and chest is fixed and exercise only changes how much of that space each breath uses.

### Summary

> A spirometer records lung volume against time, and ==a capacity is the sum of two or more volumes==. Tidal volume is about 500 mL, the inspiratory reserve about 3.0 L and the expiratory reserve about 1.2 L, and together they make the ==forced vital capacity, about 4.7 L==. The air that can never be breathed out is the ==residual volume, about 1.2 L==, which keeps gas exchange going between breaths, rises with age through fibrosis and rises for a while after exercise. FEV1 is the air blown out in the first second of a forced exhalation, and ==FEV1/FVC is about 85 per cent in health==. A ratio of ==70 per cent or less means obstruction==, as in COPD, asthma, bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis, because narrowed airways slow the air out. ==Restriction gives a low FVC with a normal or high ratio==, because stiff lungs cannot fill but what they hold leaves fast. In exercise ==tidal volume rises up to four times== by eating into both reserves, breathing rate can reach 60 breaths per minute, and the capacities change only slightly.
