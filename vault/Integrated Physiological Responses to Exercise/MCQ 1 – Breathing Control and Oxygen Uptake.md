Ten multiple-choice questions on the control of breathing and oxygen uptake: the brainstem centres, the dorsal respiratory group, the central and peripheral chemoreceptors, the three phases of exercise ventilation, the ventilatory threshold, VO2max and the onset of blood lactate accumulation. Written in the style of the lecturer's example questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## The respiratory control centres are located in the

- [ ] cerebellum and cortex
- [x] medulla and pons
- [ ] hypothalamus and thalamus
- [ ] spinal cord and cerebellum

> The medulla directly controls automatic breathing, taking input from the control centres of the pons and from the chemoreceptors, and it can also take input from higher brain centres to produce voluntary breathing. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#2. The rhythm generators in the medulla]].

## The neurons that set the normal respiratory cycle at rest are the

- [ ] pneumotaxic centre
- [ ] ventral respiratory group
- [x] dorsal respiratory group of the medulla
- [ ] apneustic centre

> The DRG acts as the pacemaker, generating repetitive bursts of action potentials that contract the diaphragm and intercostal muscles. As exercise intensity rises the ventral respiratory group adds further inspiratory signals and drives the expiratory muscles. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#2. The rhythm generators in the medulla]].

## The central chemoreceptors of the medulla provide what share of the ventilatory response to a rise in arterial PCO2?

- [x] 80%
- [ ] 20%
- [ ] 50%
- [ ] 5%

> About 80% comes from the central chemoreceptors and 20% from the peripheral ones; the peripheral response is weaker but about five times faster, and the peripheral receptors signal to the medulla through the glossopharyngeal and vagal nerves. The increased medullary activity raises ventilation until PaCO2 falls again. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## Compared with the central chemoreceptors, the peripheral chemoreceptors' response to CO2 is

- [ ] stronger and faster
- [ ] stronger but slower
- [ ] weaker and slower
- [x] weaker but about five times faster

> The peripheral receptors give the initial quick response to a rise in PaCO2 (sensed as acidosis, a rise in H+), while the central receptors supply most of the response. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## A fall in arterial PO2 increases alveolar ventilation through the

- [ ] central chemoreceptors of the medulla
- [x] aortic and carotid chemoreceptors
- [ ] apneustic centre
- [ ] stretch receptors of the lung

> Only the peripheral chemoreceptors respond to low PO2; the carotid bodies monitor arterial blood just before it perfuses the brain. These receptors protect against reduced oxygen in inspired air and become especially important in lung disease and at high altitude. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## The abrupt rise in breathing at the very start of exercise (phase I) is caused by

- [ ] a rise in arterial PCO2
- [ ] a fall in arterial PO2
- [x] neurogenic stimuli from the cerebral cortex and the active limbs
- [ ] a rise in body temperature

> Phase I is neurogenic. Phase II is the gradual rise to a steady level from central command plus the medullary neurons, chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors, and phase III is the fine tuning by peripheral sensory feedback (temperature, CO2 and H+). No single factor controls breathing in exercise. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#7. Putting it together the three phases]].

## The ventilatory threshold is the point at which

- [x] pulmonary ventilation increases disproportionately with oxygen uptake
- [ ] oxygen uptake stops rising
- [ ] breathing rate begins to rise
- [ ] arterial PO2 falls

> Up to that point ventilation rises in line with VO2; beyond it, the extra CO2 released by the buffering of the lactate that is beginning to accumulate from anaerobic metabolism drives ventilation up faster than VO2. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#8. Ventilation against work the ventilatory threshold]].

## VO2max is expressed, to allow comparison between endurance athletes, in

- [ ] mmHg
- [ ] litres per kilogram
- [ ] breaths per minute
- [x] mL·kg-1·min-1

> VO2max, the maximum capacity to transport and use oxygen in incremental exercise, can be given as litres of O2 per minute, or as millilitres per kilogram of body weight per minute, which removes the effect of body size. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#3. The ceiling VO2max and how it is tested]].

## In healthy untrained subjects the onset of blood lactate accumulation occurs at about

- [ ] 25–35% of VO2max
- [x] 55–65% of VO2max
- [ ] 80% of VO2max
- [ ] 100% of VO2max

> OBLA is the break point where lactate production outruns clearance and blood lactate rises sharply, marking the shift from predominantly aerobic to predominantly anaerobic energy production. It can be at or above 80% of VO2max in highly trained endurance athletes. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#4. How much of the ceiling you can use the lactate threshold]].

## VO2max is reached when

- [ ] heart rate stops rising
- [ ] blood lactate first rises
- [x] oxygen consumption stays at a steady state despite a further increase in workload
- [ ] ventilation stops rising

> The plateau of VO2 against workload defines VO2max. It measures aerobic endurance potential but is not necessarily a predictor of performance; the lactate threshold shows how much of that potential is being used. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#3. The ceiling VO2max and how it is tested]].
