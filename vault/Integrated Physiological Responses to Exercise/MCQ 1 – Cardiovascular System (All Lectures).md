## In relation to the arrangement of the vascular beds of the systemic circulation, which statement is correct:

- [ ] The whole of the cardiac output passes through each organ in turn
- [ ] Blood flow to an individual organ cannot be altered without altering flow to the others
- [x] The total resistance of resistances arranged in parallel is much lower than if arranged in series
- [ ] The pulmonary and systemic circulations are arranged in parallel

> In parallel the conductances add (1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...), so two equal resistances give R/2 in parallel but 2R in series, and each organ's own arterioles set its share of the output independently. Only part of the cardiac output crosses any one tissue at a time, and the pulmonary and systemic circuits are in series. See [[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation#3. Vascular beds in parallel]].

## In relation to the distribution of blood volume (which statement is correct):

- [ ] About a quarter of the blood volume is in the capillaries
- [x] About two-thirds of the blood volume is in the systemic veins and venules
- [ ] About half of the blood volume is in the pulmonary circulation
- [ ] The systemic arteries contain more blood than the systemic veins

> The venous side has more and larger vessels, and these thin-walled, distensible capacitance vessels hold about two-thirds of the 4 to 6 L; the arteries hold about an eighth, the capillaries about a twentieth and the pulmonary circuit about a tenth. See [[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation#6. Where the blood is]].

## In relation to the cardiac cycle at rest (which statement is correct):

- [ ] Closure of the AV valves gives rise to the second heart sound
- [ ] Approximately 80% of ventricular filling is due to atrial contraction
- [x] Closure of the semilunar valves marks the end of systole and the beginning of diastole
- [ ] End-diastolic volume at rest is approximately 60 mL

> When ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure the aortic and pulmonary valves close (S2, the incisura) and the cycle enters isovolumetric relaxation. AV-valve closure gives S1; filling is about 80% passive with atrial contraction adding the final 20%; 60 mL is the end-systolic volume, the EDV being about 130 mL. See [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#5. Closing the loop]] and [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#3. Diastole in three steps]].

## In relation to the right and left sides of the heart, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Systolic pressure in the pulmonary artery is approximately 120 mmHg
- [ ] End-diastolic volume of the left ventricle is about twice that of the right ventricle
- [ ] Diastolic pressure in the pulmonary artery is higher than in the aorta
- [x] The right ventricle ejects the same stroke volume as the left ventricle, but at a much lower pressure

> Because the two circuits are in series both ventricles handle the same volumes (EDV 130 mL, ESV 60 mL, SV 70 mL), but pulmonary resistance is far below TPR, so the pulmonary artery runs at 25/8 mmHg against 120/80 mmHg in the aorta. See [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#6. The numbers for both sides]].

## In relation to Starling's law of the heart, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Preload is the arterial pressure the ventricle must overcome in order to eject
- [x] An increase in venous return raises end-diastolic volume and therefore stroke volume
- [ ] An increase in preload moves the heart onto a higher Starling curve
- [ ] The law depends on an intact sympathetic innervation of the heart

> Preload is the stretched condition of the muscle at the end of diastole (EDV): more venous return stretches the fibres towards their optimum length and they contract harder, along the same Starling curve. Afterload is the pressure to be overcome; moving to a higher curve is a change in contractility; and the law needs no nerve, which is why it keeps the two ventricles' outputs equal and serves the transplanted heart. See [[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling’s Law and Contractility#3. Starling's law of the heart]].

## In relation to excitation–contraction coupling in cardiac muscle, which statement is correct:

- [ ] About 75% of the Ca2+ needed for contraction enters from the extracellular fluid
- [ ] Ca2+ binds to tropomyosin, which then moves troponin away from the myosin-binding sites
- [ ] During systole the intracellular Ca2+ concentration rises about 2-fold
- [x] Ca2+ entering through L-type channels triggers the release of a larger amount of Ca2+ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum

> This is calcium-induced calcium release: the 25% that enters through the L-type channels opens the ryanodine receptors and the SR supplies the other 75%, raising intracellular Ca2+ 10- to 100-fold. Ca2+ binds troponin C, and it is tropomyosin that moves to uncover the binding sites on actin. See [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation#2. The calcium cycle during systole]].

## In relation to β-adrenergic (sympathetic) stimulation of the cardiac myocyte, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [x] Phosphorylation of phospholamban by protein kinase A increases its inhibition of SERCA
- [ ] Noradrenaline binds to β1-adrenergic receptors and raises intracellular cyclic AMP
- [ ] Protein kinase A phosphorylates the L-type Ca2+ channel, increasing Ca2+ entry
- [ ] Phosphorylation of troponin I inhibits the binding of Ca2+ to troponin C and speeds relaxation

> Protein kinase means phosphorylation, and phosphorylated phospholamban inhibits SERCA less, so Ca2+ is pumped back into the SR faster (the lusitropic effect) and the SR is loaded for the next beats. The other three steps are the cascade as drawn: β1 receptor, cAMP, PKA, L-type channel (positive inotropic effect) and troponin I. See [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation#4. Sympathetic stimulation more force, faster, and faster relaxation]].

## In relation to the ventricular muscle action potential, which statement is correct:

- [x] The plateau (phase 2) is produced by a small but sustained inward Ca2+ current through L-type channels
- [ ] The upstroke (phase 0) is caused by a rapid increase in permeability to Ca2+
- [ ] Early repolarisation (phase 1) is due to an inward Na+ current
- [ ] Late repolarisation (phase 3) is caused by Na+ leaving the cell

> The inward Ca2+ current almost balances the outward K+ current, holding the membrane near 0 to −20 mV. The upstroke is the fast Na+ current (iNa), the notch of phase 1 is the transient outward K+ current (ito), and phase 3 is K+ leaving the cell (iK) as the Ca2+ channels inactivate. See [[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period#3. The five phases]].

## In relation to the refractory periods of cardiac muscle, which statement is correct:

- [ ] The absolute refractory period lasts about 50 ms
- [ ] The refractory period is shorter than the contraction, so a fused (tetanic) contraction is possible
- [ ] Excitability returns as soon as the h gates of the fast Na+ channels close
- [x] The relative refractory period lasts about 50 ms and only a stronger than normal stimulus can excite the cell

> The absolute refractory period (about 200 ms) covers the plateau, while the h gates are shut; once repolarisation reaches about −40 mV some h gates reopen and excitability returns gradually over about 50 ms, giving a smaller, slower upstroke. The cell is refractory for 200 to 400 ms but contracts for only 200 to 250 ms, so contractions can never fuse. See [[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period#5. Refractory periods and the sodium-channel gates]].

## Which one of the following responses is caused by sympathetic stimulation of the SA node?

- [x] Increased slope of the pacemaker potential
- [ ] Hyperpolarisation of the SA node cells
- [ ] Activation of acetylcholine-sensitive K+ channels
- [ ] Reduction of the inward "funny" Na+ current (if)

> Noradrenaline on β1 receptors raises cAMP, which boosts if directly and, through protein kinase A, phosphorylates the Ca2+ channels, so the climb of phase 4 steepens and threshold comes sooner. Hyperpolarisation, KACh activation and a smaller if are the vagal (acetylcholine) effects. See [[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate#3. Autonomic control of heart rate]].

## In relation to the transplanted (denervated) heart, which statement is correct:

- [x] The resting heart rate is about 100 beats/min because the vagal brake is absent
- [ ] Heart rate cannot increase during exercise
- [ ] The resting heart rate is about 40 beats/min, the intrinsic rate of the AV node
- [ ] The baroreceptor reflex slows the heart when arterial pressure rises

> With its nerves cut the heart beats at the SA node's own rate, about 100/min. In exercise the rate still rises, but later and less, because only adrenaline reaching the β1 receptors through the blood can drive it, while stroke volume rises first by Starling's law; the baroreflex, which works through the cut nerves, cannot change its rate. See [[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate#4. Why the SA node is the pacemaker, and the heart without nerves]].

## When travelling at +60° in the frontal plane, a ventricular depolarisation wave causes a large positive deflection in which lead?

- [ ] aVL
- [ ] aVR
- [ ] Lead I
- [x] Lead II

> Lead II runs from the right arm to the left leg, which is +60° on the hexaxial system, so a wave along it gives the tallest R; this is the normal mean QRS axis, which is why lead II is the usual rhythm strip. Lead I is 0°, aVL −30° and aVR −150°, where the same wave is mainly negative. See [[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System#5. The twelve leads limb leads, the hexaxial system and chest leads]].

## In relation to the ECG leads, which statement is correct:

- [ ] The augmented limb leads require three additional electrodes placed on the chest
- [ ] The electrode on the right leg is the negative terminal of lead II
- [ ] The T wave represents repolarisation of the atria
- [x] Lead I records the voltage between the left arm (positive) and the right arm (negative)

> Einthoven's leads use the left arm, right arm and left leg: I is LA(+) to RA(−), II is LL(+) to RA(−) and III is LL(+) to LA(−); the augmented leads use the same three electrodes, combining two as the negative end, and the right leg is only the earth. ## In relation to sinus arrhythmia, which statement is correct:

- [x] Heart rate increases during inspiration because vagal activity falls
- [ ] It is an abnormal rhythm that requires treatment
- [ ] Heart rate increases during expiration
- [ ] It is caused by slowed conduction through the AV node

> During expiration lung stretch receptors raise vagal activity, slowing the SA node, and during inspiration vagal activity falls and the rate rises, so the beats bunch together on breathing in; this irregular rhythm is perfectly normal. Slowed AV conduction is first degree block. See [[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation#1. A normal irregularity sinus arrhythmia]].

## An ECG strip shows regular P waves and regular but slow QRS complexes, with no constant relationship between them. This is most likely:

- [ ] First degree AV-nodal block
- [ ] Second degree AV-nodal block
- [x] Third degree (complete) AV-nodal block
- [ ] Sinus arrhythmia

> In third degree block the AV node conducts nothing, so the atria keep their own rhythm while a slower secondary pacemaker below the block drives the ventricles, and the PR interval keeps changing. First degree block is a PR interval over 0.20 s with every P wave conducted; second degree block drops some beats (3:2, 2:1). See [[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation#2. AV-nodal block, three degrees]].

## In relation to the pressure profile of the systemic circulation, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Pressure is lowest in the capillaries because they are the narrowest vessels
- [x] The major fall in mean pressure occurs across the arterioles, from about 75 to about 35 mmHg
- [ ] The pulsatility of arterial pressure is lost in the large arteries
- [ ] Pressure falls from about 35 to 15 mmHg across the arterioles

> With the same flow crossing every level, the level that costs the most pressure has the most resistance, which makes the arterioles the resistance vessels, and it is there that the pulse is lost. Capillary pressure falls from 35 to 15 mmHg, and the lowest pressures are in the veins and right atrium (about 0 mmHg), not the capillaries. See [[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles#1. The pressure profile]] and [[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles#2. The site of resistance]].

## If the radius of an arteriole is halved, its resistance to blood flow:

- [ ] Doubles
- [ ] Increases 4-fold
- [ ] Increases 8-fold
- [x] Increases 16-fold

> Flow is proportional to r^4 (a tube of radius 2 units passes 16 units of flow where a tube of radius 1 passes 1), so resistance is proportional to 1/r^4 and halving the radius multiplies it by 2^4 = 16. See [[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles#3. Radius to the fourth power]].

## In relation to the measurement of arterial blood pressure by sphygmomanometry, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Diastolic pressure is the cuff pressure at which the Korotkoff sounds are first heard
- [ ] The Korotkoff sounds are produced by laminar flow through the brachial artery
- [x] Systolic pressure is the cuff pressure at which the Korotkoff sounds are first heard
- [ ] The cuff is inflated to just below diastolic pressure before it is deflated

> The cuff is inflated above systolic to shut the artery; as it is deflated the first tapping sounds, from turbulent spurts of blood at the peak of each beat, mark systolic pressure, and the sounds disappear when the artery stays open throughout the beat, marking diastolic pressure. ## A brachial artery pressure of 125/80 mmHg gives a pulse pressure and a mean arterial pressure of approximately:

- [x] 45 mmHg and 95 mmHg
- [ ] 45 mmHg and 102.5 mmHg
- [ ] 80 mmHg and 95 mmHg

## In relation to the definition and classification of hypertension, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Secondary hypertension represents about 90% of all cases
- [x] Most physicians diagnose hypertension when repeated resting measurements exceed 140/90 mmHg in a patient under 50 years old
- [ ] Essential hypertension has an identifiable pathological cause
- [ ] A high-normal pressure of 130–139/85–89 mmHg carries no increase in cardiovascular risk

> The dividing line is 140/90 mmHg under 50 and 160/95 mmHg over 50; secondary hypertension, with an identifiable cause, is about 10% of cases and essential hypertension, with no obvious organic cause, 90%; high-normal pressure doubles the ten-year cardiovascular risk. See [[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment#1. What counts as hypertension]].

## In relation to cardiac hypertrophy induced by hypertension, which statement is correct:

- [ ] It occurs in response to an increase in preload
- [ ] The number and diameter of the capillaries increase in proportion to the growth of the myocytes
- [x] The ventricular wall thickens without an increase in chamber size (concentric hypertrophy)
- [ ] The ventricular wall becomes more distensible, so diastolic filling improves

> The heart works harder against the raised afterload and its wall grows thicker and stiffer, impairing relaxation and filling; the myocytes widen while the capillaries stay unchanged, so they lie further apart and each cell gets less O2. The eccentric form, with a larger chamber, is the endurance athlete's heart. See [[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment#8. Ventricular hypertrophy]].

## In relation to the regulation of coronary blood flow, which statement is correct:

- [ ] The coronary arterioles dilate mainly through direct sympathetic stimulation
- [x] Increased cardiac work causes local release of adenosine and hypoxia, which dilate the coronary arterioles
- [ ] At rest the myocardium extracts only about 25% of the O2 in its blood, so it meets extra demand by extracting more
- [ ] Coronary blood flow can rise about 20-fold during exercise

> Heart muscle already removes 70 to 80% of the O2 from its blood, so more O2 can come only from more flow, which rises up to fourfold through metabolic vasodilation; sympathetic nerves raise the heart's work but do not dilate its arteries directly. See [[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow#1. Coronary supply]] and [[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow#2. Coronary flow through the cardiac cycle]].

## In relation to skin blood flow during exercise, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Vasodilation in glabrous skin is produced by an increase in sympathetic discharge
- [x] Vasodilation in non-glabrous (hairy) skin is produced by sympathetic cholinergic fibres releasing acetylcholine
- [ ] Skin blood flow rises at the onset of exercise, before core temperature changes
- [ ] The rise in skin blood flow as core temperature rises is an example of autoregulation

> At the start of exercise the sympathetic system constricts skin vessels; as warmth receptors in the hypothalamus sense the rising core temperature, hairy skin dilates actively through ACh (and NO) with sweating, while glabrous skin dilates passively because sympathetic firing to its AVAs falls. Flow that follows temperature rather than metabolism is thermoregulation, not autoregulation. See [[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow#5. Heat, cold and exercise]].
