Eight multiple-choice questions on Exercise Immunology: acute and chronic inflammation, C-reactive protein, the J-shaped curve, the open window, the mediators of exercise-induced immune changes and IL-6. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note that covers it.

## Which of the following is NOT one of the five symptoms of acute inflammation?

- [x] Fever
- [ ] Heat
- [ ] Redness
- [ ] Loss of function

> The five symptoms are heat, redness, swelling, pain and loss of function, all local and all due to inflammatory mediators causing vasodilation, increased permeability of peripheral blood vessels and recruitment of cells. Fever is a systemic effect (prostaglandins and cytokines cause it), not one of the five local signs. See [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP#2. The acute inflammatory event, step by step]].

## Which of the following is NOT one of the inflammatory mediators that cause vasodilation, increased vessel permeability and cell recruitment in acute inflammation?

- [ ] Histamine
- [ ] Prostaglandins
- [ ] Complement
- [x] Perforin

> The mediators listed in the lecture are cytokines, histamine, complement (C3a, C4a and C5a) and prostaglandins. Perforin is the hole-punching protein released by NK cells and cytotoxic T cells to kill a target cell, not a mediator of inflammation. See [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP#2. The acute inflammatory event, step by step]], [[Complement and Natural Killer Cells#3. The three functions]] and [[Complement and Natural Killer Cells#4. Natural killer cells]].

## Regarding chronic inflammation, which of the following statements is INCORRECT?

- [x] It involves the innate immune response only.
- [ ] It occurs when the immune system remains activated.
- [ ] It can be caused by a persistent stimulus such as an ongoing infection.
- [ ] In obesity, adipose tissue is an immunologically active tissue containing macrophages.

> Chronic inflammation involves both innate and adaptive immune responses; it is acute inflammation that is mainly innate, with neutrophil infiltration. Its causes are a persistent stimulus, a failure of regulation (autoimmune disease such as rheumatoid arthritis) and metabolic dysfunction such as obesity. See [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP#4. Chronic inflammation]].

## C-reactive protein (CRP) is

- [ ] a cytokine released from contracting skeletal muscle during exercise.
- [x] a protein synthesised in the liver that functions as a pattern recognition receptor.
- [ ] an antibody found in saliva and other secretions whose secretion falls after intense exercise.
- [ ] a complement protein that assembles into the membrane attack complex.

> CRP is made by the liver in response to injury (IL-6 triggers it), functions as a PRR, activates complement, acts as an opsonin and regulates inflammatory responses. It is a non-specific marker of inflammation used to screen for infection, chronic inflammation and heart disease, and in sport a persistently elevated baseline CRP can indicate chronic overload or inadequate recovery. See [[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP#5. C-reactive protein]].

## According to the J-shaped curve, which group has a below-average risk of upper respiratory tract infection?

- [ ] Sedentary individuals
- [x] Individuals doing moderate exercise workloads
- [ ] Athletes doing unusually high exercise workloads
- [ ] Both sedentary individuals and athletes doing high workloads

> Nieman’s curve takes the sedentary risk as average: moderate workloads improve immunosurveillance and lower URTI risk (near-daily brisk walking for 45 minutes gave significantly fewer days with URTI symptoms), whereas unusually high workloads decrease immunosurveillance and raise the risk above average. See [[Exercise and Infection Risk – J-Shaped Curve and Open Window#2. The J-shaped curve]] and [[Exercise and Infection Risk – J-Shaped Curve and Open Window#3. The evidence for the left arm moderate activity]].

## During the ‘open window’ that follows a bout of prolonged exhaustive exercise, the most consistent sign of weakened immune defence is

- [ ] an increase in plasma IL-6.
- [ ] an increase in neutrophil concentration.
- [x] a decrease in salivary secretion of IgA.
- [ ] a decrease in plasma cortisol.

> Decreased salivary IgA secretion is the most consistent observation during the open window, alongside changes in the numbers of circulating immune cells and the functional capacity of white blood cells. IL-6 and neutrophils do rise with exercise, but neither is a sign of weakened defence, and cortisol rises rather than falls. See [[Exercise and Infection Risk – J-Shaped Curve and Open Window#5. The open window hypothesis]].

## The sympathetic nervous system can alter immune function during exercise because

- [ ] immune cells have glucocorticoid receptors that bind the cortisol it releases.
- [ ] contracting skeletal muscle releases IL-6 into the circulation.
- [ ] immune cells are recruited into the circulation from the spleen and bone marrow.
- [x] immune cells have adrenergic receptors that bind adrenaline and noradrenaline.

> The fight-or-flight response stimulates the adrenal glands to release adrenaline and noradrenaline, and immune cells carry adrenergic receptors for them (lymphocytes have many β2-adrenergic receptors). Glucocorticoid receptors respond to cortisol from the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, the other hormonal mediator. See [[Mechanisms of Exercise-Induced Immune Changes#1. Three messengers]].

## In acute exercise, IL-6 released from contracting muscle

- [ ] increases TNF-α production.
- [ ] reduces glucose uptake by muscle.
- [x] stimulates the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and IL-1ra.
- [ ] suppresses the mobilisation of natural killer cells into the circulation.

> Although IL-6 is usually thought of as pro-inflammatory, muscle-derived IL-6 in acute exercise is anti-inflammatory: it stimulates IL-10 and IL-1ra and decreases TNF-α. It also mobilises NK cells (anti-tumour activity), neutrophils and lymphocytes, enhances glucose uptake and fat oxidation, and triggers liver production of acute-phase proteins such as CRP. See [[IL-6 in Exercise#4. Immune effects of exercise-induced IL-6]].
