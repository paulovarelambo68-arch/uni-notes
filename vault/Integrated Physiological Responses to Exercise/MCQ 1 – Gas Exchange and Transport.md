Ten multiple-choice questions on the first half of the second pulmonary lecture: the composition and partial pressures of air, the gradients that drive gas exchange, dissolved oxygen, haemoglobin, the oxygen dissociation curve, the Bohr effect, myoglobin and the carriage of carbon dioxide. Written in the style of the lecturer's example questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## Oxygen makes up what percentage of dry ambient air?

- [ ] 0.03%
- [ ] 78.9%
- [x] 20.9%
- [ ] 15.9%

> Air is 20.9% O2, 78.9% N2, 0.03% CO2 and 0.04% water vapour; 20.9% of 760 mmHg gives the 159 mmHg PO2 of sea-level air. 78.9% is nitrogen and 0.03% is CO2. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#1. What is in the air and how hard it presses]].

## The partial pressure of oxygen in dry air at sea level is

- [x] 159 mmHg
- [ ] 100 mmHg
- [ ] 760 mmHg
- [ ] 600 mmHg

> Each gas in a mixture exerts its own pressure in proportion to its concentration (Dalton's law): 0.209 x 760 = 159 mmHg. 600 mmHg is the nitrogen partial pressure, 100 mmHg is alveolar and arterial PO2, and 760 mmHg is the whole barometric pressure. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#2. Each gas pushes on its own partial pressure]].

## Alveolar gas and pulmonary capillary blood reach equilibrium in about

- [ ] 2.5 s
- [ ] 0.025 s
- [ ] 1 min
- [x] 0.25 s

> Transfer is rapid, about 0.25 s, which is why even the shorter transit time of exercise is usually adequate; thickening of the membrane or loss of alveolar surface area in lung disease lengthens the time and can compromise aeration in exercise. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#4. When the exchange is impaired thick membranes, lost surface and too little time]].

## The PO2 of venous blood returning to the lungs at rest is about

- [ ] 100 mmHg
- [x] 40 mmHg
- [ ] 46 mmHg
- [ ] 159 mmHg

> Venous blood arrives with a PO2 of 40 mmHg and a PCO2 of 46 mmHg; alveolar and arterial PO2 are 100 mmHg and PCO2 40 mmHg, so O2 diffuses into the blood down a 60 mmHg gradient and CO2 into the alveoli down a 6 mmHg one. 46 mmHg is the venous PCO2, not the PO2. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#3. The pressure staircase from air to muscle and back]].

## For each 1 mmHg of PO2, the volume of O2 dissolved in 100 mL of blood is

- [ ] 0.3 mL
- [ ] 3 mL
- [x] 0.003 mL
- [ ] 15 mL

> Oxygen is relatively insoluble in water, so at an arterial PO2 of 100 mmHg only 0.3 mL is dissolved in each 100 mL of blood; with a resting cardiac output of 5 L/min, dissolved O2 alone would deliver just 15 mL/min against a resting need of 250 mL/min. The dissolved fraction nevertheless sets the PO2 of blood and tissue fluid. See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#1. Why Dissolved Oxygen Cannot Supply the Body]].

## Haemoglobin raises the blood's O2-carrying capacity compared with plasma alone by about

- [x] 70 times
- [ ] 7 times
- [ ] 700 times
- [ ] 2 times

> About 98.5% of the O2 in blood is "hidden" in haemoglobin; each 100 mL of a man's blood holds 15–16 g of Hb, and a woman's about 14 g (5–10% less). See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#2. Haemoglobin – Carrying Capacity, Structure and the Route of Oxygen]].

## Venous blood with a PO2 of 40 mmHg is already about

- [ ] 40% saturated
- [x] 75% saturated
- [ ] 50% saturated
- [ ] 98% saturated

> Haemoglobin binds O2 rapidly up to a PO2 of about 50 mmHg and then the curve flattens, so arterial blood at 100 mmHg is 98% saturated and venous blood at 40 mmHg still 75%: at rest the tissues take only about a quarter of the O2 delivered, leaving a large reserve for exercise. See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#4. The Oxygen Dissociation Curve and What Its Shape Does]].

## A rise in acidity and temperature in exercising muscle shifts the oxygen dissociation curve

- [ ] upward and to the left
- [ ] upward only
- [ ] to the left, increasing Hb–O2 affinity
- [x] downward and to the right, releasing more O2 to the tissues

> This is the Bohr effect: H+ and CO2 alter the molecular structure of haemoglobin and lower its O2-binding affinity, so for the same PO2 more O2 is unloaded. It predominates in intense exercise, when metabolic heat, CO2 and lactate acidity all rise. A left shift would mean tighter binding and less unloading. See [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate#1. Muscle tells haemoglobin to let go the Bohr effect]].

## Most of the carbon dioxide in blood is carried as

- [ ] CO2 dissolved in plasma
- [ ] carbaminohaemoglobin
- [x] bicarbonate
- [ ] carbonic acid in the red cell

> About 70% travels as bicarbonate, formed when carbonic anhydrase combines CO2 with water to make carbonic acid, which splits into HCO3- and H+; about 23% is in loose combination with haemoglobin and about 7% is in physical solution, which is the fraction that sets the PCO2. The reaction reverses in the lungs so that CO2 is re-formed and expired. See [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate#4. The return journey carrying carbon dioxide]].

## Myoglobin releases the greatest quantity of its oxygen when tissue PO2 falls below

- [ ] 40 mmHg
- [x] 5 mmHg
- [ ] 50 mmHg
- [ ] 100 mmHg

> Myoglobin has a higher O2 affinity than haemoglobin, so it holds its O2 through rest and moderate exercise and lets it go to the mitochondria only when intramuscular PO2 falls very low, below about 5 mmHg. 40 mmHg is venous PO2 and 50 mmHg is where the haemoglobin curve starts to flatten. See [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate#3. The muscle's own oxygen store myoglobin]].
