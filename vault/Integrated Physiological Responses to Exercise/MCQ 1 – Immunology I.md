Eight multiple-choice questions on Immunology I: the functions of the immune system, its cells and tissues, cytokines, the innate and adaptive branches, pathogen recognition, phagocytosis, complement and natural killer cells. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## Which of the following best describes an antigen?

- [ ] A protein produced by B cells that binds to a pathogen.
- [x] Any molecule or structure that provokes an immune response.
- [ ] A receptor on innate cells that recognises structures shared by groups of microbes.
- [ ] A structure on a microbe that is recognised by Toll-like receptors.

> An antigen is any molecule or structure that provokes an immune response, the name meaning anti-gen, antibody generating. The first option describes an antibody, the third a pathogen recognition receptor and the fourth a PAMP, which is only one kind of antigen. See [[Immune System Overview – Functions and the Two Branches#1. What the immune system is for]].

## Which of the following is a primary lymphoid organ?

- [x] Thymus
- [ ] Spleen
- [ ] Lymph node
- [ ] Tonsil

> The primary lymphoid organs, the bone marrow and the thymus, are where immune cells are formed and mature. The spleen, the lymph nodes and the tonsils (part of the mucosal immune system) are secondary lymphoid organs, where immune cells are concentrated and function. See [[Cells and tissues of the immune system#1. Where immune cells are made and mature the primary lymphoid organs]].

## Which of the following cells is resident in the tissues as a sentinel cell rather than circulating between the blood and the tissues?

- [ ] Neutrophil
- [ ] Eosinophil
- [ ] Monocyte
- [x] Mast cell

> Dendritic cells, macrophages and mast cells are the tissue-resident sentinel cells. Neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, eosinophils and lymphocytes circulate, although they can enter tissues, and a monocyte only becomes a resident macrophage once it settles in a tissue. See [[Cells and tissues of the immune system#4. Cells that circulate and cells that stand guard]].

## Regarding cytokines, which of the following statements is INCORRECT?

- [ ] They are small proteins (5 to 20 kDa) that are secreted by cells.
- [ ] They function in an autocrine and paracrine manner.
- [x] They circulate inactive in the plasma until a pathogen activates them.
- [ ] They bind to specific receptors on the surface of the cells they act on.

> Cytokines are small proteins that cells secrete when needed and that act on the secreting cell (autocrine) or on neighbours (paracrine) through specific receptors. Circulating in an inactive state until a pathogen activates them describes complement, not cytokines. See [[Cytokines#1. What a cytokine is]].

## Which of the following is a feature of the innate immune system?

- [ ] It develops and adapts throughout a lifetime.
- [ ] It recognises antigens with high specificity.
- [ ] It has a memory function.
- [x] It instructs the adaptive immune system.

> Innate immunity is inborn, always present and functional, responds immediately, recognises non-self in a non-specific manner, has no memory and instructs the adaptive immune system, for example when a dendritic cell presents antigen to T cells. The other three are features of adaptive immunity. See [[Immune System Overview – Functions and the Two Branches#3. Features of innate (natural) immunity]].

## Lipopolysaccharide, which is present on all Gram-negative bacteria, is an example of a

- [ ] pathogen recognition receptor.
- [x] pathogen-associated molecular pattern.
- [ ] Toll-like receptor expressed by innate cells.
- [ ] complement opsonin such as C3b.

> PAMPs are structures on microbes that are shared by groups of microbes, and lipopolysaccharide on all Gram-negative bacteria is the lecture’s example. They are what the pathogen recognition receptors of innate cells, such as the Toll-like receptors, bind, which is why innate recognition is non-specific. An opsonin is a molecule such as C3b that coats a microbe for phagocytosis. See [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis#1. How innate cells know an enemy]].

## During phagocytosis, the phagosome fuses with a __________ to form a phagolysosome.

- [ ] residual body
- [ ] pseudopod
- [x] lysosome
- [ ] phagocytic vesicle

> The phagosome, also called the phagocytic vesicle, forms when pseudopods close around the ingested microbe, and it then fuses with a lysosome, a vesicle of digestive enzymes, to form the phagolysosome in which the microbe is digested. The residual body holds the indigestible material left afterwards. See [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis#3. The seven phases]].

## Which of the following complement proteins binds to the surface of a pathogen and makes it more amenable to phagocytosis?

- [x] C3b
- [ ] C3a
- [ ] C5a
- [ ] C9

> This is opsonisation, which may be the most important function of complement, carried out by C3b and C4b, the large fragments that stick to the pathogen’s surface. C3a and C5a are the small fragments that mediate inflammation, and C9 forms the ring of the membrane attack complex. See [[Complement and Natural Killer Cells#3. The three functions]].
