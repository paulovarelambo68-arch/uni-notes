Ten multiple-choice questions on the first pulmonary lecture: the lungs and alveoli, the conducting and respiratory zones, the mechanics of inspiration and expiration, surfactant, the lung volumes and capacities, FEV1 and FVC, minute and alveolar ventilation, the ventilation–perfusion ratio and shunt. Written in the style of the lecturer's example questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## How many alveoli do the lungs contain?

- [ ] About 600 thousand
- [ ] About 60 million
- [x] About 600 million
- [ ] About 6 billion

> The lungs hold about 600 million alveoli, which between them provide a gas-exchange surface of 70–100 m2, and each alveolus lies only 0.2–2.5 µm from its capillary. The wrong options are powers-of-ten slips. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#2. The alveoli]].

## The anatomic dead space is approximately

- [x] 150 mL
- [ ] 350 mL
- [ ] 500 mL
- [ ] 1.2 L

> About 150 mL (150–200 mL) of each breath stays in the conducting airways, the trachea, bronchi and bronchioles, and never meets the blood. 350 mL is the part of a 500 mL tidal breath that does reach the alveoli, and 1.2 L is the expiratory reserve volume. See [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt#4. Dead space and alveolar ventilation]].

## During inspiration the diaphragm

- [ ] relaxes and rises
- [x] contracts, flattens and moves downward
- [ ] contracts and rises
- [ ] relaxes and flattens

> Contraction flattens the dome of the diaphragm and pushes it towards the abdomen, enlarging the chest cavity; the air in the lungs expands and its pressure falls slightly below atmospheric (Boyle's law), so air flows in until the two pressures are equal. The diaphragm rises again only when it relaxes during expiration. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#5. Breathing in]].

## Surfactant is produced by

- [ ] type I alveolar epithelial cells
- [ ] goblet cells
- [ ] alveolar macrophages
- [x] type II alveolar epithelial cells

> Type II cells secrete the lipoprotein "wetting agent" (proteins, phospholipids and Ca2+) that interrupts the water layer lining the alveoli, lowers its surface tension and so raises lung compliance. Type I cells form the thin exchange wall, goblet cells make mucus and macrophages are immune cells. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#7. Surfactant and compliance]].

## Forced vital capacity (FVC) =

- [x] VT + IRV + ERV
- [ ] VT + IRV + RLV
- [ ] TLC – IRV
- [ ] VT x breathing rate

> FVC is all the air that can be moved voluntarily in one maximal breath: the tidal volume (about 500 mL) plus the inspiratory reserve (about 3.0 L) plus the expiratory reserve (about 1.2 L). The residual volume cannot be exhaled, so it belongs to total lung capacity (TLC = FVC + RLV), not to FVC, and VT x breathing rate is minute ventilation. See [[Lung Volumes and Capacities – Spirometry, FEV1 and FVC#1. Measuring a breath the spirogram]].

## In healthy individuals FEV1/FVC is approximately

- [ ] 42%
- [ ] 70%
- [x] 85%
- [ ] 100%

> A healthy person can blow out about 85% of the forced vital capacity in the first second (FEV1). A ratio of 70% or less signals airway obstruction, as in COPD, asthma, bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis, and 42% is the bronchial asthma example (FEV 1.3 L of an FVC of 3.1 L). See [[Lung Volumes and Capacities – Spirometry, FEV1 and FVC#3. How fast, not just how much dynamic volumes and FEV1]].

## Minute ventilation at rest is approximately

- [ ] 0.5 L/min
- [x] 6 L/min
- [ ] 60 L/min
- [ ] 100 L/min

> About 12 breaths/min x 0.5 L = 6 L/min. 0.5 L is a single tidal breath, and about 100 L/min is what a healthy adult reaches in strenuous exercise (35–45 breaths/min x 2.0 L). See [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt#2. Minute ventilation and its numbers]].

## The average ventilation–perfusion ratio at rest is

- [x] 0.84
- [ ] 1.2
- [ ] 4.2
- [ ] 5.0

> About 4.2 L of air ventilates the alveoli each minute while about 5.0 L of blood flows through the pulmonary capillaries, and 4.2/5.0 = 0.84, so 0.84 L of alveolar ventilation matches each litre of pulmonary blood flow. 4.2 and 5.0 are the two halves of the ratio, not the ratio. See [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt#5. Matching air to blood the ventilation–perfusion ratio]].

## During intense exercise the ventilation–perfusion ratio

- [ ] falls to about 0.25
- [ ] stays at about 0.84
- [ ] falls below 0.5
- [x] rises above 5.0

> Ventilation increases far more than pulmonary blood flow, so the ratio rises above 5.0 in intense exercise, while in light exercise it stays near 0.8. Alveolar ventilation outpaces perfusion, which is one reason the respiratory system is rarely the limiting factor in athletic performance. See [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt#5. Matching air to blood the ventilation–perfusion ratio]].

## Expiration at rest is

- [ ] an active process that uses the expiratory muscles
- [ ] driven by contraction of the diaphragm
- [x] a passive process driven by elastic recoil of the lungs
- [ ] completed when intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric

> At rest and in light exercise the inspiratory muscles simply relax and the stretched lung tissue recoils: the sternum and ribs drop, the diaphragm rises and the alveolar gas is compressed and flows out. Expiration becomes active only in vigorous exercise, and it ends when intrapulmonary pressure returns to atmospheric, not below it. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#6. Breathing out]].
