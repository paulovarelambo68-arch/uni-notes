Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from all five pulmonary lectures, two per lecture, revisiting the most examinable facts with new stems. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## Surfactant production is a function of the

- [ ] conducting zone (trachea, bronchi and bronchioles)
- [ ] nasal mucosa
- [ ] pleura
- [x] transitional and respiratory zones

> The respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveoli exchange gas, produce surfactant, activate and inactivate molecules, regulate blood clotting and have endocrine and innate immune functions; the conducting zone only transports, warms, humidifies and filters the air. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#3. The two zones of the airways]].

## Total lung capacity (TLC) =

- [x] FVC + RLV
- [ ] VT + IRV + ERV
- [ ] IRV + ERV
- [ ] FVC – RLV

> Everything the lungs can hold is the forced vital capacity plus the residual volume that cannot be exhaled: about 4.8 L + 1.2 L = 6.0 L in men. VT + IRV + ERV is the FVC itself. See [[Lung Volumes and Capacities – Spirometry, FEV1 and FVC#2. The air that never leaves residual volume]].

## The PO2 of air in the trachea, after it has been humidified, is about

- [ ] 159 mmHg
- [x] 149 mmHg
- [ ] 100 mmHg
- [ ] 40 mmHg

> Water vapour takes its share of the pressure, so inspired air at 159 mmHg becomes 149 mmHg in the trachea, 100 mmHg in the alveoli and arterial blood and 40 mmHg in venous blood. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#3. The pressure staircase from air to muscle and back]].

## Binding of the first O2 molecule to deoxyhaemoglobin

- [ ] lowers the affinity for the next
- [ ] has no effect, because each of the four haems binds O2 independently
- [x] raises the affinity for the next
- [ ] drives off 2,3-DPG permanently

> The second, third and fourth O2 then bind more easily. This cooperativity is an allosteric change in the molecule's shape; it works in reverse too, so once oxyhaemoglobin releases its first O2 the next come off more easily, and as O2 binds it drives off CO2 (the Haldane effect). See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#3. Cooperativity – Four Seats That Help Each Other]].

## The increased activity of the medullary respiratory centre reaches it from the peripheral chemoreceptors via the

- [ ] phrenic and intercostal nerves
- [ ] sympathetic chain
- [ ] optic nerve
- [x] glossopharyngeal and vagal nerves

> The carotid and aortic bodies signal through the glossopharyngeal and vagal nerves; the resulting rise in ventilation lowers PaCO2 again. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## Phase II of exercise ventilation is

- [ ] the abrupt rise at the start of exercise
- [x] the gradual rise to a steady level
- [ ] the fine tuning by temperature, CO2 and H+
- [ ] the return to rest in recovery

> Phase II is driven by central command, the medullary neurons, chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors. Phase I is the abrupt neurogenic rise and phase III the fine tuning; the combined and simultaneous action of chemical and neural stimuli means no single factor controls exercise breathing. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#7. Putting it together the three phases]].

## During a Valsalva manoeuvre the intrathoracic pressure can reach

- [ ] 3 mmHg above atmospheric
- [ ] 40 mmHg above atmospheric
- [x] 150 mmHg above atmospheric
- [ ] 760 mmHg above atmospheric

> Quiet breathing swings intrapulmonary pressure by only about 3 mmHg; closing the glottis while the expiratory muscles contract raises intrathoracic pressure up to 150 mmHg, with even higher pressures in the abdomen. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#8. Straining against a closed throat the Valsalva manoeuvre]].

## Chronic altitude exposure shifts the oxygen dissociation curve

- [x] slightly to the right, for more unloading
- [ ] to the left, for more loading
- [ ] not at all
- [ ] upward

> The acute left shift of hyperventilation gives way, as red cell 2,3-DPG rises, to a slight right shift that unloads more O2 to the tissues. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#4. The first weeks acclimatisation]].

## Natural killer cells destroy infected and tumour cells by releasing

- [ ] histamine
- [x] perforin and granzymes
- [ ] lysozyme and lactoferrin
- [ ] mucins

> NK cells, about 15% of circulating lymphocytes and living 7–10 days, release perforin and granzymes that induce apoptosis in virus-infected and tumour cells; histamine is released by basophils and mast cells, and lysozyme and lactoferrin are antimicrobial proteins of airway mucus. See [[Lung Immune Cells – Toll-like Receptors, Macrophages and Neutrophils]].

## The incidence of ARDS in Europe is about

- [ ] 3–6 per 1,000 person-years
- [ ] 300 per 100,000 person-years
- [x] 3–6 per 100,000 person-years
- [ ] 1 per million person-years

> 3–6 per 100,000 person-years in Europe and 1.5–5.3 globally; the incidence appears to be rising with better diagnosis while mortality slowly falls thanks to therapies aimed at the cause, earlier supportive care and gentler artificial ventilation. See [[Lung Disease and Exercise – ARDS, Asthma and COVID#1. When the alveoli flood ARDS]].
