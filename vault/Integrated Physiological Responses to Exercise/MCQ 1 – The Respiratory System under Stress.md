Ten multiple-choice questions on the third and fourth pulmonary lectures: airway resistance, the breathing reserve, the oxygen cost of breathing, the normal pH, the kidney as the last line of defence, the altitude bands, the pressure on Everest, the acute effect of smoking, cold and the Valsalva manoeuvre. Written in the style of the lecturer's example questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## Halving the diameter of an airway increases its resistance to airflow

- [ ] 2-fold
- [x] 16-fold
- [ ] 4-fold
- [ ] 8-fold

> Resistance rises with the fourth power of the narrowing, so half the diameter gives 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 = 16 times the resistance, which is why the narrowed airways of asthma and COPD raise the work of breathing so much. The other factor that sets the energy cost of breathing is the compliance of the lungs and thorax. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#1. What breathing costs – compliance, airway resistance and the oxygen cost of breathing]].

## At VO2max, minute ventilation in a healthy person is what fraction of the maximum voluntary ventilation (MVV)?

- [ ] 100%
- [ ] 20–30%
- [ ] 40–50%
- [x] 60–85%

> Because VE at VO2max reaches only 60–85% of MVV, a breathing reserve remains even at maximal effort, which is one reason ventilation is not usually what limits aerobic power in healthy people. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#3. The breathing reserve – why ventilation does not limit healthy people]].

## In healthy individuals the oxygen cost of breathing at VO2max is about

- [x] 8–11% of total VO2
- [ ] 3–5% of total VO2
- [ ] 15% of total VO2
- [ ] 40% of total VO2

> Breathing costs 1.5–2.0 mL of O2 per litre of air at low ventilation, 3–5% of VO2 in moderate exercise and 8–11% at VO2max; it reaches 15% or more in elite endurance athletes ventilating 150 L/min or more, and 40% is the figure for pulmonary disease. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#1. What breathing costs – compliance, airway resistance and the oxygen cost of breathing]].

## The normal pH of the body fluids is

- [ ] 7.0–7.1
- [ ] 7.2–7.3
- [x] 7.35–7.45
- [ ] 7.6–7.8

> Buffering holds pH in this slightly basic range; a rise in H+ is acidosis and a fall is alkalosis, and a plasma pH of 7.0 or below in maximal exercise brings nausea, headache and dizziness. See [[Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney#1. What pH is and why exercise threatens it]].

## The body's final defence against a change in H+ concentration is

- [ ] the chemical buffers
- [x] the renal tubules
- [ ] pulmonary ventilation
- [ ] haemoglobin

> The chemical buffers (bicarbonate, phosphate, protein) are the rapid first line, ventilation acts within minutes by changing CO2, and the kidneys act last and slowest, secreting H+ and ammonia into the urine and reabsorbing bicarbonate. See [[Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney#5. The kidney, the slow final defence]].

## Moderate altitude is defined as

- [ ] above 5,500 m
- [ ] 3,000–5,500 m
- [ ] 500–1,500 m
- [x] 1,500–3,000 m

> Moderate altitude is 1,500–3,000 m, high altitude 3,000–5,500 m and extreme altitude above 5,500 m; 5,820 m is the upper limit of permanent human habitation. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#1. Less oxygen in every breath]].

## Atmospheric pressure on the summit of Everest (8,850 m) is about

- [x] 235 mmHg
- [ ] 450 mmHg
- [ ] 760 mmHg
- [ ] 159 mmHg

> 235 mmHg on Everest against 450 mmHg at the Pikes Peak research centre (4,300 m) and 760 mmHg at sea level; 159 mmHg is the sea-level PO2. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#1. Less oxygen in every breath]].

## Smoking 15 puffs over 5 minutes

- [ ] has no effect on the airways
- [ ] halves airway resistance
- [x] triples airway resistance for about 35 minutes
- [ ] doubles airway resistance permanently

> The 3-fold rise occurs in both non-smokers and chronic smokers and is caused by a vagal (parasympathetic) reflex through nicotinic receptors, possibly with irritation by smoke particles. It raises the O2 cost of breathing at high intensity from about 9% to 15%, and the effects reverse completely within 24 hours. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#6. Smoking – acute effects of one cigarette and chronic damage]].

## Cold lowers aerobic endurance partly because

- [ ] resting VO2 falls
- [x] maximal heart rate and VO2max fall
- [ ] ventilation falls
- [ ] airway resistance halves

> Cold raises the metabolic cost of exercise, lowers cardiovascular endurance (maximum heart rate, cardiac output and VO2max) and delivers less O2 to muscle, because cold haemoglobin holds O2 more tightly and active-muscle blood flow falls. Resting VO2 actually rises in the cold. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#7. Cold air – oxygen uptake, ventilation, airway water loss and endurance]].

## The Valsalva manoeuvre is

- [ ] a forced inhalation against a closed glottis
- [ ] rapid shallow breathing
- [ ] a breath-hold after a maximal inhalation
- [x] a forced exhalation against a closed glottis

> Activating the expiratory muscles against a closed glottis raises intrathoracic pressure up to 150 mmHg above atmospheric (against swings of about 3 mmHg in quiet breathing) and even more in the abdomen, which stabilises the chest and abdomen for heavy lifting but can cause herniation. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#8. Straining against a closed throat the Valsalva manoeuvre]].
