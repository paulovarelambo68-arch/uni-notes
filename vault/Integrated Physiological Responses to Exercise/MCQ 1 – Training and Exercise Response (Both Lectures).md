Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## According to the lecture, which of the following is one of the four primary principles of exercise training?

- [ ] Progression
- [ ] Periodisation
- [ ] Variation
- [x] Reversibility

> The four primary principles are overload, specificity, individual differences and reversibility. Progression is one of the seven factors that affect the aerobic training response (with initial fitness, intensity, duration, volume, frequency and mode), not one of the four principles, and periodisation and variation are not named in the lecture. See Training & Detraining, slide 3 (Principles of exercise training) and slide 19 (Seven Factors Affecting Aerobic Training Responses).

## Compared with the effort needed to improve aerobic capacity, achieving the health-related benefits of regular exercise requires:

- [x] A lower intensity but a greater volume
- [ ] A higher intensity and a lower volume
- [ ] A lower intensity and a lower volume
- [ ] The same intensity at a greater frequency

> Health benefits accrue at a lower effort intensity than the one needed to raise aerobic capacity, but they require a greater volume of activity. The other options either raise the intensity or lower the volume, the opposite of the slide's point. See Training & Detraining, slide 4 (Overload Principle).

## According to the classification of physical activity by duration of all-out effort, a 200–400 m dash or a 100 m swim relies predominantly on:

- [ ] ATP alone (strength–power)
- [ ] ATP + PCr (sustained power)
- [x] ATP + PCr + lactic acid (anaerobic power–endurance)
- [ ] Electron transport–oxidative phosphorylation (aerobic endurance)

> Efforts of roughly 10 s to 1.5 min are the anaerobic power–endurance band, fuelled by the phosphagens plus rapid glycolysis to lactate. ATP alone covers the first few seconds (a power lift, a javelin throw), ATP + PCr covers sprints and fast breaks of up to about 10 s, and the aerobic–oxidative system dominates beyond about 3 min (beyond an 800 m run). See Training & Detraining, slide 5 (Specificity Principle: classification of physical activity based on duration of all-out effort).

## Within 5 to 10 days of starting aerobic training, the activity of the aerobic system enzymes:

- [ ] Increases by about 10%
- [x] Approximately doubles
- [ ] Increases about fourfold
- [ ] Is unchanged until the mitochondria enlarge

> A twofold increase in aerobic enzymes within 5 to 10 training days coincides with the increased mitochondrial capacity to generate ATP aerobically; it is one of the earliest adaptations, in keeping with the improvement in aerobic fitness that appears within the first training week. See Training & Detraining, slide 12 (Metabolic Adaptations) and slide 20 (Initial Aerobic Fitness Level).

## After endurance training, maximal cardiac output is higher because:

- [ ] Maximal heart rate increases
- [ ] Both stroke volume and maximal heart rate increase
- [x] Stroke volume increases while maximal heart rate is maintained
- [ ] Maximal heart rate increases while stroke volume is maintained

> Training does not raise HRmax; the larger maximal cardiac output comes entirely from the larger stroke volume (eccentric hypertrophy, greater contractility, larger blood volume) at an unchanged HRmax, and this is what raises VO₂max. See Training & Detraining, slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training: ↑ cardiac output at max).

## According to the lecture, aerobic capacity improves if exercise intensity keeps the heart rate between what percentage of maximum?

- [ ] 40 and 55%
- [x] 55 and 70%
- [ ] 70 and 85%
- [ ] 85 and 95%

> Heart rates of 55–70% of maximum are enough to improve aerobic capacity; for healthy persons 70% HRmax is "moderate activity" without discomfort, sufficient for a training effect without lactate accumulation (the figure's training-sensitive zone runs from the 60–70% lines up to the 90% line). 40–55% is below the zone, and the higher bands correspond to the "hard" and "very hard" RPE ranges. See Training & Detraining, slide 22 (Training Intensity: Train at HRmax Percentage).

## The respiratory exchange ratio (RER) is calculated as:

- [x] VCO₂ / VO₂
- [ ] VO₂ / VCO₂
- [ ] VE / VO₂
- [ ] VE / VCO₂

> RER is the ratio of CO₂ released to O₂ consumed, VCO₂/VO₂, which ranges from 0.7 (fat) to 1.0 (carbohydrate) for macronutrient metabolism. VE/VO₂ and VE/VCO₂ are the ventilatory equivalents, measures of ventilatory efficiency. See Integrated Response, slide 12 (Respiratory Exchange Ratio (RER)).

## From rest into moderate exercise, the increase in ventilation is achieved mainly by:

- [ ] An increase in breathing frequency
- [ ] Equal increases in tidal volume and breathing frequency
- [ ] A reduction in dead space
- [x] An increase in tidal volume

> During light and moderate activity ventilation rises linearly with VO₂, and this is done chiefly by deeper breaths: tidal volume increases substantially from rest into moderate exercise. Breathing frequency takes over only at higher intensities, when tidal volume plateaus. See Integrated Response, slide 6 (Ventilation must match the metabolic challenge) and slide 7 (Ventilation Vt vs BF shift to increase ventilation).

## The ventilatory threshold (VT) is defined as the point at which:

- [ ] Ventilation first begins to rise above resting values
- [ ] VO₂ plateaus despite an increase in work rate
- [x] Ventilation increases disproportionately with VO₂ during graded activity
- [ ] Blood lactate reaches 4 mM

> VT is the break from the linear VE–VO₂ relationship, caused by the extra CO₂ released when bicarbonate buffers the lactate that begins to accumulate. A VO₂ plateau is a criterion for VO₂max, and 4 mM lactate marks OBLA, which lies beyond the lactate threshold. See Integrated Response, slide 15 (Ventilation During Non-Steady-Rate PA: Ventilatory Threshold (VT)) and slide 14 (Pulmonary Ventilation During PA).

## Which of the following is one of the four criteria used to accept the highest VO₂ measured during a test as VO₂max?

- [ ] Heart rate at least 20 beats/min below the age-predicted HRmax
- [ ] RER greater than 0.90 in the final stage
- [ ] RPE greater than 12
- [x] VO₂ rises by less than 150 mL/min despite an increase in work rate

> The four criteria are a VO₂ plateau (rise of less than 150 mL/min with a further increase in work rate), a final-stage heart rate no more than 10 beats/min below the age-predicted HRmax, RER greater than 1.10 and RPE greater than 17 on the original Borg scale; two of the four must be met. The distractors carry the wrong numbers. See Integrated Response, slide 29 (criteria for VO₂max).
