Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## By about 5 minutes of continuous exercise, the largest percentage of the energy is supplied by:

- [ ] The phosphagen system
- [ ] The glycolytic–lactate system
- [ ] The phosphagen and glycolytic systems equally
- [x] Mitochondrial respiration

> The phosphagen contribution collapses within the first minute, the glycolytic–lactate contribution peaks in the next minute or two and then declines, and mitochondrial respiration climbs to supply most of the energy by 5 min. See Integrated Response, slide 3 (The energy systems and exercise).

## The statement "same workload ≠ same physiological demand" means that:

- [x] Two people performing the same external workload may be in different physiological domains
- [ ] External workload determines the physiological demand for everyone
- [ ] Physiological demand is independent of workload
- [ ] Workload cannot be measured during CPET

> Disease or deconditioning raises the metabolic and ventilatory cost of a given external workload, so two subjects at the same power output can sit on opposite sides of their lactate or ventilatory thresholds. See Integrated Response, slide 17 (Why the axis matters?) and slide 18 (Examples).

## Persons A and B have the same VO₂ (2.0 L/min), but B has a VCO₂ of 2.1 L/min (RER 1.05) and a VE of 85 L/min against A's 1.8 L/min (RER 0.90) and 60 L/min. B's values most likely indicate:

- [ ] A greater reliance on fat oxidation
- [ ] A lower metabolic demand than A
- [x] Exercise above the lactate/ventilatory threshold
- [ ] A measurement error, since RER cannot exceed 1.0

> An RER above 1.0 with a disproportionately high VE is the signature of non-metabolic CO₂ from buffering, so B is in a higher physiological domain at the same external workload; fat oxidation would lower the RER, and measured RER can exceed 1.0 (only metabolic RER cannot). Further CPET variables to inspect would include blood lactate, VE/VCO₂ and heart rate. See Integrated Response, slide 18 (Examples) and slide 13 (The crucial link: H⁺ buffering creates additional CO₂).

## Which of the following best describes the measurement of VO₂max as used clinically?

- [ ] Invasive but highly accurate
- [ ] Subjective, based on perceived exertion
- [x] Non-invasive, objective, accurate and quantitative
- [ ] Qualitative, used only in athletes

> VO₂max testing evaluates exercise capacity and fitness, identifies and evaluates heart and lung status, finds the cause of unexplained dyspnoea, follows interstitial lung disease, detects fibrosis from chemotherapy or radiation, grades impairment and disability, and guides reconditioning and rehabilitation programmes; in sport it tracks the aerobic capacity of all athletes, not only endurance athletes. See Integrated Response, slide 23 (VO₂max – clinically) and slide 24 (VO₂max – in Sport).

## Reduced cardiac output and altered peripheral extraction coexisting with abnormal ventilatory efficiency describe the CPET picture of:

- [ ] COPD
- [ ] Pulmonary vascular disease
- [ ] Deconditioning
- [x] Heart failure

> Heart failure affects the delivery and extraction terms of the Fick equation and often shows poor ventilatory efficiency (a high VE/VCO₂); COPD is ventilatory limitation, pulmonary vascular disease perfusion–ventilation mismatch, and deconditioning a higher cost for the same workload. See Integrated Response, slide 17 (Why the axis matters?).

## Which of the following best describes the interaction between training duration and intensity?

- [x] Extending the duration can compensate for a reduced intensity
- [ ] A higher intensity cannot compensate for a shorter duration
- [ ] Duration and intensity act independently of each other
- [ ] Only intensity affects the training response

> Duration and intensity interact: a longer duration offsets the benefit lost at a lower intensity, which is why health benefits can be gained at a lower intensity with a greater volume, and why more frequent training is especially beneficial at lower intensity. See Training & Detraining, slide 27 (Training: Duration & Volume) and slide 26 (Is Strenuous Training More Effective?).

## About 150 min per week of moderate-intensity physical activity approximates a weekly energy expenditure of:

- [ ] 500 kcal
- [x] 1000 kcal
- [ ] 2000 kcal
- [ ] 3000 kcal

> Roughly 150 min per week of moderate activity is about 1000 kcal, and a weekly volume of 500–1000 METs is the range associated with lower cardiovascular disease and premature mortality. See Training & Detraining, slide 27 (Training: Duration & Volume).

## Compared with less fit persons, more fit males and females generally require:

- [ ] A lower threshold intensity to stimulate a training response
- [x] A higher threshold intensity to stimulate a training response
- [ ] The same threshold intensity
- [ ] No threshold, since they are already adapted

> The fitter the person, the higher the intensity needed to provoke further adaptation (and the smaller the improvement that remains available), while the less fit respond to lower intensities. See Training & Detraining, slide 26 (Is Strenuous Training More Effective?) and slide 20 (Initial Aerobic Fitness Level).

## In the summary figure, after training stops, which adaptation is lost most slowly?

- [ ] Aerobic enzymes
- [ ] Glycogen
- [ ] Slow-twitch fibre cross-sectional size
- [x] Capillaries

> Aerobic enzyme activity, the fastest to rise, falls steeply within weeks of stopping; capillary density declines most gradually, with VO₂max falling at an intermediate rate. Use it or lose it. See Training & Detraining, slide 34 (Summary of Training Adaptations) and slide 33 (Use it or lose it!).

## The "talk test" is described in the lecture as:

- [x] A simple check of whether comfortable speech is possible
- [ ] A laboratory method for determining the ventilatory threshold
- [ ] A method for determining HRmax
- [ ] A test of respiratory muscle strength

> If comfortable speech is still possible, the intensity falls within the accepted prescription guidelines for the treadmill and cycle ergometer; like RPE it is a psychophysiological rather than a laboratory measure. See Training & Detraining, slide 24 (Training Intensity: Rating of Perceived Exertion).
