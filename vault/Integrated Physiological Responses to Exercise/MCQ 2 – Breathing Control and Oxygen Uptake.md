Ten more multiple-choice questions on the control of breathing and oxygen uptake: the pneumotaxic and apneustic centres, the ventral respiratory group, what stimulates the chemoreceptors, the carotid bodies, the alveolar gases during exercise, tidal volume versus rate, phase III, the lactate threshold after training and the effect of training on ventilation. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## The pneumotaxic centre of the pons

- [x] coordinates the switch from inspiration to expiration and prevents over-expansion of the lungs
- [ ] prolongs inspiration and deepens the breath
- [ ] sets the basic rhythm of breathing at rest
- [ ] senses arterial PCO2

> It modifies the expiratory neurons of the VRG, directly promotes expiration and indirectly inhibits the apneustic centre's promotion of inspiration. Prolonging inspiration is the apneustic centre, the rhythm is the DRG and PCO2 is sensed by the chemoreceptors. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#3. The fine-tuners in the pons]].

## The apneustic centre

- [ ] terminates inspiration
- [ ] controls the abdominal muscles
- [ ] is located in the medulla
- [x] activates and prolongs inspiration, deepening the breath

> It acts on the DRG inspiratory centre, prolonging the excitatory "ramp" signals and the activity of the diaphragm. Terminating inspiration is the job of the pneumotaxic centre, and both lie in the pons, not the medulla. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#3. The fine-tuners in the pons]].

## The ventral respiratory group of the medulla

- [ ] sets the respiratory rhythm at rest
- [x] contains inspiratory and expiratory neurons, is active in forceful breathing and drives the abdominal and accessory muscles
- [ ] lies in the pons
- [ ] responds to arterial PO2

> The VRG is associated mainly with expiration and is recruited as breathing becomes more forceful in exercise, when exhalation becomes active and costs energy. The resting rhythm belongs to the DRG. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#2. The rhythm generators in the medulla]].

## Chemoreceptors stimulate ventilation in response to

- [ ] a fall in CO2
- [ ] a rise in PO2
- [x] a rise in CO2, acidity and temperature and a fall in O2 and blood pressure
- [ ] a rise in blood pressure

> The chemical state of the blood ("humoral" factors, carried in the blood) largely regulates ventilation at rest, and during exercise the peripheral chemoreceptors also respond to rising potassium. The other options reverse the direction of the stimuli. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## The carotid bodies monitor arterial blood

- [x] just before it perfuses the brain
- [ ] as it leaves the lungs
- [ ] in the right atrium
- [ ] in the medulla

> They sit where the carotid arteries head for the brain, giving early warning of low PO2, high CO2 or acidity; the central chemoreceptors, in the medulla, sense the chemical state of the fluid around the brainstem. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## During exercise of increasing intensity, alveolar PO2 and PCO2

- [ ] both fall
- [ ] PO2 falls while PCO2 rises
- [ ] both rise sharply
- [x] remain near resting levels

> Ventilation rises with metabolism, so alveolar gas pressures stay near rest despite a large rise in mixed-venous PCO2 (from about 45 to over 70 mmHg); in intense exercise the large ventilatory volumes can even push alveolar PO2 above 100 mmHg. This is why a fall in alveolar PO2 is not what drives exercise breathing. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#5. Why chemistry alone cannot explain exercise breathing]].

## At moderate steady-rate workloads the initial rise in ventilation is mainly due to

- [ ] a rise in breathing rate
- [x] a rise in tidal volume
- [ ] a fall in dead space
- [ ] a rise in arterial PCO2

> At moderate workloads ventilation rises linearly with O2 consumption and CO2 production, mostly through deeper breaths; at higher workloads the further rise comes mainly from rate. PaO2 and PaCO2 stay near baseline and the transit time of blood across the alveoli remains adequate. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#8. Ventilation against work the ventilatory threshold]].

## Phase III of exercise ventilation is

- [ ] the abrupt rise at the start of exercise
- [ ] the gradual rise to a steady level
- [x] the fine tuning of ventilation by peripheral sensory feedback such as temperature, CO2 and H+
- [ ] the fall in ventilation during recovery

> Phase I is the abrupt neurogenic rise, phase II the gradual rise from central command with the medullary neurons, chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors, and phase III the fine tuning. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#7. Putting it together the three phases]].

## With aerobic training, the point at which OBLA occurs

- [ ] moves to a lower percentage of VO2max
- [ ] does not change
- [ ] rises only if VO2max also rises
- [x] rises, often without an accompanying increase in VO2max

> Training lets the athlete work at a higher fraction of VO2max before lactate accumulates (80% or more in highly trained endurance athletes against 55–65% in the untrained), which is why the lactate threshold, not VO2max alone, shows how much of the aerobic potential is used. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#4. How much of the ceiling you can use the lactate threshold]].

## After endurance training, ventilation at the same work rate is

- [x] lower, because lower blood lactate gives less feedback to stimulate breathing
- [ ] higher, because tidal volume is larger
- [ ] unchanged
- [ ] lower, because dead space is smaller

> This is a chronic effect of exercise: the same work produces less lactic acid, so there is less chemical drive to the respiratory centre and the ventilation-work curve shifts down. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#9. Training and heat]].
