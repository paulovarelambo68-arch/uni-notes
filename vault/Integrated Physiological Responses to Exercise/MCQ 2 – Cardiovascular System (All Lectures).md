## Mark the correct option (haemodynamic relationship):

- [ ] SV = MAP × HR × TPR
- [ ] MAP = CO / TPR
- [ ] CO = MAP × TPR
- [ ] HR = (MAP × TPR) / SV
- [x] TPR = MAP / (SV × HR)

> MAP = CO x TPR, the hydrostatic form of Ohm's law (V = i x R), and CO = SV x HR, so TPR = MAP / CO = MAP / (SV x HR). The others put a term on the wrong side: MAP = CO x TPR, CO = MAP / TPR, SV = MAP / (HR x TPR) and HR = MAP / (TPR x SV). See [[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation#4. The three features and the one equation]].

## In relation to blood flow velocity in the systemic circulation, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Volume flow (L/min) is highest in the aorta and lowest in the capillaries
- [x] Velocity is inversely proportional to total cross-sectional area
- [ ] Blood flow velocity in the vena cava is lower than in the capillaries
- [ ] The total cross-sectional area is greatest at the level of the arterioles

> Volume flow is the same in every section (the whole output crosses each level per minute), and v = Q / A, so velocity falls from about 20 cm/s in the arteries to 0.05 cm/s in the capillaries, whose combined area is about 1000 cm², and rises again to about 15 cm/s in the vena cava. See [[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation#5. Vessel numbers, cross-sectional area and velocity]].

## In relation to the cardiac cycle and heart rate, which statement is correct:

- [ ] At a heart rate of 60 beats/min the cardiac cycle lasts 0.5 s
- [ ] At maximum heart rate (~180 beats/min) systole is reduced to about 0.13 s
- [x] At rest (60 beats/min) diastole lasts about 0.66 s and systole about 0.34 s
- [ ] As heart rate rises, systole shortens more than diastole

> Cycle length = 60 / HR, so 60/min gives 1.0 s, two-thirds of it diastole; at about 180/min the cycle falls to 0.33 s, with diastole 0.13 s and systole 0.2 s, so diastole, the filling time, gives up most of the time (0.53 s against 0.14 s). See [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#2. What a cardiac cycle is]].

## In relation to the heart sounds, which statement is correct:

- [ ] The second heart sound (S2) is caused by the opening of the aortic and pulmonary valves
- [ ] The third heart sound (S3) is caused by atrial contraction
- [x] The first heart sound (S1) is caused by closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves
- [ ] Murmurs are caused by laminar flow through a narrowed valve

> S1 is the low, long 'lub' of AV-valve closure (M1 before T1) at the start of isovolumetric contraction; S2 is the short, high 'dub' of semilunar-valve closure (A2 before P2). S3 comes from passive early-diastolic filling and S4 from the rapid filling after atrial contraction, both detected by phonocardiography; murmurs are turbulent flow. See [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#7. Heart sounds and the ECG on the same timeline]] and [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#8. Murmurs]].

## In relation to myocardial contractility, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Contractility is the stroke volume for a given heart rate
- [x] dP/dt max in isovolumetric contraction is a good measure of contractility
- [ ] Every point on a single Starling curve represents a different contractility
- [ ] An increase in venous return is an example of a positive inotropic effect

> Contractility is the SV for a given EDV, the force developed at a given length, so every point on one Starling curve has the same contractility and only a rise to a higher curve (as with sympathetic stimulation) is a rise in contractility. During isovolumetric contraction fibre length is fixed and no blood is yet being ejected, so the steepness of the pressure rise (dP/dt max, the maximum rate of rise of ventricular pressure) reflects the muscle alone. See [[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling’s Law and Contractility#4. Contractility what it actually means]] and [[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling’s Law and Contractility#5. Measuring contractility]].

## In relation to cardiac muscle relaxation, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Most of the cytoplasmic Ca2+ is extruded from the cell by the membrane Ca-ATPase
- [ ] The Na+–Ca2+ exchanger is an ATP-dependent pump
- [ ] Phospholamban stimulates Ca2+ uptake into the SR while in its dephosphorylated state
- [x] In humans about 70% of the cytoplasmic Ca2+ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by SERCA

> Three mechanisms clear the Ca2+: SERCA (ATP-dependent, about 70%), the Na+–Ca2+ exchanger (most of the rest, powered by the Na+ gradient, not by ATP) and the membrane Ca pump (a small amount). Dephosphorylated phospholamban inhibits SERCA; phosphorylation by PKA relieves the brake. See [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation#3. Relaxation three ways to remove calcium]].

## Which of the following has a POSITIVE inotropic effect on the heart:

- [ ] Hypoxia of the cardiac myocytes
- [ ] Calcium channel blockers
- [x] Digoxin
- [ ] β-adrenergic blockers

> Digoxin blocks the Na-K pump, so intracellular Na+ rises, Na-Ca exchange slows and more Ca2+ stays in the cell. Hypoxia starves the cell of ATP, calcium channel blockers reduce Ca2+ entry and β-blockers stop the cAMP cascade, so all three are negative inotropes, as on the slide. See [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation#5. Inotropic agents and the mechanism of hypoxia]].

## In relation to the resting membrane potential of ventricular muscle cells, which statement is correct:

- [x] It is about −90 mV, close to the equilibrium potential for K+ (−94 mV)
- [ ] It is due primarily to the high permeability of the membrane to Na+
- [ ] Membrane permeability to Ca2+ is very high at rest
- [ ] It drifts spontaneously towards threshold between beats

> The resting potential comes from the high intracellular K+ concentration and the high K+ permeability (iK1); Na+ permeability is only about 10% of that to K+ (the small inward background current ib) and Ca2+ permeability is very low. It is stable, unlike the pacemaker potential of nodal cells. See [[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period#2. The resting membrane]].

## The threshold potential for the ventricular muscle action potential is approximately:

- [ ] −90 mV
- [x] −65 mV
- [ ] −40 mV
- [ ] +40 mV

> At about −65 mV the voltage-sensitive fast Na+ channels open and the cell overshoots to about +40 mV within 1 to 2 ms; −90 mV is the resting potential and −40 mV is where excitability begins to return during repolarisation. See [[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period#3. The five phases]].

## In relation to the action potential of SA node cells, which statement is correct:

- [ ] It has five phases, like the ventricular action potential
- [ ] The pacemaker potential (phase 4) is caused by a progressive rise in K+ permeability
- [x] The upstroke is due primarily to a slow inward Ca2+ current
- [ ] It shows a prolonged plateau phase

> The SA node action potential has only phases 4, 0 and 3: the pre-potential climbs from about −60 to −70 mV because the funny inward Na+ current (if) grows while K+ permeability (and iK) falls, the upstroke is slow and small and carried by Ca2+ because the cells lack functional fast Na+ channels, and iK repolarises it; there is no plateau. See [[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate#2. The SA node action potential]].

## In relation to the intrinsic rates of the pacemaker tissues, which statement is correct:

- [x] The SA node's intrinsic rate is about 100/min
- [ ] The AV node normally sets the heart rate because it is the fastest pacemaker
- [ ] A resting heart rate of 60–70/min is the intrinsic rate of the SA node
- [ ] The Purkinje fibres cannot generate impulses spontaneously

> The fastest pacemaker wins, firing the slower ones before they reach threshold, so the SA node (about 100/min) leads the AV node (40–60/min) and the Purkinje fibres (20–40/min); a resting rate below 100 is the SA node under vagal tone. If the SA impulses cannot reach the ventricles (complete block) a lower pacemaker takes over at its slower rate. See [[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate#4. Why the SA node is the pacemaker, and the heart without nerves]].

## In relation to the spread of excitation through the heart, which statement is correct:

- [ ] The delay at the AV node allows the ventricles to contract before the atria
- [ ] Conduction through atrial muscle is slower than through the AV node
- [ ] The bundle of His is the only electrical connection between the atria and the ventricles
- [x] Conduction velocity is highest in the Purkinje fibres (about 5 m/s)

> Speeds run 0.5 m/s in atrial muscle, 0.05 m/s in the AV node (the only bridge from atria to ventricles, whose delay lets the atria empty first), 1.0 m/s in the bundle of His and 5 m/s in the Purkinje fibres, which excite both ventricles almost at once from the apex up. See [[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System#1. How excitation spreads]].

## In relation to AV-nodal block, which statement is correct:

- [x] In first degree block the PR interval exceeds 0.20 s but every P wave is followed by a QRS complex
- [ ] In second degree block the atria and ventricles are electrically disconnected
- [ ] In third degree block every P wave is followed by a QRS complex after a prolonged PR interval
- [ ] In first degree block some P waves are not followed by a QRS complex

> Grade the strip in order: a long PR with every beat conducted is first degree; some P waves without a QRS (3:2, 2:1) is second degree; regular P waves with no relation to slow, regular QRS complexes is third degree. ## In relation to a premature ventricular contraction (PVC), mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [x] The abnormal QRS complex is preceded by a P wave
- [ ] The QRS complex is wide because the impulse spreads through ordinary ventricular muscle rather than the fast conducting pathways
- [ ] It is followed by a compensatory pause
- [ ] It may arise during acute myocardial ischaemia

> A PVC is an ectopic beat that starts in the ventricle, so there is no P wave before it; the complex is early, abnormal in shape, amplitude and duration, and the next SA impulse finds the ventricles refractory, leaving a long diastolic gap that fills the ventricle more for the following beat. See [[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation#3. Premature ventricular contractions]].

## In relation to Ohm's law applied to the circulation (which statement is correct):

- [ ] Flow is directly proportional to resistance
- [x] With constant flow, the biggest pressure drop marks the biggest resistance
- [ ] Pressure difference equals flow divided by resistance
- [ ] Resistance is directly proportional to the fourth power of the vessel radius

> V = iR becomes ΔP = Q x R, so flow is proportional to the pressure difference and inversely proportional to resistance; with Q the same at every level, R is proportional to ΔP, which puts the resistance in the arterioles. Resistance is proportional to 1/r^4. See [[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles#2. The site of resistance]].

## In relation to nervous control of the peripheral circulation, which statement is correct:

- [ ] The parasympathetic nervous system plays the major role in the control of total peripheral resistance
- [x] Sympathetic innervation is dense in the arterioles of the skin, kidney and gut and poor in those of the brain and heart
- [ ] Capillaries are densely innervated by the sympathetic nervous system
- [ ] A decrease in sympathetic discharge to an organ with resting vasomotor tone causes vasoconstriction

> The sympathetic system, releasing noradrenaline onto α receptors, is the main controller of TPR; all vessels except capillaries and small arterioles are innervated, and where the nerves fire constantly (resting vasomotor tone) less firing dilates and more constricts. The parasympathetic contribution is to heart rate. See [[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles#6. Neural control]].

## In relation to the baroreceptor reflex, which statement is correct:

- [x] Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch increase their firing rate when arterial pressure rises
- [ ] Baroreceptors are located in the SA node and the left ventricle
- [ ] A rise in MAP causes an increase in sympathetic and a decrease in parasympathetic activity
- [ ] The baroreceptor reflex is responsible for the long-term control of arterial pressure

> The carotid sinus (glossopharyngeal nerve) and aortic arch (vagus) report to the medulla, which answers a rise in MAP with more parasympathetic and less sympathetic activity; the reflex resets within days, so long-term control belongs to the kidneys and blood volume. See [[Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex#5. The baroreceptor reflex]].

## In relation to the effect of posture on arterial blood pressure and its regulation, which statement is correct:

- [ ] When standing, the pressure in the femoral artery is about 50 mmHg lower than at heart level
- [ ] When standing, blood pools in the leg arteries, reducing venous return
- [x] On suddenly lying down, the carotid sinus detects a higher pressure and the heart slows
- [ ] On lying down the carotid sinus detects a lower pressure, so heart rate rises

> Standing adds about 50 mmHg to pressures below the heart (femoral 170/130 for brachial 120/80) and blood pools in the distensible leg veins, lowering venous return; lying down removes the column, raises venous return and CO and brings the carotid sinus level with the heart, so the reflex slows the heart and dilates vessels. See [[Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex#5. The baroreceptor reflex]].

## In relation to secondary hypertension, which statement is correct:

- [ ] A phaeochromocytoma raises pressure through excessive aldosterone secretion
- [ ] Conn's syndrome is caused by a catecholamine-secreting tumour
- [ ] Renal artery stenosis lowers blood pressure by suppressing renin release
- [x] Pre-eclamptic toxaemia is the commonest form and remits on delivery

> Pre-eclampsia affects 7 to 10% of pregnancies; Conn's syndrome is excess aldosterone (salt and water retention), a phaeochromocytoma secretes catecholamines that act on α-adrenoceptors, and renal artery stenosis raises pressure by activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. See [[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment#2. Secondary hypertension]].

## In relation to the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, which statement is correct:

- [x] Angiotensin II is a powerful vasoconstrictor and stimulates aldosterone secretion by the adrenal cortex
- [ ] Renin is released from the adrenal medulla in response to a rise in renal blood flow
- [ ] Aldosterone increases Na+ excretion by the kidney
- [ ] ACE inhibitors such as captopril raise blood pressure by increasing angiotensin II formation

> A fall in renal blood flow (the renal artery clip) makes the juxtaglomerular cells release renin, which converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I; ACE makes angiotensin II, which raises TPR at once and, through aldosterone and Na+ reabsorption, raises blood volume. Captopril blocks ACE and lowers pressure. See [[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment#4. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system]].

## In relation to coronary blood flow during the cardiac cycle (which statement is correct):

- [ ] Blood flow into the right ventricular wall is completely interrupted during systole
- [ ] Coronary sinus (venous) outflow is greatest during diastole
- [ ] Compression of the coronary vessels during systole is greatest in the sub-epicardium
- [x] Blood flow into the left ventricular wall is completely interrupted during early systole

> In early systole the rising intramural pressure, greatest in the sub-endocardium, squeezes the left coronary vessels shut while aortic pressure is still low, so the left ventricle is fed mainly in diastole; the right ventricle's lower intramural pressure lets right coronary flow continue in systole, following aortic pressure; and the squeeze wrings blood out of the veins, so venous outflow surges in systole. See [[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow#2. Coronary flow through the cardiac cycle]].

## In relation to cutaneous blood flow, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [ ] Temperature is the principal factor regulating skin blood flow
- [ ] Skin blood flow can vary from about 1 to 150–200 mL/min per 100 g
- [x] Skin blood flow correlates closely with the O2 consumption of the skin
- [ ] Blood flowing through the arteriovenous anastomoses into the venous plexus bypasses the capillaries

> Skin has a low, constant metabolic rate, so unlike muscle, gut and brain its flow does not follow O2 use; temperature sets it over a more than 100-fold range, and the AVAs shunt blood straight from arterioles into the heat-losing venous plexus. See [[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow#3. Skin blood flow is for temperature, not metabolism]] and [[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow#4. Two routes through the skin]].
