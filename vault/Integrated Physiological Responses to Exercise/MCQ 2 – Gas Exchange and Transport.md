## The volume of oxygen in each litre of dry air at sea level is

- [ ] 159 mL
- [x] 209.3 mL
- [ ] 790.3 mL
- [ ] 0.4 mL

> 20.9% of a litre is 209.3 mL. 159 is the PO2 in mmHg, 790.3 mL is the nitrogen volume and 0.4 mL the CO2 volume in a litre of air. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#2. Each gas pushes on its own partial pressure]].

## The two factors that impair gas transfer across the alveolar-capillary membrane are

- [ ] a rise in cardiac output and a rise in ventilation
- [ ] surfactant and the alveolar pores
- [ ] a high PO2 gradient and a short transit time
- [x] thickening of the alveolar membrane and loss of alveolar surface area

> Each lengthens the time needed for alveolar-capillary equilibrium beyond the normal 0.25 s. For someone with pulmonary disease, the added demand for rapid gas exchange in exercise then compromises aeration and performance. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#4. When the exchange is impaired thick membranes, lost surface and too little time]].

## The normal arterial PO2 is

- [x] 75–100 mmHg (10.5–13.5 kPa)
- [ ] 40–46 mmHg
- [ ] 149–159 mmHg
- [ ] 600–760 mmHg

> Arterial blood leaves the lung at about 100 mmHg PO2, and 75–100 mmHg (10.5–13.5 kPa) counts as normal. 40 mmHg is the venous PO2, 149 mmHg is the PO2 in the trachea after the air is humidified, and 159 mmHg is the inspired air. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#3. The pressure staircase from air to muscle and back]].

## The amount of haemoglobin in 100 mL of a man's blood is about

- [ ] 1.5 g
- [ ] 5 g
- [x] 15–16 g
- [ ] 150 g

> About 15–16 g per 100 mL in men and 5–10% less (about 14 g) in women, because testosterone stimulates erythropoietin and so red cell production. More haemoglobin means more oxygen carried, which is why red cell count matters. See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#2. Haemoglobin – Carrying Capacity, Structure and the Route of Oxygen]].

## The iron in haemoglobin that binds oxygen is

- [ ] Fe3+
- [x] Fe2+
- [ ] metallic iron
- [ ] haemosiderin

> One Fe2+ ion sits at the centre of each of the four haem groups and acts as an O2 "magnet"; the Fe2+–O2 bond is weak and completely reversible and does not alter the haem. Oxygenated blood is bright red and deoxygenated blood dark red, the pulmonary artery and vein being the exceptions to the usual artery-vein colours. See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#2. Haemoglobin – Carrying Capacity, Structure and the Route of Oxygen]].

## The oxygen dissociation curve rises steeply up to a PO2 of about

- [x] 50 mmHg
- [ ] 100 mmHg
- [ ] 20 mmHg
- [ ] 159 mmHg

> Saturation climbs rapidly up to about 50 mmHg and then more slowly to a plateau. The flat top means arterial PO2 can fall a long way (as at altitude) before saturation drops much, while the steep part lets the tissues unload O2 easily. See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#4. The Oxygen Dissociation Curve and What Its Shape Does]].

## 2,3-DPG

- [ ] binds to oxyhaemoglobin and stabilises it
- [ ] is made in red cell mitochondria
- [ ] falls at high altitude
- [x] binds to deoxyhaemoglobin and increases O2 unloading

> Red cells have no mitochondria, so 2,3-DPG is an intermediate of their anaerobic metabolism; it binds deoxyhaemoglobin, stabilises that state and so shifts the curve towards unloading. The enzyme that makes it is inhibited by oxyhaemoglobin, so when oxyhaemoglobin falls (altitude, anaemia) 2,3-DPG rises; it also rises in pregnancy to help O2 and CO2 cross the placenta. See [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate#2. The red cell's own release signal 2,3-DPG]].

## The Haldane effect is

- [ ] the right shift of the dissociation curve by acidity
- [ ] the stabilisation of deoxyhaemoglobin by 2,3-DPG
- [x] the driving-off of CO2 from haemoglobin as O2 binds
- [ ] the higher O2 affinity of myoglobin

> As haemoglobin loads oxygen in the lungs it gives up its CO2, the mirror image of the Bohr effect in the tissues. The first option is the Bohr effect and the second is 2,3-DPG. See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#3. Cooperativity – Four Seats That Help Each Other]].

## Carbonic anhydrase catalyses

- [x] CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3
- [ ] the binding of O2 to Fe2+
- [ ] the formation of 2,3-DPG
- [ ] the breakdown of lactate

> It combines CO2 with water to form carbonic acid, which dissociates to bicarbonate and H+; the reaction is reversible and runs the other way in the lungs, where CO2 re-forms and is expired. This is the route for about 70% of the CO2 carried in blood. See [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate#4. The return journey carrying carbon dioxide]].

## Myoglobin

- [ ] has four haem groups like haemoglobin
- [x] has one Fe2+ per molecule and a higher O2 affinity than haemoglobin
- [ ] is more abundant in fast-twitch fibres
- [ ] is strongly affected by the Bohr effect

> Myoglobin is an Fe2+-containing globular protein of skeletal and cardiac muscle with a single iron, so it binds one O2 reversibly; it keeps O2 at low PO2, is more plentiful in slow-twitch fibres (which it colours redder), matters in endurance and is not subject to the Bohr effect. See [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate#3. The muscle's own oxygen store myoglobin]].
