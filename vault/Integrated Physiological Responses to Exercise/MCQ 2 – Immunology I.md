Eight more multiple-choice questions on Immunology I, covering the functions of the immune system, the lymphoid organs, resident cells, cytokines, the components of the two branches, Toll-like receptors, neutrophil extracellular traps and natural killer cells. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## Which of the following is NOT one of the functions of the immune system?

- [ ] Defence against infectious diseases
- [ ] Promotion of wound healing and tissue regeneration
- [x] Regulation of body temperature
- [ ] Elimination of cell debris

> The four functions in the lecture are defence against infectious diseases, cancer fighting, promoting wound healing and tissue regeneration, and eliminating cell debris. Fever can accompany an immune response (IL-1 causes it), but regulating body temperature is not a function of the immune system. See [[Immune System Overview – Functions and the Two Branches#1. What the immune system is for]].

## The lymph nodes, spleen and mucosal immune system are secondary lymphoid organs, which means that they are where immune cells

- [x] are concentrated and function.
- [ ] are formed and mature.
- [ ] are synthesised by haematopoiesis.
- [ ] undergo apoptosis once an infection has resolved.

> The secondary (peripheral) lymphoid organs are where immune cells are concentrated and function, the lymph nodes filtering lymph and the spleen filtering blood. Formation and maturation happen in the primary lymphoid organs, the bone marrow, where haematopoiesis takes place, and the thymus. See [[Cells and tissues of the immune system#2. Where immune cells gather and work the secondary (peripheral) lymphoid organs]].

## Regarding the cells resident in the tissues, which of the following statements is INCORRECT?

- [ ] Dendritic cells detect pathogens and present them to lymphocytes.
- [ ] Mast cells are involved in allergy and defence against helminths.
- [ ] Macrophages carry out phagocytosis.
- [x] Eosinophils are resident in the tissues as sentinel cells.

> The three sentinel cells of the lecture are the dendritic cell, the macrophage and the mast cell, with exactly those jobs. Eosinophils circulate between the blood and the tissues, like neutrophils, monocytes and lymphocytes. See [[Cells and tissues of the immune system#4. Cells that circulate and cells that stand guard]].

## Which of the following is a family of cytokines?

- [ ] Immunoglobulins
- [x] Interferons
- [ ] Complement proteins
- [ ] Prostaglandins

> The cytokine families are the interleukins (IL-1 to IL-22), the interferons (IFN-α, β, γ), the tumour necrosis factors (TNF-α, β) and transforming growth factor-β. Immunoglobulins are antibodies, complement proteins are plasma proteins activated by a pathogen, and prostaglandins are lipid mediators of inflammation, not cytokines. See [[Cytokines#1. What a cytokine is]].

## Which of the following belongs to the adaptive branch of the immune system?

- [ ] Lysozyme in secretions
- [ ] Natural killer cells
- [x] Cell-mediated immunity
- [ ] Complement

> The adaptive branch consists of antibodies, made by B cells, and cell-mediated immunity, carried out by T cells. Physical barriers such as skin and lysozyme, phagocytosis, natural killer cells, complement, inflammatory mediators and antimicrobial peptides all belong to the innate branch, even though NK cells are lymphocytes. See [[Immune System Overview – Functions and the Two Branches#2. Two branches, one system]].

## The same Toll-like receptor on an innate cell can recognise several different microbes because

- [ ] innate cells keep a memory of the microbes they have met before.
- [x] the receptor binds a structure that is shared by a whole group of microbes.
- [ ] each innate cell carries receptors of many different specificities.
- [ ] the receptor is made to fit the microbe only after the microbe has been met.

> Identical Toll-like receptors recognise a common structure on different microbes, because they bind PAMPs that groups of microbes share, such as lipopolysaccharide on all Gram-negative bacteria. This is what non-specific recognition means, and it is why innate immunity needs no memory and is functional from birth. Innate cells do carry several kinds of receptor, but that lets one cell, not one receptor, recognise different microbes. See [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis#1. How innate cells know an enemy]].

## Regarding neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which of the following statements is INCORRECT?

- [x] Neutrophils always die following NETosis.
- [ ] They are formed from nuclear chromatin released into the extracellular space.
- [ ] They trap pathogens, which may then be phagocytosed by other neutrophils and macrophages.
- [ ] Excessive NETosis contributes to thrombosis, autoimmunity and cancer progression.

> Neutrophils may or may not die following NETosis. After phagocytosis, neutrophils more commonly undergo apoptosis, which contributes to the resolution of inflammation, while NETosis releases chromatin as a matrix that traps pathogens and contributes to pathogen killing, but in excess to thrombosis, autoimmunity and cancer progression. See [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis#4. What happens to neutrophils afterwards NETs]].

## Natural killer cells kill their target cells by releasing perforin and granzyme. What does granzyme do?

- [ ] It punches holes in the target cell membrane.
- [ ] It coats the target cell so that phagocytes can engulf it.
- [ ] It activates the complement cascade on the surface of the target cell.
- [x] It enters the cell through the perforin pore and induces apoptosis.

> Perforin punches holes in the target cell membrane, and granzyme enters through the perforin pore and induces the apoptosis (programmed cell death) pathway. Cytotoxic T cells kill with the same two proteins. See [[Complement and Natural Killer Cells#4. Natural killer cells]].
