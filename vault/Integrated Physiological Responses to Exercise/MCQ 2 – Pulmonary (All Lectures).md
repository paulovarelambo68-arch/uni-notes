Ten more multiple-choice questions drawn from all five pulmonary lectures, two per lecture, revisiting the most examinable facts with new stems. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## The membrane separating alveolar air from capillary blood is

- [ ] 2–25 mm thick
- [x] 0.2–2.5 µm thick
- [ ] 20–25 µm thick
- [ ] 1 mm thick

> The capillaries and alveoli lie side by side separated by only 0.2–2.5 µm, which is what makes equilibrium possible in 0.25 s; thickening of this membrane, as in disease, impairs transfer. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#2. The alveoli]].

## Elite athletes, especially swimmers, may show a vital capacity increased by

- [ ] 2–3%
- [ ] 50–60%
- [ ] 100%
- [x] 25–30%

> Breathing against the resistance of water strengthens the respiratory muscles; this is a functional adaptation of the muscles, because the lung tissue itself shows no structural adaptation to training. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#5. What training changes in the lung, and why women reach their limit sooner]].

## Each red blood cell contains about

- [x] 280 million haemoglobin molecules
- [ ] 280 thousand haemoglobin molecules
- [ ] 4 haemoglobin molecules
- [ ] 15 g of haemoglobin

> 280 million molecules, each able to bind four O2, so each cell carries more than 10^9 O2 molecules; 15 g is the haemoglobin in 100 mL of a man's blood. See [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve#2. Haemoglobin – Carrying Capacity, Structure and the Route of Oxygen]].

## The rise in 2,3-DPG during pregnancy

- [ ] lowers the mother's O2 carrying capacity
- [ ] shifts the maternal curve to the left
- [x] facilitates the transport of O2 and CO2 across the placenta
- [ ] has no effect on gas exchange

> More 2,3-DPG stabilises deoxyhaemoglobin and increases unloading, so the maternal blood gives up its O2 more readily to the foetus. See [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate#2. The red cell's own release signal 2,3-DPG]].

## The peripheral chemoreceptors' share of the ventilatory response to a rise in PaCO2 is about

- [ ] 80%
- [x] 20%
- [ ] 50%
- [ ] 0%

> 20% from the peripheral receptors, which respond to the acidosis (rise in H+) that CO2 causes, and 80% from the central chemoreceptors of the medulla; the peripheral response is weaker but about five times faster. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## Ventilation at the same work rate after a period of endurance training is

- [x] lower, because less lactate gives less feedback to breathing
- [ ] higher, because the lungs have grown
- [ ] unchanged
- [ ] higher, because dead space grows

> This is a chronic effect of exercise: the same work produces less lactic acid, so there is less chemical feedback to the respiratory centre. A blunted blood lactate response is also one of the three factors behind the small endurance gain of altitude acclimatisation, together with ventilatory acclimatisation and a higher arterial O2 saturation. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#9. Training and heat]].

## Atmospheric pressure at the Pikes Peak research centre (4,300 m) is about

- [ ] 760 mmHg
- [ ] 235 mmHg
- [ ] 600 mmHg
- [x] 450 mmHg

> 450 mmHg at 4,300 m, 235 mmHg on Everest and 760 mmHg at sea level; at 4,300 m a 100 W effort that uses 2.0 L/min of O2 is 70% of the reduced VO2max instead of 50% at sea level. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#1. Less oxygen in every breath]].

## At about 80% of VO2max, the oxygen cost of hyperventilation is

- [ ] lower in smokers than in non-smokers
- [ ] the same in both
- [x] higher in smokers (about 10.1 against 7.2 mL per litre of air)
- [ ] zero in non-smokers

> Smoking raises airway resistance through a vagal reflex, so each litre breathed costs more oxygen; this competes with the working muscles for O2 at high intensity. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#6. Smoking – acute effects of one cigarette and chronic damage]].

## The pH inside the mature phagolysosome is about

- [ ] 7.4
- [x] 4.5
- [ ] 6.1
- [ ] 9.0

> The phagosome acidifies as it matures, from pH 6.1 in the early phagosome (Rab5, EEA1) to 5.5 in the late phagosome (Rab7, LAMP-1) and 4.5 after fusion with the lysosome, where hydrolases and NADPH oxidase destroy the particle. See [[Lung Immune Cells – Toll-like Receptors, Macrophages and Neutrophils#6. The oxidative burst]].

## Neutrophils make up what share of white blood cells?

- [ ] 1–6%
- [ ] 20–40%
- [x] 40–75%
- [ ] under 1%

> Neutrophils are 40–75% of white cells and live hours to a few days, targeting bacteria and fungi; lymphocytes are 20–40%, eosinophils 1–6% (parasites), monocytes 2–6% and basophils under 1%. See [[Lung Immune Cells – Toll-like Receptors, Macrophages and Neutrophils#5. The neutrophil]].
