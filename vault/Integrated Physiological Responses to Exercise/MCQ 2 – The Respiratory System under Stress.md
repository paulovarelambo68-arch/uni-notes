Ten more multiple-choice questions on the respiratory system under stress: what sets the energy cost of breathing, the cost in lung disease, exercise-induced arterial hypoxaemia, haemoglobin and protein buffering, the acute left shift at altitude, the fall in VO2max with altitude, blood doping, chronic bronchitis and the prolonged Valsalva. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## The energy requirement of breathing is determined by

- [ ] haemoglobin concentration
- [ ] cardiac output
- [x] the compliance of the lungs and thorax and the resistance of the airways
- [ ] the anatomic dead space alone

> Compliance is reduced by smoking-related COPD, ARDS and fibrosis, and airway resistance is raised by asthma and COPD, so in respiratory disease the work of breathing becomes excessive and alveolar ventilation often becomes inadequate in exercise. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#1. What breathing costs – compliance, airway resistance and the oxygen cost of breathing]].

## In pulmonary disease, the energy requirement of breathing during exercise can reach

- [x] 40% of total exercise O2 use
- [ ] 5% of total exercise O2 use
- [ ] 15% of total exercise O2 use
- [ ] 90% of total exercise O2 use

> Against 3–5% in moderate exercise and 8–11% at VO2max in health, and 15% or more in elite athletes, a diseased lung can spend 40% of the oxygen taken up just on breathing, leaving the locomotor muscles short. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#1. What breathing costs – compliance, airway resistance and the oxygen cost of breathing]].

## Exercise-induced arterial hypoxaemia is

- [ ] the normal rise in PaO2 with hyperventilation
- [ ] a fall in alveolar PO2 at maximal exercise
- [ ] the fall in ventilation after training
- [x] an impaired ventilation–perfusion ratio in intense exercise that lowers arterial saturation

> In endurance athletes the pulmonary system lags behind their exceptional cardiovascular and muscular adaptations, so during maximal exercise the A-a gradient widens and PaO2 falls. The possible causes are V-P inequality within the lungs, shunting between venous and arterial circulations and failure to reach end-capillary equilibrium. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#4. The exception – exercise-induced arterial hypoxaemia]].

## As a buffer, haemoglobin is more potent than the other plasma proteins by a factor of about

- [ ] 2
- [x] 6
- [ ] 60
- [ ] 0.5

> Haemoglobin is an important H+ acceptor and is six times more potent in buffering than the other plasma proteins. Proteins buffer through the charged amino and carboxyl groups of their amino acids. See [[Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney#4. Phosphate and protein buffers, and why haemoglobin leads]].

## The proteins account for what share of the buffering power of the blood?

- [ ] One-tenth
- [ ] One-third
- [x] Two-thirds
- [ ] All of it

> Protein buffering provides two-thirds of the blood's buffering power and most of the buffering inside cells; bicarbonate and phosphate buffers provide the rest. See [[Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney#4. Phosphate and protein buffers, and why haemoglobin leads]].

## On acute exposure to altitude the oxygen dissociation curve shifts

- [x] to the left
- [ ] to the right
- [ ] not at all
- [ ] it flattens

> Hyperventilation blows off CO2 and lowers H+ in blood and CSF, so haemoglobin binds O2 more tightly: a left shift, together with raised heart rate, blood pressure and catecholamines and a fall in VO2max. Only with chronic exposure, as 2,3-DPG rises, does the curve shift slightly right for more unloading. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#3. The first days the acute response]].

## VO2max declines with altitude by about

- [ ] 1% per 1,000 m
- [ ] 10% per 300 m
- [ ] 3% per 1,000 m
- [x] 3% per 300 m (1,000 ft)

> The decline begins above about 1,524 m in most people, and above 6,000 m there is progressive deterioration leading to death. The O2 cost of a given task is the same at altitude, so it is performed at a higher percentage of the reduced VO2max and feels harder. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#6. Exercising up there]].

## Marco Pantani's haematocrit at the height of EPO abuse was

- [ ] 45%
- [x] 60.1%
- [ ] 38%
- [ ] 75%

> Normal haematocrit is about 45% in men and 40% in women; recombinant EPO, invented in the late 1980s for kidney-failure patients, raised Pantani's to 60.1%, and the high blood viscosity that comes with such abuse is dangerous. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#4. The first weeks acclimatisation]].

## Frequent cough and phlegm in a smoker (chronic bronchitis) indicate

- [ ] lung cancer
- [ ] emphysema
- [x] the first stage of COPD
- [ ] pulmonary hypertension

> Smoke chemicals and particulates inhibit the cilia of the mucociliary escalator, so mucus and foreign matter clog the bronchi. Emphysema follows when the alveoli lose their elastic material, and tobacco-induced atherosclerosis of the vessels between lungs and heart causes pulmonary hypertension. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#6. Smoking – acute effects of one cigarette and chronic damage]].

## A prolonged Valsalva manoeuvre lowers blood pressure because

- [x] the raised intrathoracic pressure collapses the thoracic veins and cuts venous return
- [ ] the glottis opens and air escapes
- [ ] the diaphragm relaxes
- [ ] the heart rate falls

> Less venous return means a smaller stroke volume, a lower blood pressure and less blood to the brain, hence dizziness, spots before the eyes and fainting (syncope). The manoeuvre is thought to improve leverage during lifting, but it should not be held. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#8. Straining against a closed throat the Valsalva manoeuvre]].
