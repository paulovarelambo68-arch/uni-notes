Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## During the first few seconds of all-out exercise, the largest share of the energy comes from:

- [ ] The glycolytic–lactate system
- [ ] Mitochondrial respiration
- [ ] Fat oxidation
- [x] The phosphagen system

> In the energy-system contribution curve the phosphagen (ATP–PCr) system peaks almost immediately and falls away within about a minute; glycolysis peaks next, and mitochondrial respiration rises to dominate the later minutes. See Integrated Response, slide 3 (The energy systems and exercise).

## Which of the following best summarises the relationship between metabolism and ventilation during exercise?

- [ ] Ventilation sets the rate of ATP production in muscle
- [x] Metabolism drives gas exchange
- [ ] Ventilation and metabolism change independently of each other
- [ ] Gas exchange determines the ATP demand of exercise

> Metabolism drives gas exchange, while ventilation helps regulate the metabolic environment. The respiratory–metabolic axis runs from ATP demand to muscle VO₂ and VCO₂, then acid–base change, ventilatory drive and finally ventilation, which eliminates CO₂ and restores homeostasis; the demand comes from metabolism and ventilation answers it. See Integrated Response, slide 4 (The respiratory–metabolic axis).

## Central chemoreceptors increase ventilation in response to:

- [x] CO₂-related changes in H⁺ in the central nervous system
- [ ] A fall in arterial O₂
- [ ] Blood lactate acting directly on the brainstem
- [ ] Motor command from the motor cortex

> The central chemoreceptors respond primarily to CO₂-related changes in CNS H⁺; O₂, CO₂ and H⁺ in the arterial blood are sensed by the peripheral chemoreceptors of the carotid and aortic bodies, and motor command is the neural feed-forward component. See Integrated Response, slide 8 (What tells the brain to increase ventilation?).

## An RER of 0.70 indicates that energy is being derived mainly from:

- [ ] Carbohydrate
- [ ] Protein
- [x] Fat
- [ ] An equal mixture of carbohydrate and fat

> RER ranges from 0.7, when fat is the main fuel, to 1.0, when carbohydrate is; a mixed fuel supply at rest gives about 0.78–0.80. See Integrated Response, slide 12 (Respiratory Exchange Ratio (RER)).

## According to the Fick equation, VO₂ is the product of:

- [ ] Heart rate and stroke volume
- [ ] Minute ventilation and the inspired O₂ fraction
- [ ] VCO₂ and RER
- [x] Cardiac output and the arteriovenous O₂ difference (CaO₂ − CvO₂)

> VO₂ = Q × (CaO₂ − CvO₂): oxygen uptake is constrained by delivery (cardiac output = HR × SV, and the arterial O₂ content) and by extraction (muscle blood flow and mitochondrial O₂ use). HR × SV on its own gives only cardiac output. See Integrated Response, slide 16 (VO₂ is constrained by delivery and extraction).

## When a relatively homogeneous group begins the same training programme, which of the following should be expected?

- [x] Individuals do not respond similarly to the same training stimulus
- [ ] Each person achieves the same fitness improvement
- [ ] Those with the highest initial fitness improve the most
- [ ] Improvement depends only on the training volume completed

> The individual differences principle: one cannot expect each person to achieve the same improvement, so optimal benefits come from programmes built around the participant's own needs and capacities. The fittest at the start in fact have the least room to improve. See Training & Detraining, slide 7 (Individual Differences Principle) and slide 20 (Initial Aerobic Fitness Level).

## Which of the following is an adaptation to sprint–power (anaerobic) training?

- [ ] Larger and more numerous mitochondria in the fast-twitch fibres
- [ ] Lower blood lactate during all-out effort
- [x] A greater capacity to produce and tolerate lactate
- [ ] A greater proportion of slow-twitch fibres

> Anaerobic training raises the anaerobic substrates (ATP, PCr, glycogen), the quantity and activity of the enzymes of the anaerobic phase of glucose catabolism, the capacity to produce and tolerate high lactate, and motivation and tolerance of "pain". Bigger mitochondria and lower lactate are aerobic-training adaptations. See Training & Detraining, slide 10 (Anaerobic System Changes with Training).

## Which mechanism underlies the increase in stroke volume with endurance training?

- [ ] Concentric left ventricular hypertrophy with a smaller chamber
- [x] Eccentric hypertrophy with a larger left ventricle
- [ ] A higher resting heart rate
- [ ] A reduced blood volume

> The trained ventricle enlarges its cavity (eccentric hypertrophy) and contracts more forcefully, so end-diastolic volume and ejection fraction both rise; concentric hypertrophy is the pressure-load pattern of resistance training. Resting heart rate falls and blood volume rises with endurance training. See Training & Detraining, slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training) and slide 14 (Aerobic Training Increases Oxygen Delivery to Active Muscles).

## The modified equation for age-predicted maximum heart rate given in the lecture is:

- [ ] HRmax = 220 − age
- [ ] HRmax = 210 − (0.5 × age)
- [ ] HRmax = 200 − (0.7 × age)
- [x] HRmax = 206.9 − (0.67 × age)

> The classic rule is 220 − age; the modified equation is 206.9 − 0.67 × age, which gives slightly lower values in the young and higher values in older adults (for a 70-year-old, 160 rather than 150 beats/min). Both are rules of thumb that do not determine a particular person's true HRmax. See Training & Detraining, slide 23 (Determining the "Training-Sensitive Zone").

## After more than 4 weeks without training, VO₂max may fall by up to:

- [ ] 5%
- [ ] 10%
- [x] 20%
- [ ] 50%

> Beyond 4 weeks of detraining, left ventricular mass and mitochondrial enzymes fall and the drop in VO₂max becomes large, up to 20%; the earliest change (weeks 1–2) is a fall in plasma volume with a smaller loss of stroke volume and VO₂max. See Training & Detraining, slide 32 (Detraining (Reversal of Adaptations)).
