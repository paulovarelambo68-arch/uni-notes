Ten more multiple-choice questions on the control of breathing and oxygen uptake: humoral and neural factors, temperature, the starting-line response, breathing rate in exercise, which gas the respiratory centre answers, the VO2 equation, expired oxygen, QO2 and VO2, the trained lactate threshold and the VO2max test. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## "Humoral" factors in the control of ventilation are those

- [ ] arising in the motor cortex
- [x] carried in the blood, such as PCO2, acidity, PO2 and temperature
- [ ] arising in joints and tendons
- [ ] arising in the pons

> Humoral means transported by the blood. Variations in arterial PCO2, acidity, PO2 and temperature activate sensitive neural units in the medulla and the arterial system, the chemoreceptors. The cortex and the joints supply the neural (non-chemical) factors. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## Which of the following has little effect on breathing during exercise, except in extreme hyperthermia?

- [ ] Arterial PCO2
- [ ] Neural outflow from the motor cortex
- [ ] Sensory input from joints, tendons and muscles
- [x] Core temperature

> Ventilation rises at exercise onset and falls in recovery far too quickly to be following core temperature; temperature matters instead through its effect on the oxygen dissociation curve. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#6. The nervous drives]].

## The abrupt rise in ventilation at the starting line, before the race begins, comes from

- [x] cortical activation in anticipation of exercise
- [ ] a rise in arterial PCO2
- [ ] the peripheral chemoreceptors
- [ ] a fall in blood pressure

> Neural outflow from regions of the motor cortex and cortical activation in anticipation stimulate the respiratory neurons of the medulla; input from joints, tendons and muscles then adjusts ventilation throughout the exercise. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#6. The nervous drives]].

## In exercise, breathing rate can rise from 12–15 breaths/min to about

- [ ] 20–25 breaths/min
- [ ] 30 breaths/min
- [x] 48–60 breaths/min
- [ ] 120 breaths/min

> Rate rises to 48–60 breaths/min and tidal volume from 0.5 L to 2.5 L or more, so gas exchange rises at the lungs and the tissues and VO2 climbs from its resting 0.25 L/min to power aerobic energy production. See [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt#2. Minute ventilation and its numbers]].

## The respiratory centre in the brain responds to

- [ ] a fall in O2 but not a rise in CO2
- [x] a rise in CO2 but not a fall in O2
- [ ] both equally
- [ ] neither; only to temperature

> The central chemoreceptors answer CO2 (through H+); a fall in O2 is sensed only by the peripheral chemoreceptors in the aortic and carotid bodies, and in exercise alveolar PO2 does not fall enough to drive them. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## Oxygen consumption (VO2) =

- [ ] VE x FeO2
- [ ] VI x FiO2
- [ ] FiO2 – FeO2
- [x] [(VI x FiO2) – (VE x FeO2)] / t

> VO2 is the oxygen inhaled minus the oxygen exhaled per unit time: inhaled volume times its O2 fraction, minus exhaled volume times its O2 fraction, divided by the time of the measurement. The first two options are only the inhaled or the exhaled oxygen. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#2. Measuring VO2 from the air breathed]].

## The fraction of oxygen in expired air at rest is about

- [x] 16%
- [ ] 20.9%
- [ ] 12–14%
- [ ] 4%

> Room air holds 20.9% O2 and expired air about 16% at rest, falling to 12–14% during exercise as more of the inhaled oxygen is extracted. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#2. Measuring VO2 from the air breathed]].

## In sub-maximal steady-state exercise, oxygen utilisation by the tissues (QO2)

- [ ] exceeds VO2
- [ ] is less than VO2
- [x] equals the VO2 measured at the mouth
- [ ] cannot be related to VO2

> Because the lung is treated as an otherwise sealed system, external respiration measured at the mouth (VO2 and VCO2) represents internal respiration (QO2 and QCO2) once a steady state is reached. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#1. Oxygen used equals oxygen taken in]].

## In highly trained endurance athletes, OBLA can occur at

- [ ] 25% of VO2max
- [x] 80% of VO2max or more
- [ ] 55–65% of VO2max
- [ ] 100% of VO2max

> Training pushes the lactate break point from 55–65% of VO2max in the untrained to 80% or higher, often without VO2max itself rising. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#4. How much of the ceiling you can use the lactate threshold]].

## Measuring VO2max requires

- [ ] resting spirometry
- [ ] a single-stage six-minute walk
- [ ] a resting blood lactate sample
- [x] a graded exercise test to exhaustion while measuring ventilation, O2 and CO2 in the inhaled and exhaled air

> The cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPX) on a treadmill or cycle ergometer raises intensity progressively, for example an incline raised every 2 minutes (0, 3, 6, 8% over 8:30 of running), so that the effort is long and hard enough to fully tax the aerobic energy system. Estimates without gas measurement are less accurate. See [[Oxygen Uptake – VO2, VO2max and the Lactate Threshold#3. The ceiling VO2max and how it is tested]].
