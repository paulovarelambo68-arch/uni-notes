## In relation to cardiac output in a normal young adult, which statement is correct:

- [x] Cardiac output is the volume of blood ejected by either ventricle in 1 min
- [ ] Left ventricular output is about 10% greater than right ventricular output
- [ ] Cardiac output at rest is 30–35 L/min
- [ ] During heavy exercise the proportion of the cardiac output going to the kidneys increases

> CO = SV x HR, 5 to 6 L/min at rest and 30 to 35 L/min in severe exercise, and because the circuits are in series the two ventricles pump the same volume. In exercise sympathetic vasoconstriction cuts renal and splanchnic flow while working muscle takes 80 to 85%. See [[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation#4. The three features and the one equation]] and [[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation#7. How the cardiac output is shared out]].

## In relation to the pulmonary circulation, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [ ] Blood is pumped into the pulmonary arteries by the right ventricle
- [ ] The pulmonary vasculature has a very low resistance to flow
- [x] The bronchi receive their blood supply from the pulmonary arteries
- [ ] Blood in the pulmonary veins has a high O2 content

> The airways, which do no gas exchange, are fed with oxygenated blood by the bronchial arteries of the systemic circulation. The lungs sit close to the heart with very low resistance, so little energy is needed to pump the right ventricle's deoxygenated output through them and back, oxygenated, to the left atrium. See [[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation#2. Two circuits in series]].

## In relation to ventricular ejection (which statement is correct):

- [ ] Ejection begins when left ventricular pressure exceeds left atrial pressure
- [ ] Ventricular ejection lasts about 0.05 s at rest
- [ ] The ventricles depolarise (QRS complex) during the ejection phase
- [x] Approximately two-thirds of the blood is ejected in the first one-third of the ejection phase

> Ejection (0.3 s) begins when LVP exceeds aortic pressure and the aortic valve opens; LVP and AP rise together to a maximum at mid-systole, most blood leaving in the rapid phase, and in reduced ejection both fall while the ventricles repolarise (T wave). The QRS precedes contraction, at the start of isovolumetric contraction. See [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#4. Systole in two steps]].

## In relation to isovolumetric ventricular relaxation, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [ ] All four valves are closed
- [ ] Ventricular volume is at its minimum (end-systolic volume)
- [x] It begins with the opening of the AV valves
- [ ] It lasts approximately 0.05 s

> Isovolumetric relaxation begins when the semilunar valves close and ends when falling ventricular pressure drops below atrial pressure and the AV valve opens, which starts filling; in between there is no flow and no change in volume. See [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds#3. Diastole in three steps]].

## In relation to the length–tension relationship of cardiac muscle, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [x] An overly dilated heart produces more active tension because its fibres are stretched further
- [ ] Passive tension rises steeply at long muscle lengths
- [ ] Active tension rises with length up to an optimum and then falls
- [ ] Resting length is just below the optimum, so a slight stretch increases total tension

> Active tension peaks at the optimum overlap of actin and myosin; past it fewer cross-bridges can form, so an over-stretched, dilated heart develops less active tension and is not a good pump. The slight stretch that raises force underlies the heart's control of stroke volume. See [[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling’s Law and Contractility#1. Length and tension in cardiac muscle]].

## In relation to the mechanism by which hypoxia reduces myocardial contractility, which statement is correct:

- [x] Reduced ATP availability and the accumulation of H+ and phosphate reduce the force produced for a given Ca2+ concentration and length
- [ ] Hypoxia increases Ca2+ entry through the L-type channels
- [ ] Hypoxia increases SERCA activity so that Ca2+ is removed from the cytoplasm more rapidly
- [ ] Hypoxia raises the intracellular cAMP concentration

> Without O2 the cell makes little ATP, so the myosin heads cycle more slowly and ATP-dependent SERCA refills the SR less; the acid and phosphate from anaerobic metabolism compete with Ca2+ for troponin C and weaken each cross-bridge, so less force comes from the same Ca2+ and the same stretch, which is a fall in contractility. See [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation#5. Inotropic agents and the mechanism of hypoxia]].

## In relation to sympathetic stimulation of cardiac muscle, which statement is correct:

- [ ] It increases active tension but slows relaxation
- [ ] It increases contractile force by recruiting additional muscle fibres
- [ ] Its positive inotropic effect is due to a fall in intracellular cAMP
- [x] It increases active tension, the rate of tension development and the rate of relaxation

> The adrenaline trace peaks higher, rises faster and falls sooner than the control at the same preload, a true rise in contractility; because the heart is a syncytium every cell already contracts on every beat (all-or-none), so there are no extra fibres to recruit, and the effect runs through more cAMP, not less. See [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation#4. Sympathetic stimulation more force, faster, and faster relaxation]] and [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation#1. The contractile proteins]].

## In relation to the plateau phase of the cardiac action potential, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [ ] It is a feature unique to cardiac muscle
- [ ] The Ca2+ that enters during the plateau directly influences the strength of contraction
- [ ] The inward Ca2+ current almost counterbalances the outward K+ current, holding the potential at about 0 to −20 mV
- [x] Its long duration makes a fused (tetanic) series of contractions possible in cardiac muscle

> The long plateau keeps the cell refractory for 200 to 400 ms while its contraction lasts only 200 to 250 ms, so contractions can never fuse; tetanus, which skeletal muscle can show, would stop the heart filling and be fatal. See [[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period#4. Why the plateau is long]].

## In relation to the fast Na+ channel of cardiac muscle, which statement is correct:

- [ ] The m gate is the inactivation gate
- [x] During the plateau the h (inactivation) gate is closed, so the cell is inexcitable
- [ ] Both gates are closed during the upstroke of the action potential
- [ ] The h gate reopens as soon as the membrane potential reaches +40 mV

> The m (activation) gate opens on depolarisation and the h (inactivation) gate shuts shortly after, so Na+ flows only during the upstroke when both are open; the h gate reopens only when the membrane repolarises, which is the basis of both refractory periods. See [[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period#5. Refractory periods and the sodium-channel gates]].

## In relation to parasympathetic (vagal) stimulation of the SA node, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [ ] Acetylcholine binds to muscarinic (M2) receptors
- [x] It raises the intracellular concentration of cyclic AMP
- [ ] It hyperpolarises the SA node cells by increasing K+ conductance
- [ ] It reduces the slope of the pacemaker potential, so threshold is reached later

> Acetylcholine lowers cAMP, which reduces if and iCa and flattens the climb, and opens KACh channels, which hyperpolarises the cell; both delay threshold and slow the heart. A rise in cAMP is the sympathetic (noradrenaline, β1) effect. See [[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate#3. Autonomic control of heart rate]].

## In relation to sympathetic control of heart rate, which statement is correct:

- [x] Noradrenaline binds β1-adrenergic receptors and increases both if and iCa, steepening the pacemaker potential
- [ ] Noradrenaline binds α1-adrenergic receptors on the SA node
- [ ] Sympathetic stimulation prolongs the action potential of working myocardial cells
- [ ] Sympathetic stimulation lowers cAMP in the SA node cells

> Through cAMP and protein kinase A the sympathetic transmitter raises GNa (if) and GCa (iCa) in the node, and in working muscle PKA also phosphorylates the K+ channel, increasing iK and shortening the action potential so that its length does not limit heart rate. α receptors are the vascular ones. See [[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate#3. Autonomic control of heart rate]].

## When travelling at −150° in the frontal plane, a ventricular depolarisation wave causes a large positive deflection in which lead?

- [ ] aVL
- [x] aVR
- [ ] Lead II
- [ ] aVF

> aVR looks at the heart from the right arm, at −150°; because the normal mean QRS axis is about +60°, almost opposite, aVR is normally the one mainly negative limb lead, and a wave running at −150° would instead be upright in it and negative in lead II. aVL is −30° and aVF +90°. See [[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System#5. The twelve leads limb leads, the hexaxial system and chest leads]].

## In relation to the PR interval, which statement is correct:

- [ ] It is measured from the end of the P wave to the start of the QRS complex
- [ ] It normally lasts about 0.04 s, one small square
- [x] It reflects conduction time through the AV node and normally lasts 0.12–0.20 s
- [ ] It represents the time taken for the ventricles to depolarise

> The PR interval runs from the start of the P wave (atrial depolarisation) to the start of the QRS (ventricular depolarisation), three to five small squares, most of it the wait in the AV node; longer than 0.20 s is first degree block. See [[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System#6. Reading the paper calibration, intervals and heart rate]].

## In relation to defibrillation, which statement is correct:

- [x] The shock depolarises all the heart muscle cells simultaneously, so the re-entrant circuits die out and the SA node regains control
- [ ] The shock selectively stimulates the SA node
- [ ] It is the treatment of choice for first degree AV-nodal block
- [ ] It works by hyperpolarising the ventricular muscle

> A looping signal can only run into recovered tissue; with every cell made refractory at the same instant the loops of ventricular fibrillation have nowhere to go, and as the cells recover the fastest pacemaker takes over again. See [[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation#5. Ventricular fibrillation and defibrillation]].

## In relation to re-entry and ventricular fibrillation, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Ventricular fibrillation is compatible with a normal cardiac output for several hours
- [x] Re-entry requires an area of injured tissue that conducts in one direction only
- [ ] In normal tissue two depolarisation waves that meet sum to give a larger action potential
- [ ] ST segment elevation is the ECG sign of sinus arrhythmia

> Normally the two halves of a wave meeting around an obstacle cancel out, because each runs into refractory tissue; a one-way injured patch lets one half return slowly and re-excite recovered tissue too early. Uncontrolled re-entry gives VF, with no cardiac output and death within minutes; ST elevation marks a myocardial infarction. See [[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation#4. Re-entry]], [[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation#5. Ventricular fibrillation and defibrillation]] and [[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation#6. The ischaemic marker on the trace]].

## In relation to autoregulation of blood flow, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [ ] Between MAPs of 70 and 170 mmHg blood flow is kept constant
- [x] It is mediated by the sympathetic nervous system
- [ ] A rise in arterial pressure causes the arterioles to constrict
- [ ] Flow drops to zero at the critical closing pressure of 20–30 mmHg

> Autoregulation is the myogenic response: the arteriolar smooth muscle itself contracts when wall tension rises and relaxes when it falls, with no nerve or hormone, keeping flow to brain, kidney and heart constant when their metabolism is constant. See [[Vascular Resistance – Pressure Profile, Radius and Control of Arterioles#4. Autoregulation]].

## In relation to hormonal and paracrine control of arteriolar tone, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Adrenaline causes vasoconstriction in skeletal muscle because of its high β-receptor density
- [ ] Endothelin is a vasodilator paracrine produced by endothelial cells
- [x] Nitric oxide released by endothelial cells raises cGMP in the vascular smooth muscle, which relaxes
- [ ] Noradrenaline causes vasodilation in organs with a high α-receptor density

> NO diffuses from the endothelium to the smooth muscle and is released continuously, so resting arterioles are already partly dilated (Viagra blocks cGMP breakdown); endothelin and thromboxane are the paracrine constrictors, noradrenaline always constricts through α receptors, and adrenaline dilates the vessels of heart and skeletal muscle through their β receptors. ## In relation to normal arterial blood pressure and its change with age, which statement is correct:

- [ ] Diastolic pressure continues to rise throughout life
- [ ] Mean arterial pressure falls with age because TPR falls
- [x] Systolic pressure rises with age because the compliance of the aorta falls
- [ ] A typical blood pressure at 20 years old is 140/80 mmHg

> Normal resting values are 110 to 139 over 70 to 84 mmHg, MAP 95 to 100; a typical 20-year-old reads 110/70 and a 60-year-old 140/80. The stiffening aorta pushes systolic up throughout life, diastolic peaks at about 50 and then falls (which may reduce coronary flow), and MAP rises moderately because TPR rises. See [[Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex#4. Normal values and age]].

## In the baroreceptor reflex response to a rise in mean arterial pressure, which of the following is INCORRECT:

- [ ] Reduced sympathetic and increased parasympathetic activity to the SA node lower heart rate
- [ ] Reduced contractility lowers stroke volume
- [ ] Vasodilation of the systemic arterioles lowers TPR
- [x] Venoconstriction increases venous return and end-diastolic volume

> A rise in MAP makes the medulla withdraw sympathetic drive to the veins as well, so they dilate, venous pressure and venous return fall, EDV and SV fall, and with the lower HR and TPR the pressure comes back down; venoconstriction is the response to a fall in pressure. See [[Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex#5. The baroreceptor reflex]].

## In relation to essential hypertension, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [ ] In young hypertensives the raised MAP is associated with increased heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output
- [x] The malignant form is the commonest and is slowly progressive and almost symptomless
- [ ] In older hypertensives the raised MAP is maintained by an elevated total peripheral resistance
- [ ] Untreated, about 50% of patients eventually develop heart failure

> Benign essential hypertension is the common, slow, symptomless form found at routine check-ups; malignant hypertension is rare and rapidly progressive, with cardiac failure, renal damage and cerebral damage with papilloedema. Of untreated patients about 50% develop heart failure, 25% renal failure and 25% cerebral complications. See [[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment#5. Essential hypertension benign, malignant, young and old]] and [[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment#7. What hypertension does]].

## In relation to the treatment of hypertension, mark the INCORRECT statement:

- [x] β-adrenoceptor blockers such as propranolol lower blood pressure by increasing cardiac output
- [ ] Calcium channel blockers (nifedipine) and α1-adrenoceptor blockers (prazosin) lower TPR
- [ ] Diuretics lower blood pressure by reducing the extracellular fluid volume
- [ ] ACE inhibitors (captopril) reduce the formation of both angiotensin II and aldosterone

> Each avenue attacks one side of MAP = CO x TPR, and β-blockers lower pressure by reducing cardiac output (lower rate and contractility); treatment aims at 135–145/85 mmHg and greatly reduces mortality and morbidity. See [[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment#9. Treatment]].

## Which of the following would be expected to REDUCE blood flow to the left ventricular myocardium:

- [ ] An increase in the local release of adenosine
- [ ] A rise in aortic diastolic pressure
- [ ] Hypoxia of the myocardium
- [x] A reduction in the duration of diastole (tachycardia)

> The left ventricle is fed mainly in diastole, so less diastolic time, or a lower diastolic pressure to push blood in, cuts its supply and can cause ischaemia; adenosine and hypoxia dilate the coronary arterioles and raise flow. ## In relation to heat exhaustion during heavy exercise in a hot environment, which statement is correct:

- [ ] The fall in MAP is caused by a rise in TPR
- [ ] Sweating increases plasma volume and venous return
- [x] Cutaneous and muscle vasodilation lower TPR while sweating lowers plasma volume and venous return, so cardiac output and MAP fall
- [ ] Cardiac output rises because the Frank–Starling mechanism increases stroke volume

> With MAP = CO x TPR both terms fall: skin and muscle beds in parallel compete for an output that cannot rise without venous return, and the Frank-Starling mechanism lowers stroke volume as filling drops, so the athlete collapses; the soldier standing to attention in the heat faints by the same route. See [[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow#6. When skin vasodilation lowers blood pressure]].
