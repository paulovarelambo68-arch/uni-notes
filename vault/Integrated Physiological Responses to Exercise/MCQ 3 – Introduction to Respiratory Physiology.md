Ten more multiple-choice questions on the first pulmonary lecture: the respiratory adaptations to training, how athletes breathe in exercise, the lung volumes in acute exercise, airflow in the small airways, the differences between women and men, the conducting zone, lung size and what lung function tests show in athletes. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## Which structural adaptation does the respiratory system itself show after high-level endurance training?

- [ ] Larger alveoli
- [x] None
- [ ] A thinner alveolar membrane
- [ ] More alveoli

> The lung tissue itself does not adapt; the chest muscles and diaphragm do, giving greater respiratory muscle endurance and strength, a possible 25–30% rise in vital capacity in elite athletes (swimmers especially, who breathe against the resistance of the water) and a higher VE/VO2 ratio. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#5. What training changes in the lung, and why women reach their limit sooner]].

## In moderate exercise, well-trained athletes maintain alveolar ventilation mainly by

- [ ] a large rise in breathing rate
- [ ] breathing deeper into the expiratory reserve volume
- [x] raising tidal volume with only a small rise in breathing rate
- [ ] shortening the inspiratory phase

> They deepen each breath, encroaching on the inspiratory reserve more than the expiratory reserve. Tidal volume plateaus at about 60% of FVC, after which further rises in minute ventilation come from a subconscious rise in rate. See [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt#3. How trained athletes grow the breath first]].

## In acute exercise the tidal volume

- [x] can increase up to 4 times
- [ ] stays at 500 mL while the rate rises
- [ ] decreases slightly
- [ ] increases up to 60 times

> Tidal volume rises from 500 mL up to about four times that, breathing frequency from 12–15 up to 60 breaths/min, and minute ventilation from 6 L/min up to 175 L/min in large, aerobically trained athletes. IRV and ERV decrease, and VC, residual volume and TLC decrease slightly. See [[Lung Volumes and Capacities – Spirometry, FEV1 and FVC#5. What one bout of exercise does to each volume]].

## Residual lung volume rises temporarily after a bout of exercise because of

- [ ] stronger elastic recoil
- [ ] a rise in surfactant
- [ ] a fall in thoracic blood volume
- [x] closure of small peripheral airways and a rise in thoracic blood volume

> Both changes trap air at the end of a maximal exhalation, and the effect passes. Residual volume rises permanently only with age, as elastic tissue is lost. See [[Lung Volumes and Capacities – Spirometry, FEV1 and FVC#2. The air that never leaves residual volume]].

## Forward airflow velocity falls sharply in the terminal bronchioles because

- [ ] the airways narrow
- [x] the total cross-sectional area of the airways increases greatly
- [ ] flow becomes turbulent
- [ ] the air is warmed

> The same flow spread across a far larger total area moves more slowly, laminar flow is maintained, and in the distal lung the air moves by diffusion rather than convection. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#4. Why air slows down deep in the lung]].

## Compared with men, women have

- [ ] larger airway diameters
- [ ] a larger diffusion surface
- [x] a smaller lung size and airway diameter, leading to expiratory flow limitation
- [ ] higher static lung volumes

> Reduced lung size, airway diameter and diffusion surface mean expiratory flow limitation, greater respiratory muscle work and use of the ventilatory reserve during maximal exercise, especially in highly trained women, which can impair alveolar-to-arterial oxygen exchange compared with male athletes. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#5. What training changes in the lung, and why women reach their limit sooner]].

## The conducting zone consists of

- [x] the trachea, bronchi and bronchioles
- [ ] the alveolar ducts and alveoli
- [ ] the alveoli only
- [ ] the nose and pharynx only

> These airways transport, humidify, warm and filter the air, allow vocalisation and secrete immunoglobulin, but exchange no gas, which is why their volume is the anatomic dead space of about 150 mL. The transitional and respiratory zones (bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveoli) exchange gas, make surfactant and have endocrine and innate immune functions. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#3. The two zones of the airways]].

## An average adult's lungs weigh about

- [ ] 100 g
- [ ] 5 kg
- [ ] 4–6 kg
- [x] 1 kg

> About 1 kg, with a volume of 4–6 L; only 10% of the lung is solid tissue, the rest being air and blood. 4–6 is the volume in litres, not the mass in kilograms. See [[The Lungs and the Breathing Pump – Alveoli, Airways, Muscles, Surfactant and the Valsalva Manoeuvre#1. What the lungs are for]].

## Pulmonary function tests in Olympic middle-distance runners compared with healthy untrained subjects show

- [ ] a much larger FVC and TLC
- [x] few differences
- [ ] a lower FEV1/FVC ratio
- [ ] a doubled maximum voluntary ventilation

> Regular endurance exercise does not stimulate large increases in lung functional capacity; genetic factors are the major contributor, and lung function tests do not separate highly trained athletes from normal subjects (many NFL players have below-average FVCs). The tests are still useful for spotting illness in athletes. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#5. What training changes in the lung, and why women reach their limit sooner]].

## In light exercise the ventilation–perfusion ratio

- [ ] falls to about 0.5
- [ ] rises above 5.0
- [x] stays at about 0.8
- [ ] falls to zero

> Alveolar ventilation and pulmonary blood flow rise together in light exercise, keeping the ratio near its resting value of 0.84; only in intense exercise does ventilation outstrip perfusion and the ratio rise above 5.0. See [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt#5. Matching air to blood the ventilation–perfusion ratio]].
