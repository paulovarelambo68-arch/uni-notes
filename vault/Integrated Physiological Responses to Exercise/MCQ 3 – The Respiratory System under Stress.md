## For an elite endurance athlete ventilating 150 L/min or more, the oxygen cost of breathing

- [ ] is 3–5% of VO2
- [x] is 15% of VO2 or more
- [ ] is 40% of VO2
- [ ] is negligible

> At such ventilations the respiratory muscles compete with the locomotor muscles for oxygen and blood flow, and the cost of exercise hyperpnoea (the increased volume of air breathed) encroaches on the O2 available to the working limbs. ## Hyperventilation during strenuous effort

- [ ] raises alveolar PCO2 and PO2
- [ ] lowers alveolar PCO2 and PO2
- [ ] raises alveolar PCO2, as more CO2 is produced in strenuous effort
- [x] lowers alveolar PCO2 and slightly raises alveolar PO2

> Healthy people hyperventilate at the higher levels of VO2, washing CO2 out of the alveoli and pushing alveolar PO2 a little above 100 mmHg; a breathing reserve still exists because VE at VO2max is only 60–85% of MVV. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#3. The breathing reserve – why ventilation does not limit healthy people]].

## The bicarbonate buffer system consists of

- [x] carbonic acid and sodium bicarbonate
- [ ] phosphoric acid and sodium phosphate
- [ ] haemoglobin and amino acids
- [ ] lactic acid and hydrochloric acid

> A buffer is a weak acid and its conjugate base. In acidosis the extra H+ combines with bicarbonate to form carbonic acid, which becomes CO2 and water; the rise in CO2 and H+ stimulates ventilation so that the CO2 is blown off. Phosphoric acid and sodium phosphate make the phosphate buffer. See [[Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney#3. The bicarbonate buffer and how breathing removes acid]].

## A plasma pH of 7.0 or below during maximal exercise can cause

- [ ] no symptoms
- [ ] improved performance
- [x] nausea, headache and dizziness
- [ ] alkalosis

> Humans tolerate pronounced acid–base disturbance only temporarily; repeated all-out bouts that raise blood lactate to 30 mM or more make pH regulation very difficult. A pH of 7.0 is acidosis, not alkalosis. See [[Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney#6. Blood pH and lactate in all-out exercise]].

## On arrival at high altitude, ventilation keeps increasing for about the first

- [ ] 2 days
- [x] 2 weeks
- [ ] 2 months
- [ ] 2 hours

> Hypoxia drives the hyperventilation; the kidneys then excrete bicarbonate to correct the resulting alkalosis, and as PaCO2 falls the central and peripheral sensitivity for ventilation increases, allowing even greater hyperventilation. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#3. The first days the acute response]].

## The recommended time to adjust to altitudes up to 2,300 m is

- [ ] 2 days
- [ ] 1 week
- [ ] 1 month
- [x] 2 weeks

> Two weeks up to 2,300 m, plus an additional week for each 610 m up to 4,600 m; with a slow ascent, 5,500 m can be reached with few symptoms, whereas rapid ascent brings acute mountain sickness within a few hours. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#5. Time, and mountain sickness]].

## Cheyne-Stokes breathing, seen in acute mountain sickness, is

- [x] alternating fast and slow breathing
- [ ] a permanent rise in breathing rate
- [ ] breath-holding during sleep
- [ ] shallow rapid breathing only

> It accompanies the headache, nausea, irritability, weakness, poor appetite, vomiting and tachycardia of AMS, which is common above 3,000 m and more likely in people with a blunted breathing response. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#5. Time, and mountain sickness]].

## In live high–train low, athletes live at

- [ ] 500–1,000 m
- [ ] 5,000–6,000 m
- [x] 2,200–3,500 m
- [ ] 8,000 m

> That altitude stimulates erythropoiesis without causing AMS, while driving down to train below 1,200 m keeps the high-intensity stimulus; alternatively the athlete sleeps in a hypoxic tent (about 14% O2, PiO2 106 mmHg). In the 1997 study VO2max, running endurance and 3,000 m performance improved, but only in the "responders" whose EPO rose. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#7. Altitude training]].

## Cigarette smoking blunts

- [ ] the ventilatory response to CO2
- [x] the exercise heart rate response
- [ ] the rise in lactate
- [ ] the Bohr effect

> Because the heart rate does not rise as fast, a smoker can look fit on a heart-rate test; after one day of abstinence the heart rate is 5–7% lower. Smoking also acutely and chronically reduces ciliary motility, and the CO in smoke blocks O2 binding to haemoglobin. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#6. Smoking – acute effects of one cigarette and chronic damage]].

## Breathing cold air during strenuous exercise

- [ ] freezes and damages the respiratory passages
- [ ] lowers resting VO2
- [ ] reduces water loss from the airways
- [x] loses considerable water and heat from the respiratory tract

> Cold air is warmed so quickly that it does not damage the passages, but warming greatly increases its capacity to hold moisture, so the airways lose water and heat; the fluid loss contributes to dehydration, dry mouth, a burning throat and general irritation of the passages. See [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold#7. Cold air – oxygen uptake, ventilation, airway water loss and endurance]].
