Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## Which of the following best describes the SAID (specific adaptations to imposed demands) principle?

- [x] Specific exercise elicits specific adaptations that produce specific performance improvements
- [ ] Training any muscle group improves performance in all activities
- [ ] Adaptations are independent of the mode of overload imposed
- [ ] General fitness transfers equally to every sport

> Adaptations in metabolic and physiologic function depend on the type and mode of overload imposed, so the training effect, and the performance gain, are specific to the exercise performed. See Training & Detraining, slide 5 (Specificity Principle).

## A group completes a period of swim training and is then tested both on a treadmill and by tethered swimming. According to the lecture, which measure shows the greatest percentage improvement?

- [ ] Treadmill VO₂max
- [ ] Maximal run time
- [x] Maximal swim time
- [ ] Tethered-swim VO₂max

> Improvement was greatest when the test duplicated the training: maximal swim time rose about 34% and swim VO₂max about 11%, against 4.6% for run time and 1.5% for treadmill VO₂max. Overload for a specific aerobic activity must engage the muscles that activity uses and still stress the cardiovascular system. See Training & Detraining, slide 6 (Maximum Oxygen Uptake Specificity).

## Aerobic training increases total blood volume through an increase in:

- [ ] Plasma volume only, which dilutes the red cells
- [x] Plasma volume and red blood cell mass
- [ ] Haematocrit only
- [ ] Venous compliance

> Both plasma volume and red cell mass rise, so total blood volume rises, improving thermoregulation and O₂-carrying capacity and raising venous return and end-diastolic volume. See Training & Detraining, slide 14 (Aerobic Training Increases Oxygen Delivery to Active Muscles) and slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training: ↑ blood volume).

## Which breathing-pattern adaptation occurs with endurance training?

- [ ] Increased breathing frequency and decreased tidal volume
- [ ] Increases in both tidal volume and breathing frequency at rest
- [ ] Decreases in both tidal volume and breathing frequency during exercise
- [x] Increased tidal volume and decreased breathing frequency

> The trained person breathes more deeply and less often, a more efficient pattern that causes less respiratory fatigue; together with better respiratory muscle endurance it lowers the ventilatory equivalent for O₂. See Training & Detraining, slide 16 (Respiratory adaptations to endurance training).

## Compared with simply taking 70% of HRmax, the Karvonen method:

- [x] Gives a higher training threshold heart rate
- [ ] Gives a lower training threshold heart rate
- [ ] Gives the same threshold heart rate
- [ ] Does not require an estimate of HRmax, only the resting heart rate

> HRthreshold = HRrest + 0.70 (HRmax − HRrest) adds 70% of the heart-rate reserve to the resting rate, which always lands above 70% of HRmax (for HRmax 190 and HRrest 60: 151 versus 133 beats/min). See Training & Detraining, slide 23 (Determining the "Training-Sensitive Zone").

## The training frequency and duration needed to maintain an improved aerobic fitness are:

- [ ] The same as those needed to improve it
- [ ] Higher than those needed to improve it
- [ ] Unrelated to those needed to improve it
- [x] Lower than those needed to improve it

> Once fitness has improved, less frequent and shorter training keeps it, which is why athletes can hold their fitness with a moderate, sport-specific training load in the off-season. See Training & Detraining, slide 28 (Training: Frequency & Mode) and slide 8 (Reversibility Principle).

## At higher exercise intensities, tidal volume:

- [ ] Continues to rise in proportion to VO₂
- [ ] Falls sharply to resting values
- [x] Plateaus or falls slightly
- [ ] Rises while breathing frequency falls

> Tidal volume carries the rise in ventilation through light and moderate work, then levels off (or drops slightly) near the upper domains, where breathing frequency climbs steeply and minute ventilation rises sharply. See Integrated Response, slide 7 (Ventilation Vt vs BF shift to increase ventilation) and slide 6 (Ventilation must match the metabolic challenge).

## In the reaction H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ CO₂ + H₂O, the buffering of metabolic H⁺ by bicarbonate during high-intensity exercise results in:

- [ ] A fall in CO₂ output
- [x] Extra CO₂ that is exhaled
- [ ] Increased lactate production
- [ ] A fall in minute ventilation

> Buffering converts bicarbonate and H⁺ into carbonic acid, which releases CO₂ beyond what the mitochondria produce; this extra CO₂ drives ventilation up and is breathed out, which is why pulmonary VCO₂ is not a direct read-out of mitochondrial CO₂ production. See Integrated Response, slide 13 (The crucial link: H⁺ buffering creates additional CO₂) and slide 5 (Cellular metabolism sets the gas-exchange demand).

## In COPD, exercise tolerance is constrained mainly by:

- [x] Ventilatory limitation, dynamic hyperinflation and increased work of breathing
- [ ] Reduced cardiac output and altered peripheral extraction
- [ ] Perfusion–ventilation mismatch and impaired pulmonary vascular reserve
- [ ] Higher metabolic stress for a given external workload

> Each condition breaks the respiratory–metabolic axis at a different point: COPD limits ventilation itself; reduced cardiac output with altered extraction and poor ventilatory efficiency is heart failure; perfusion–ventilation mismatch is pulmonary vascular disease; and higher metabolic and ventilatory stress for a given workload is deconditioning. See Integrated Response, slide 17 (Why the axis matters?).

## VO₂max is defined in the lecture as:

- [ ] The oxygen uptake at the lactate threshold
- [x] The most oxygen a person can use in exhausting exercise
- [ ] The highest minute ventilation reached during exercise
- [ ] The oxygen uptake at the ventilatory threshold

> VO₂max, the largest amount of oxygen an individual can utilise during strenuous exercise to complete exhaustion, is the most commonly used index of cardiorespiratory endurance and its accepted measure. The oxygen uptakes at the lactate and ventilatory thresholds are submaximal landmarks reached well before exhaustion. See Integrated Response, slide 22 (Cardiorespiratory endurance is measured by: VO₂max).
