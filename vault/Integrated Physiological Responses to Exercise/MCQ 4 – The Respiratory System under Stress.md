Ten multiple-choice questions on the altitude lecture: the oxygen fraction at altitude, inspired PO2, the limit of habitation, where VO2max starts to fall, event duration, EPO, the long-term blood changes, the lactate paradox, altitude training and acute mountain sickness. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the note and section that cover it.

## At altitude, the percentage of oxygen in the air

- [ ] falls to about 15%
- [ ] falls to about 10%
- [x] stays at about 21%, but there is less O2 in a given volume of air
- [ ] rises slightly

> The fraction is unchanged; it is the barometric pressure that falls, so the same 21% gives a lower PO2 and fewer O2 molecules per breath. At 3,000 m the pressure is 526 mmHg and the PO2 110 mmHg, against 760 and 159 mmHg at sea level. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#1. Less oxygen in every breath]].

## The PO2 of inspired air is calculated as

- [x] 0.2093 x barometric pressure
- [ ] barometric pressure – 47 mmHg
- [ ] 0.79 x barometric pressure
- [ ] 760 mmHg x altitude

> Oxygen is 20.93% of the air, so its partial pressure is that fraction of whatever the barometric pressure is: 0.2093 x 760 = 159 mmHg at sea level, and 0.2093 x 235 = 49 mmHg on Everest. 0.79 is the nitrogen fraction. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#1. Less oxygen in every breath]].

## The upper limit of permanent human habitation is about

- [ ] 1,500 m
- [ ] 3,000 m
- [ ] 8,850 m
- [x] 5,820 m

> 5,820 m (about 19,000 ft) is the highest permanent settlement; above 5,500 m is extreme altitude, and above 6,000 m the body deteriorates progressively. 8,850 m is the summit of Everest. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#1. Less oxygen in every breath]].

## In most people, decreases in VO2max begin above an altitude of

- [ ] 580 m
- [x] 1,524 m (5,000 ft)
- [ ] 3,000 m
- [ ] 5,500 m

> Moderate altitude begins at 1,524 m, higher than any Irish mountain (Carrauntoohil is 1,038 m), but elite athletes may lose VO2max from as low as 580 m. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#1. Less oxygen in every breath]].

## Altitude has no adverse effect on events lasting

- [ ] 20 minutes or more
- [ ] 2 to 5 minutes
- [x] 2 minutes or less
- [ ] any duration

> Short events are anaerobic and unaffected. The threshold for decrements appears at 1,600 m for events of 2–5 minutes, and only 600–700 m already impairs events of 20 minutes or more. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#6. Exercising up there]].

## Erythropoietin is produced by the

- [x] kidneys
- [ ] bone marrow
- [ ] liver
- [ ] spleen

> The kidneys release EPO in response to hypoxia, from altitude or blood loss, and EPO then initiates red cell formation in the bone marrow, raising red cell number (normally 4.7–6.0 million/µL in men, 4.2–5.4 in women) and haemoglobin synthesis. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#4. The first weeks acclimatisation]].

## Longer-term haematological acclimatisation to altitude includes

- [ ] an increase in plasma volume
- [ ] a fall in haematocrit
- [ ] fewer red blood cells
- [x] a fall in plasma volume with a rise in haematocrit, haemoglobin and red cell number

> Plasma volume shrinks and red cells multiply, so haematocrit and haemoglobin concentration rise; locally the muscle may gain capillaries, 2,3-DPG, mitochondria and aerobic enzymes, while body weight and lean mass are lost. The first two options are the opposite of what happens. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#4. The first weeks acclimatisation]].

## The lactate paradox is

- [ ] a rise in lactate at altitude despite better oxygenation
- [x] a fall in lactate accumulation at altitude without any rise in tissue oxygenation
- [ ] the buffering of lactate by bicarbonate
- [ ] a rise in lactate threshold with training

> On arrival at altitude, submaximal exercise produces more lactate than at sea level because of greater reliance on anaerobic glycolysis; with acclimatisation the accumulation falls even though the tissues are no better oxygenated, while the loss of alkaline reserve means the blood's capacity to buffer acid gradually decreases. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#4. The first weeks acclimatisation]].

## Sea-level exercise performance after living at altitude, judged by VO2max,

- [ ] improves by about 10%
- [ ] improves in every athlete
- [x] does not improve
- [ ] improves only in sprinters

> Muscle mass loss and the reduced maximum heart rate and stroke volume persist, and any fall in maximum cardiac output offsets the gain in the blood's O2-carrying capacity. This is why the live high–train low strategy was devised. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#7. Altitude training]].

## Acute mountain sickness is common above

- [x] 3,000 m
- [ ] 1,000 m
- [ ] 5,500 m
- [ ] 8,000 m

> Above 3,000 m AMS is common, especially in people with a blunted breathing response; slow ascent and acclimatisation hikes reduce the risk. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#5. Time, and mountain sickness]].
