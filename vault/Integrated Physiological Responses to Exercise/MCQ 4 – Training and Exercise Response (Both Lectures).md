Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## Minute ventilation (VE) is calculated as:

- [ ] Tidal volume ÷ breathing frequency
- [ ] VO₂ × VCO₂
- [x] Tidal volume × breathing frequency
- [ ] Cardiac output × (CaO₂ − CvO₂)

> VE = Vᴛ × f, the total volume of air moved per minute; cardiac output × (CaO₂ − CvO₂) is the Fick equation for VO₂, not for ventilation. See Integrated Response, slide 6 (Ventilation must match the metabolic challenge).

## The rapid rise in ventilation at the very onset of exercise is attributed mainly to:

- [ ] Central chemoreceptor sensing of CO₂
- [ ] Peripheral chemoreceptor sensing of a fall in O₂
- [ ] CO₂ released by lactate buffering
- [x] Neural feed-forward from motor command and sensory feedback

> Motor command and sensory feedback contribute particularly at exercise onset, before the chemical signals have changed; during exercise, ventilation reflects both this immediate neural drive and chemical feedback from the chemoreceptors. Lactate buffering matters at high intensity, not at the start. See Integrated Response, slide 8 (What tells the brain to increase ventilation?).

## An RER greater than 1.0 during heavy exercise is best explained by:

- [x] Extra CO₂ from bicarbonate buffering
- [ ] 100% carbohydrate oxidation, which releases more CO₂ than the O₂ it uses
- [ ] A shift towards fat oxidation
- [ ] A fall in ventilation

> Metabolic RER cannot exceed 1.0, the value for pure carbohydrate; above 1.0 the extra CO₂ is non-metabolic, released when bicarbonate buffers the H⁺ of lactic acid and blown off by the rising ventilation. This is why the lecture's true/false statement "RER > 1 means 100% carbohydrate" is false. See Integrated Response, slide 13 (The crucial link: H⁺ buffering creates additional CO₂), slide 12 (Respiratory Exchange Ratio (RER)) and slide 19 (True/False).

## Which of the following determines arterial O₂ content (CaO₂)?

- [x] Haemoglobin and its O₂ saturation
- [ ] Mitochondrial O₂ use
- [ ] Muscle blood flow and capillary density
- [ ] Stroke volume

> CaO₂ depends on haemoglobin, its O₂ saturation and the small amount of dissolved O₂. Muscle blood flow, capillary density and mitochondrial O₂ use are the peripheral extraction side of the Fick equation, and stroke volume belongs to cardiac output. See Integrated Response, slide 16 (VO₂ is constrained by delivery and extraction).

## VO₂max values range from about ___ in a sedentary person with disease to about ___ in a young endurance runner:

- [ ] 5 and 35 mL/kg/min
- [x] 15 and 75 mL/kg/min
- [ ] 30 and 100 mL/kg/min
- [ ] 40 and 60 mL/kg/min

> Expressed relative to body weight, VO₂max runs from about 15 mL/kg/min in sedentary patients with disease to about 75 mL/kg/min in young endurance runners, with women about 10–20% lower than men. See Integrated Response, slide 26 (VO₂ max: units and range).

## The heart-rate criterion for accepting a VO₂max requires that the heart rate during the last stage is:

- [ ] Equal to the age-predicted HRmax
- [x] No more than 10 beats/min below the age-predicted HRmax
- [ ] No more than 20 beats/min below the age-predicted HRmax
- [ ] Above 200 beats/min

> A heart rate within 10 beats/min of the age-predicted HRmax is one of the four criteria, with a VO₂ plateau (rise of less than 150 mL/min), RER greater than 1.10 and RPE greater than 17; any two of the four are enough. See Integrated Response, slide 29 (criteria for VO₂max).

## According to the reversibility principle, measurable reductions in metabolic and exercise capacity appear after how long without training?

- [x] Only 1 to 2 weeks
- [ ] 4 to 6 weeks
- [ ] 2 to 3 months
- [ ] About 6 months

> Detraining starts rapidly: only 1 or 2 weeks reduces both metabolic and exercise capacity, and many training improvements are fully lost within several months. See Training & Detraining, slide 8 (Reversibility Principle) and slide 32 (Detraining (Reversal of Adaptations)).

## Endurance-trained skeletal muscle fibres differ from less active fibres in that they contain:

- [ ] Fewer but larger mitochondria
- [ ] More numerous but smaller mitochondria
- [x] Larger and more numerous mitochondria
- [ ] The same mitochondria with more glycolytic enzymes

> Mitochondria grow in both size and number, improving the capacity for respiratory control in skeletal muscle and the aerobic generation of ATP; more glycolytic enzymes is an anaerobic-training adaptation. See Training & Detraining, slide 12 (Metabolic Adaptations).

## Endurance training lowers the blood lactate concentration during exercise of increasing intensity by:

- [ ] Decreasing the rate of lactate formation only
- [ ] Increasing the rate of lactate clearance only
- [x] Both decreasing lactate formation and increasing lactate clearance
- [ ] Abolishing anaerobic glycolysis

> The combined effect of less lactate being formed and more being removed during activity flattens the lactate curve and moves the onset of blood lactate accumulation (OBLA) to a higher oxygen uptake. See Training & Detraining, slide 17 (Blood Lactate Concentration Adaptations).

## Over the first 4 to 6 weeks of a programme, most individuals tolerate an increase in session duration of:

- [ ] 1 to 2 min every week
- [x] 5 to 10 min every 1 to 2 weeks
- [ ] 15 to 20 min every week
- [ ] 30 min every 2 weeks

> Progression means raising intensity, duration and frequency gradually; a 5- to 10-min increase in the session every 1 to 2 weeks over the first 4 to 6 weeks is what most people tolerate, and gradually increasing or varying intensity enhances the response. See Training & Detraining, slide 29 (Training: Progression).
