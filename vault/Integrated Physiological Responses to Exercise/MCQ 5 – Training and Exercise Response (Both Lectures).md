Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## Appropriate overload is achieved by manipulating which combination of training variables?

- [ ] Mode, intensity and specificity
- [ ] Volume, progression and reversibility
- [ ] Intensity, heredity and initial fitness
- [x] Frequency, intensity and duration

> Overload requires manipulating training frequency, intensity and duration, singly or in combination, and individualised, progressive overload applies to athletes, sedentary persons, disabled persons and cardiac patients alike. See Training & Detraining, slide 4 (Overload Principle).

## Which of the following is listed under "central blood flow" as a physiologic factor that limits VO₂max?

- [x] Cardiac output (heart rate and stroke volume)
- [ ] Muscle capillary density
- [ ] Mitochondrial size and number
- [ ] Oxygen diffusion capacity

> Central blood flow covers cardiac output, arterial blood pressure and oxygen transport capacity (haemoglobin). Capillary density is a peripheral blood-flow factor, mitochondria belong to active muscle metabolism, and diffusion capacity to ventilation–aeration. See Training & Detraining, slide 11 (How Training Impacts the Aerobic System: physiologic factors that limit VO₂max).

## The lower resting and submaximal heart rate after endurance training is attributed to:

- [x] More vagal tone and less sympathetic drive
- [ ] Increased sympathetic activity
- [ ] A smaller stroke volume
- [ ] A reduced blood volume that lowers the heart's workload

> A stronger vagal (parasympathetic) brake and less sympathetic drive slow the heart at rest and at any submaximal load, improving endurance and heart-rate recovery; stroke volume and blood volume both increase. See Training & Detraining, slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training: ↓ resting and submaximal HR).

## Aerobic fitness improvements with endurance training typically range between:

- [ ] 1 and 5%
- [x] 5 and 25%
- [ ] 25 and 50%
- [ ] 50 and 100%

> Improvements of 5–25% are typical, with some improvement already in the first training week; where in that range a person falls depends mainly on initial fitness. See Training & Detraining, slide 20 (Initial Aerobic Fitness Level).

## Using the Karvonen method, the training threshold heart rate for a person with an HRmax of 190 and a resting heart rate of 60 beats/min is approximately:

- [ ] 121 beats/min
- [ ] 133 beats/min
- [x] 151 beats/min
- [ ] 165 beats/min

> HRthreshold = HRrest + 0.70 (HRmax − HRrest) = 60 + 0.70 × 130 = 151 beats/min. 133 is simply 70% of HRmax, the lower value the Karvonen method deliberately exceeds. See Training & Detraining, slide 23 (Determining the "Training-Sensitive Zone").

## An increased heart rate at a given submaximal workload and a loss of capillarisation are detraining changes typical of:

- [ ] The first 1 to 2 weeks
- [x] 2 to 4 weeks
- [ ] More than 4 weeks
- [ ] Only after 6 months

> In the first 1–2 weeks plasma volume falls, lowering stroke volume and VO₂max; by 2–4 weeks submaximal heart rate is higher and capillarisation and respiratory muscle endurance decline, so fatigue comes sooner; beyond 4 weeks LV mass and mitochondrial enzymes fall and VO₂max drops by up to 20%. See Training & Detraining, slide 32 (Detraining (Reversal of Adaptations)).

## The peripheral chemoreceptors that help drive ventilation are located in the:

- [ ] Medulla oblongata
- [ ] Walls of the alveoli
- [x] Carotid and aortic bodies
- [ ] Diaphragm and intercostal muscles

> The carotid and aortic bodies respond to changes in O₂, CO₂ and H⁺; the central chemoreceptors in the CNS respond primarily to CO₂-related changes in H⁺, and the respiratory muscles are the effectors driven by the brainstem. See Integrated Response, slide 8 (What tells the brain to increase ventilation?).

## The RER at rest is usually about:

- [ ] 0.70
- [x] 0.80
- [ ] 0.90
- [ ] 1.00

> A resting RER of 0.78–0.80 reflects a mixture of fat and carbohydrate oxidation; it rises towards 1.0 as exercise intensity increases and more carbohydrate is used. See Integrated Response, slide 12 (Respiratory Exchange Ratio (RER)).

## Person A has a VO₂ of 2.0 L/min and a VCO₂ of 1.8 L/min. Their RER is:

- [ ] 0.80
- [x] 0.90
- [ ] 1.05
- [ ] 1.11

> RER = VCO₂/VO₂ = 1.8/2.0 = 0.90, a mixed-fuel value below the lactate threshold. Person B in the same example (VCO₂ 2.1 L/min at the same VO₂) has an RER of 1.05, and 1.11 is the ratio the wrong way up. See Integrated Response, slide 18 (Examples).

## A standard VO₂max protocol is best described as:

- [ ] A single constant-load bout to exhaustion
- [ ] A 30-s all-out sprint
- [ ] A submaximal steady-state test at 70% HRmax
- [x] A graded incremental test to exhaustion

> The load starts low and rises in equal steps every 1–3 min until the subject can continue no longer, the test itself lasting 15–30 min, preceded by a 5–10 min low-intensity warm-up and followed by a 5–10 min cool-down. See Integrated Response, slide 28 (Measurement of VO₂max).
