Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## In the respiratory–metabolic axis, which step directly follows the rise in muscle VO₂ and VCO₂?

- [ ] Ventilation
- [ ] Ventilatory drive (CO₂ and H⁺ sensing)
- [ ] Exercise demand
- [x] Acid–base changes

> The chain runs: exercise demand (↑ ATP demand) → muscle metabolism (↑ VO₂, ↑ VCO₂) → acid–base (↑ H⁺, HCO₃⁻ buffering) → ventilatory drive (CO₂ and H⁺ sensing) → ventilation (CO₂ elimination, return to homeostasis). See Integrated Response, slide 4 (The respiratory–metabolic axis).

## If oxygen delivery to the working muscle is insufficient for the energy demand, which of the following occurs?

- [x] Anaerobic metabolism produces ATP and lactic acid
- [ ] ATP production stops
- [ ] ATP is produced only from fat
- [ ] VO₂ rises to meet the demand regardless of delivery

> When the lungs–heart–muscle oxygen transport chain cannot supply enough O₂, the muscle makes ATP anaerobically and lactic acid accumulates; fat can only be used aerobically. See Integrated Response, slide 11 (Role of oxygen cont.) and slide 10 (Role of oxygen transport).

## The ventilatory threshold relates directly to:

- [ ] The increased O₂ demand of the respiratory muscles
- [ ] A fall in arterial PO₂
- [x] Extra CO₂ from the buffering of lactate
- [ ] The rise in body temperature

> Lactate + NaHCO₃ → Na lactate + H₂CO₃ → H₂O + CO₂: the CO₂ released when bicarbonate buffers the lactate accumulating from anaerobic metabolism adds to the metabolic CO₂ and makes ventilation rise out of proportion to VO₂. See Integrated Response, slide 15 (Ventilation During Non-Steady-Rate PA: Ventilatory Threshold (VT)).

## Perfusion–ventilation mismatch and impaired pulmonary vascular reserve altering the CPET response are characteristic of:

- [ ] COPD
- [ ] Heart failure
- [ ] Deconditioning
- [x] Pulmonary vascular disease

> Pulmonary vascular disease limits the lung's vascular reserve and mismatches perfusion to ventilation. COPD is ventilatory limitation with dynamic hyperinflation, heart failure is reduced cardiac output with altered extraction and ventilatory efficiency, and deconditioning is a higher metabolic and ventilatory stress for the same workload. See Integrated Response, slide 17 (Why the axis matters?).

## Compared with men, VO₂max in women is typically:

- [ ] About the same
- [x] About 10 to 20% lower
- [ ] About 30 to 40% lower
- [ ] About 10% higher

> Women average about 10–20% lower; gender is one of the factors that affect VO₂max, with mode of exercise, heredity, state of training, body size and composition, and age. See Integrated Response, slide 26 (VO₂ max) and slide 27 (Factors that affect VO₂max).

## Which RER in the final work stage satisfies one of the criteria for VO₂max?

- [ ] Greater than 0.90
- [ ] Greater than 1.00
- [x] Greater than 1.10
- [ ] Greater than 1.30

> An RER above 1.10 shows that the subject is well beyond the point of lactate buffering and hyperventilation; it counts with the VO₂ plateau, a heart rate within 10 beats/min of the age-predicted HRmax and RPE greater than 17, any two of which confirm a maximal effort. See Integrated Response, slide 29 (criteria for VO₂max).

## A sprint or a fast break lasting up to about 10 s relies predominantly on:

- [ ] ATP alone (strength–power)
- [x] ATP + PCr (sustained power)
- [ ] ATP + PCr + lactic acid (anaerobic power–endurance)
- [ ] Electron transport–oxidative phosphorylation (aerobic endurance)

> Sustained-power efforts of a few seconds up to about 10 s (sprints, fast breaks, football line play, a gymnastics routine) run on the phosphagens; ATP alone covers only the first few seconds, lactic acid joins in efforts of up to about 1.5 min, and the aerobic system dominates beyond about 3 min. See Training & Detraining, slide 5 (Specificity Principle: classification of physical activity based on duration of all-out effort).

## After aerobic training, during 2 hours of exercise at the same submaximal intensity, which of the following occurs?

- [ ] Total energy expenditure increases
- [ ] Energy from carbohydrate increases
- [x] Energy from lipid increases and energy from carbohydrate decreases
- [ ] Energy from both carbohydrate and lipid decreases

> Total energy expenditure for the same work is about the same before and after training, but the trained muscle, with its greater mitochondrial capacity, oxidises more fat and spares carbohydrate. See Training & Detraining, slide 12 (Metabolic Adaptations).

## The increase in skeletal muscle capillary density with endurance training occurs through:

- [ ] Vasodilation of existing capillaries
- [ ] Eccentric hypertrophy
- [ ] An increase in plasma volume
- [x] Angiogenesis

> New capillaries grow in the trained muscle, enhancing O₂ diffusion and nutrient exchange; eccentric hypertrophy is the cardiac adaptation and the larger plasma volume raises blood volume, not capillary number. See Training & Detraining, slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training: ↑ capillary density).

## The weekly energy expenditure that the lecture associates with a lower rate of cardiovascular disease and premature mortality is:

- [ ] 100 to 300 METs per week
- [x] 500 to 1000 METs per week
- [ ] 2000 to 3000 METs per week
- [ ] 50 to 100 kcal per week

> Exercise volume is gross energy expenditure in total kcal or METs, and 500–1000 METs per week consistently associates with lower cardiovascular disease and premature mortality; about 150 min per week of moderate activity approximates 1000 kcal. See Training & Detraining, slide 27 (Training: Duration & Volume).
