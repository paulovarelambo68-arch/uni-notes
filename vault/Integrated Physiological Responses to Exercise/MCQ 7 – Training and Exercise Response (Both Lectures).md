Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## To slow the effects of detraining, most athletes:

- [x] Maintain a moderate level of off-season, sport-specific training
- [ ] Stop all training during the off-season
- [ ] Train at maximal intensity throughout the off-season
- [ ] Switch to a different sport in the off-season

> Even in highly trained athletes the benefits of prior training are transient and reversible, so most begin reconditioning several months before the competitive season or keep a moderate, sport-specific training load in the off-season. See Training & Detraining, slide 8 (Reversibility Principle).

## Muscle capillary density and O₂ extraction are classified in the lecture as:

- [ ] Ventilation–aeration factors
- [x] Peripheral blood flow factors
- [ ] Central blood flow factors
- [ ] Active muscle metabolism factors

> The peripheral blood-flow panel covers flow to non-active regions, arterial vascular reactivity, muscle blood flow, capillary density, O₂ diffusion, vascular conductance, O₂ extraction, Hb–O₂ affinity and venous compliance; active muscle metabolism covers enzymes, energy stores, myoglobin, mitochondria, muscle mass and fibre type. See Training & Detraining, slide 11 (How Training Impacts the Aerobic System).

## Improved endurance of the respiratory muscles after endurance training results in:

- [ ] A higher ventilatory equivalent for O₂
- [ ] A higher breathing frequency at a given workload
- [ ] A greater O₂ cost of breathing
- [x] A lower ventilatory equivalent for O₂

> Less ventilation is needed for each litre of O₂ taken up, so VE/VO₂ falls; the stronger, more fatigue-resistant diaphragm and intercostals also delay fatigue during prolonged exercise. See Training & Detraining, slide 16 (Respiratory adaptations to endurance training).

## Which of the following is listed among the seven factors affecting the aerobic training response?

- [ ] Heredity
- [ ] Gender
- [ ] Body size and composition
- [x] Initial aerobic fitness level

> The seven are initial aerobic fitness level, training intensity, duration, volume, frequency, mode and progression. Heredity, gender and body size and composition are factors that affect VO₂max itself, listed in the other lecture. See Training & Detraining, slide 19 (Seven Factors Affecting Aerobic Training Responses) and Integrated Response, slide 27 (Factors that affect VO₂max).

## On the original Borg RPE scale, a rating of 15 ("hard") corresponds approximately to:

- [ ] 52–66% HRmax and 31–50% VO₂max
- [ ] 61–85% HRmax and 51–75% VO₂max
- [x] 86–91% HRmax and 76–85% VO₂max
- [ ] 92% HRmax and 85% VO₂max

> On the scale's table, 11 ("fairly light") is 52–66% HRmax and 31–50% VO₂max, 13 ("somewhat hard") 61–85% and 51–75%, 15 ("hard") 86–91% and 76–85%, and 17 ("very hard") 92% and 85%. See Training & Detraining, slide 24 (Training Intensity: Rating of Perceived Exertion).

## In the summary figure of adaptations over 24 months of training, which showed the greatest relative increase?

- [ ] VO₂max
- [x] Aerobic enzymes
- [ ] Capillaries
- [ ] Slow-twitch fibre cross-sectional size

> Aerobic enzymes rise to more than twice the control value, ahead of fast-twitch fibre oxidative potential, glycogen, capillaries and VO₂max; the cross-sectional size of the slow-twitch fibres changes least. See Training & Detraining, slide 34 (Summary of Training Adaptations).

## During a graded exercise test, which of the following occurs at the highest oxygen uptake?

- [ ] The lactate threshold
- [ ] The ventilatory threshold
- [x] Respiratory compensation
- [ ] The point of OBLA

> In the figure the lactate threshold (the first rise in lactate) and the ventilatory threshold come first, OBLA (4 mM) at a higher oxygen uptake, and the respiratory compensation point, where ventilation steepens a second time, last. See Integrated Response, slide 14 (Pulmonary Ventilation During PA).

## According to the lecture, VO₂max is determined by:

- [x] Both O₂ transport and O₂ utilisation by muscle
- [ ] The lungs alone, since all O₂ must first cross the alveolar membrane
- [ ] Breathing frequency
- [ ] Tidal volume

> Oxygen uptake has a transport side (lungs, blood and heart) and a utilisation side in the muscle (VO₂ = Q × (CaO₂ − CvO₂)); the lungs do not set VO₂max on their own, which is why the true/false statement "the lungs determine VO₂max" is false. See Integrated Response, slide 20 (Cardiorespiratory Endurance), slide 16 (VO₂ is constrained by delivery and extraction) and slide 19 (True/False).

## Which of the following is listed as a factor that affects VO₂max?

- [x] Heredity
- [ ] Training frequency
- [ ] Training progression
- [ ] Session duration

> The factors that affect VO₂max are mode of exercise, heredity, state of training, gender, body size and composition, and age; frequency, progression and duration are among the seven factors that affect the aerobic training response in the other lecture. See Integrated Response, slide 27 (Factors that affect VO₂max) and Training & Detraining, slide 19 (Seven Factors Affecting Aerobic Training Responses).

## In the treadmill protocol shown in the lecture, once the speed has reached 11.2 km/h the workload is increased by:

- [ ] Increasing the speed further
- [x] Raising the treadmill grade
- [ ] Shortening the stages
- [ ] Removing the rest between stages

> After the speed rises from 4.8 to 8.0 to 11.2 km/h, each later 2-min stage keeps 11.2 km/h and raises the grade (5.5, 7.5, 9.5, 11.5, 13.5, 15.5%) until VO₂ plateaus; in the "hills of successively increasing grade" figure the region of VO₂max is where VO₂ no longer rises with a steeper grade. See Integrated Response, slide 28 (Measurement of VO₂max) and slide 30 (McArdle, Katch & Katch figures).
