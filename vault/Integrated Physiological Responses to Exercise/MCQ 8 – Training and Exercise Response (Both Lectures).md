Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## Pulmonary VCO₂ measured during exercise:

- [ ] Is always a direct read-out of mitochondrial CO₂ production
- [x] Is not simply a direct read-out of mitochondrial CO₂ production
- [ ] Equals VO₂ at every intensity
- [ ] Is unaffected by acid–base buffering

> The mitochondria consume O₂ and make CO₂, H₂O and ATP, and the integrated result appears as pulmonary VO₂ and VCO₂; but at high intensity the CO₂ released when bicarbonate buffers H⁺ adds to the exhaled CO₂, so VCO₂ (and RER) can exceed what metabolism alone produces. See Integrated Response, slide 5 (Cellular metabolism sets the gas-exchange demand) and slide 13 (The crucial link: H⁺ buffering creates additional CO₂).

## Peripheral extraction of O₂, in the Fick equation, depends on:

- [ ] Heart rate and stroke volume
- [ ] Haemoglobin and O₂ saturation
- [ ] Tidal volume and breathing frequency
- [x] Muscle blood flow and mitochondrial O₂ use

> The arteriovenous O₂ difference widens when more blood reaches the muscle and the mitochondria use more of the O₂ it carries; HR × SV is cardiac output, and haemoglobin with its saturation sets arterial O₂ content. See Integrated Response, slide 16 (VO₂ is constrained by delivery and extraction).

## The term "anaerobic threshold" originally described:

- [x] An abrupt rise in ventilation
- [ ] The oxygen uptake at which VO₂ plateaus
- [ ] The point at which fat oxidation stops
- [ ] A blood lactate of 4 mM, the onset of blood lactate accumulation

> The anaerobic threshold was originally defined by the abrupt rise in VE caused by the non-metabolic CO₂ of lactate buffering, which is the ventilatory threshold; 4 mM is OBLA and a VO₂ plateau marks VO₂max. See Integrated Response, slide 15 (Ventilation During Non-Steady-Rate PA: Ventilatory Threshold (VT)).

## Which of the following best describes deconditioning in terms of the respiratory–metabolic axis?

- [x] Higher metabolic and ventilatory stress for a given external workload
- [ ] Reduced cardiac output with abnormal ventilatory efficiency
- [ ] Ventilatory limitation with dynamic hyperinflation
- [ ] Impaired pulmonary vascular reserve

> The deconditioned person pays more, metabolically and in ventilation, for the same external work: the same workload is not the same physiological demand. The other options are heart failure, COPD and pulmonary vascular disease. See Integrated Response, slide 17 (Why the axis matters?).

## VO₂max expressed in mL/kg/min is:

- [ ] An absolute value
- [x] A value relative to body weight
- [ ] A value relative to fat-free mass
- [ ] A value relative to cardiac output

> Litres or millilitres per minute is the absolute value, mL/kg/min is relative to body weight, and mL/kg FFM/min is relative to fat-free mass. See Integrated Response, slide 26 (VO₂ max: units).

## The RPE criterion for accepting a VO₂max, on the original Borg scale, is a rating:

- [ ] Greater than 12
- [ ] Greater than 15
- [x] Greater than 17
- [ ] Of exactly 20

> RPE greater than 17 ("very hard" and above) is one of the four criteria, with a VO₂ plateau, a heart rate within 10 beats/min of the predicted HRmax and RER greater than 1.10; two of the four must be met. See Integrated Response, slide 29 (criteria for VO₂max).

## Which of the following best describes the training improvements of highly trained athletes once training stops?

- [ ] They are retained permanently once elite status is reached
- [ ] They are lost in metabolic but not in cardiovascular capacity
- [ ] They are maintained for at least 6 months without training
- [x] They remain transient and reversible

> Even in highly trained athletes the beneficial effects of prior training remain transient and reversible, affecting cardiovascular function, muscle strength and size, and metabolic capacity; this is why the off-season needs maintenance training. See Training & Detraining, slide 8 (Reversibility Principle) and slide 31 (Detraining).

## In the lecture's schematic, the larger end-diastolic volume after aerobic training results from increased venous return together with increased:

- [ ] Heart rate and myocardial contractility
- [ ] Ejection fraction and cardiac output distribution
- [x] Ventricular compliance and internal ventricular dimensions
- [ ] Peripheral resistance and arterial pressure

> A more compliant, larger ventricle fills more from the greater venous return, while the separate rise in myocardial contractility raises the ejection fraction; the two together give the larger maximal stroke output and cardiac output. See Training & Detraining, slide 14 (Aerobic Training Increases Oxygen Delivery to Active Muscles).

## For a healthy person, exercise at 70% of HRmax:

- [ ] Produces lactate accumulation and hyperpnoea
- [x] Is enough to stimulate a training effect
- [ ] Is too light to stimulate a training effect
- [ ] Corresponds to the lactate threshold

> 70% HRmax, "moderate activity" without discomfort, sits within the training-sensitive zone: intense enough for a training effect, not so intense that lactate accumulates with the associated hyperpnoea; as fitness improves the exercise level must be raised periodically to keep reaching this heart rate. See Training & Detraining, slide 22 (Training Intensity: Train at HRmax Percentage).

## After more than 4 weeks of detraining, which of the following is correct?

- [ ] Plasma volume falls for the first time
- [ ] The lungs show marked structural change, losing alveolar surface
- [x] Left ventricular mass and mitochondrial enzymes fall
- [ ] VO₂max stops declining

> The late phase of detraining loses the structural cardiac and mitochondrial adaptations and VO₂max drops by up to 20%, yet the lungs change little structurally (ventilatory efficiency and respiratory muscle endurance are what decline, from the first weeks). Plasma volume is the first thing lost, in weeks 1–2. See Training & Detraining, slide 32 (Detraining (Reversal of Adaptations)).
