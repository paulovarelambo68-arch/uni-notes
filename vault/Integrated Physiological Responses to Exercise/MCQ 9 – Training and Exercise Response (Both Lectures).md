Ten multiple-choice questions drawn from Dr Burns' two lectures, "Training, detraining and long-term adaptations" (Training & Detraining below) and "Integrated Physiological Response to Exercise" (Integrated Response), in the style of her practice questions. Each answer comes with a short explanation and the lecture slide (PDF page number and slide title) that covers it.

## Regarding the duration of each workout needed for optimal aerobic improvement:

- [ ] A minimum of 30 min per session is required, whatever the intensity
- [ ] A minimum of 60 min per session is required
- [ ] Duration does not affect the training response
- [x] No single duration threshold per session exists

> There is no single duration threshold per session: total work accomplished (volume), exercise intensity, training frequency and initial fitness level interact, and extending the duration can compensate for a lower intensity. See Training & Detraining, slide 27 (Training: Duration & Volume).

## When exercise volume is held constant, which of the following best describes the effect of training intensity on VO₂max?

- [x] The higher the intensity above the threshold, the greater the improvement in VO₂max
- [ ] Intensity above the threshold adds nothing further
- [ ] Lower intensities produce the greater improvement
- [ ] Improvement is unrelated to intensity

> Controlling for volume, strenuous training improves VO₂max more, and fitter men and women need a higher threshold intensity to respond; but excessive intensity and abrupt rises in training volume ("overtraining") raise the risk of injury to bones, joints and muscles. See Training & Detraining, slide 26 (Is Strenuous Training More Effective?).

## In the lecture, a blood lactate concentration of 4 mM is used to define:

- [ ] The lactate threshold
- [x] OBLA (the onset of blood lactate accumulation)
- [ ] VO₂max
- [ ] The resting lactate concentration

> Training intensity can be set below, at or above the lactate threshold or OBLA, the latter taken as 4 mM lactate; the lactate threshold is the earlier point at which lactate first rises above its resting level of about 1 mM. See Training & Detraining, slide 21 (Training Intensity) and Integrated Response, slide 14 (Pulmonary Ventilation During PA).

## Training at or slightly above the lactate threshold:

- [ ] Is less effective than training at 55% HRmax
- [x] Is effective if the intensity is raised as fitness improves
- [ ] Should be avoided because it causes overtraining
- [ ] Removes the need to evaluate the lactate–intensity relationship

> It is an effective intensity, with higher levels giving the greatest benefit, but it requires the blood lactate/exercise intensity relationship to be evaluated and the intensity to be raised as fitness improves; RPE provides an effective way to estimate the lactate threshold for continuous activity. See Training & Detraining, slide 25 (Training Intensity: Train at the Lactate Threshold).

## In the same muscle of an endurance athlete, which of the following is true?

- [ ] Fast-twitch fibres are larger than slow-twitch fibres
- [ ] Slow-twitch fibres contain little myoglobin
- [x] Slow-twitch fibres are larger than fast-twitch fibres
- [ ] Fast-twitch fibres are converted into slow-twitch fibres

> All fibre types maximise their existing aerobic potential, but endurance athletes have larger slow-twitch than fast-twitch fibres in the same muscle, and these slow-twitch fibres, with their high capacity to make ATP aerobically, contain large quantities of myoglobin. See Training & Detraining, slide 13 (Muscle Fiber Type and Size).

## Which of the following influence the rate of decline during detraining?

- [ ] Gender, heredity and body composition
- [ ] Training mode, volume and progression
- [ ] Tidal volume, breathing frequency and minute ventilation
- [x] Duration of inactivity, age and fitness level

> Detraining is the loss of the adaptations gained through regular exercise, affecting cardiovascular function, muscle strength and size, and metabolic capacity; its rate depends on how long the inactivity lasts, the person's age and their fitness level. See Training & Detraining, slide 31 (Detraining).

## Cardiorespiratory endurance (CRE) is defined as:

- [x] The ability of the circulatory and respiratory systems to fuel sustained activity
- [ ] The maximal force a muscle group can produce
- [ ] The ability to perform repeated short bursts of very high-intensity exercise with little rest
- [ ] The ability to tolerate high blood lactate concentrations

> CRE, the ability of the circulatory and respiratory systems to supply fuel during sustained physical activity and to eliminate waste (fatigue) products, is a health-associated component of fitness that everyone possesses to some degree; it is measured by VO₂max. See Integrated Response, slide 21 (Assessing Cardiorespiratory Endurance (CRE)).

## In the oxygen cascade, CaO₂ and CvO₂ refer to:

- [ ] Alveolar O₂ and CO₂
- [ ] Muscle O₂ extraction and mitochondrial O₂ use
- [ ] Pulmonary O₂ uptake and CO₂ output
- [x] The O₂ content of arterial and venous blood

> O₂ moves from the atmosphere to the alveoli, into the blood (arterial content CaO₂, venous content CvO₂), is extracted by muscle and used by the mitochondria; VO₂ is pulmonary O₂ uptake and VCO₂ pulmonary CO₂ output. See Integrated Response, slide 9 (O₂ in and CO₂ out).

## The total duration of a typical VO₂max test is:

- [ ] 5 to 8 min
- [ ] 8 to 12 min
- [x] 15 to 30 min
- [ ] 45 to 60 min

> With a low starting load, equal increments every 1–3 min and a run to volitional exhaustion, the test itself takes 15–30 min, bracketed by a 5–10 min low-intensity warm-up and a 5–10 min cool-down. See Integrated Response, slide 28 (Measurement of VO₂max).

## According to the lecture summary, which absolute CPET values are important?

- [ ] Tidal volume, breathing frequency and minute ventilation
- [x] The anaerobic threshold (AT), VO₂peak and VE/VCO₂
- [ ] HRmax, RPE and the talk test
- [ ] Blood lactate, glucose and ammonia

> CPET assesses functional capacity (the integration of the cardiorespiratory system) both clinically and in sport, can be used to stratify risk, and its key absolute values are AT, VO₂peak and VE/VCO₂. See Integrated Response, slide 31 (Summary).
