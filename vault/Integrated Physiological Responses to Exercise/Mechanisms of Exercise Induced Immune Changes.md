
>Exercise reaches immune cells through ==catecholamines (adrenaline and noradrenaline) and cortisol==, which act on the cells' ==adrenergic and glucocorticoid receptors==, through cytokines, and through a higher cardiac output that flushes parked cells into the blood. After a hard bout ==neutrophils keep rising while lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells fall to low levels==, an open window lasting ==no longer than 24 hours and deepest 2 to 6 hours afterwards==. Cells leave the blood by ==rolling, adhesion and diapedesis==, using ==cell adhesion molecules== whose expression rises when catecholamines act on lymphocytes' ==β2-adrenergic receptors==.

### 1. Three messengers

The two stress systems familiar from physiology carry the news of exercise to the immune system. The sympathetic nervous system, which runs the fight-or-flight response, stimulates the adrenal glands to release ==adrenaline and noradrenaline==, grouped together as the ==catecholamines==. In the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, the hypothalamus prompts the pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which in turn makes the adrenal cortex release cortisol, and this ==cortisol release is increased in exercise==. Cortisol is a glucocorticoid, one of the adrenal steroids named after their effect on blood glucose.

What turns this physiology into immunology is that ==immune cells have adrenergic receptors== and ==glucocorticoid receptors==, so they respond to adrenaline, noradrenaline and cortisol.

The third messenger does not come from the brain at all. [[Cytokines]], small signalling proteins, are ==released from immune cells in inflammation and from skeletal muscle during activity==, and the muscle-derived ones, called myokines, are the subject of [[IL-6 in Exercise]].

### 2. The big picture

Walsh and colleagues (2011) placed these messengers in a map of the possible mechanisms whereby exercise can affect immune responses, which shows ==four groups: nervous and hormonal signals, a circulatory flush, other immune mediators, and changes in the immune cells themselves==.

![[Screenshot 2026-10-23 at 13.51.54.png]]

Reading from the top, exercise acts on the brain. Its sympathetic nerve fibres reach the heart and the adrenal medulla, which releases catecholamines, while its neuroendocrine (nerve-to-hormone) route, the HPA axis, uses ACTH to make the adrenal cortex release cortisol. On the left, the heart raises cardiac output and with it shear stress, the drag of flowing blood on vessel walls, causing ==demargination==: leukocytes (white blood cells) parked against the walls of vascular pools, perhaps the spleen, lung, liver and active muscles as the question marks suggest, are ==washed into the circulating blood==. On the right, other immune mediators act too: cytokines, chemokines (cytokines that attract cells) and heat shock proteins, protective proteins that cells release when under strain.

All of these feed into the peripheral circulation, the row below the dotted line, read from left to right. They change ==cell trafficking==, the movement of cells into and out of the blood, through adhesion molecules (surface proteins that make cells stick to one another) and apoptosis (programmed cell death). Dashed arrows lead on to the possible ==preferential mobilisation of cells with an altered effector phenotype==, meaning that the cells swept into the blood are mainly those ready to act, and then to their effector functions, microbial killing and cytokine expression. Finally, ==tissue migration, or homing==, carries cells to the lung mucosa (the airway lining), skin and gut. Exercise therefore changes both where immune cells are and what they do.

### 3. What the cell counts do during and after a hard bout

Pedersen and Toft (2000) summarised the clearest result of all this, the change in blood cell counts with strenuous exercise.

![[Screenshot 2026-10-23 at 13.52.27.png]]

In the graph, time runs left to right, the double-headed arrow marks the exercise period, and each count is shown as a percentage of its resting value, so 100% is baseline. The solid line shows that ==neutrophil concentration increases during and after exercise==. The dotted line tracks lymphocytes, which ==increase during exercise but fall to low levels after intense, long-duration exercise==, and so do NK cells, the innate killers of infected cells ([[Complement and Natural Killer Cells]]). Once exercise stops the two lines part company, and the boxed dip of the lymphocyte line below 100% is the open window ([[Exercise and Infection Risk – J-Shaped Curve and Open Window]]), which ==lasts no longer than 24 hours after exercise, with the most pronounced decrease 2 to 6 hours post-exercise==. The 3 to 72 hours quoted by the open window hypothesis is no contradiction, because the window's length depends on the immune measure followed, and this slide follows cell counts.

### 4. Where the cells come from and how they cross the wall

Where, then, do these cells come from and go to? ==Cells are recruited into the circulation from the spleen, thymus, lymph nodes, bone marrow and lung== ([[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]]) and leave it by transmigration through vessel walls. To cross, a cell must grip the wall, so ==cell adhesion molecules on lymphocytes and endothelial cells==, the flat cells lining blood vessels, ==play a crucial role in lymphocyte trafficking==.

The catecholamines of Section 1 plug straight into this step. ==Lymphocytes express high numbers of β2-adrenergic receptors==, which are ==upregulated with exercise==, and their activation by catecholamines ==increases the expression of cell adhesion molecules==, which play a role in cell migration.

![[Screenshot 2026-10-23 at 13.52.51.png]]

The cartoon shows the crossing, with blood flowing left to right over the pink endothelium. In ==rolling==, the T lymphocyte catches loosely on the wall as CD44, an adhesion molecule on its surface, binds hyaluronan, a long sugar chain (the row of beads) anchored to the endothelium by more CD44, so the cell tumbles along as the blood breaks and remakes these weak bonds. In ==adhesion==, it sticks firmly and flattens, gripped by further adhesion molecules drawn as grey hooks. In ==diapedesis==, it squeezes between the endothelial cells into the tissue while the blood keeps flowing. This is ==the same machinery that brings neutrophils into an inflamed tissue== ([[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP]]).

### Summary

>Exercise signals to immune cells through ==catecholamines acting on adrenergic receptors, cortisol acting on glucocorticoid receptors, and cytokines from immune cells and muscle==. Walsh and colleagues' map adds ==demargination== by raised cardiac output and shows all these signals converging on cell trafficking, effector function and homing to the lung mucosa, skin and gut. After strenuous exercise ==neutrophils rise during and after the bout, while lymphocytes and NK cells rise and then fall to low levels==, an open window lasting ==no longer than 24 hours and deepest at 2 to 6 hours==. Cells come from the spleen, thymus, lymph nodes, bone marrow and lung, and leave the blood by ==rolling, adhesion and diapedesis==, as neutrophils do in inflammation, using ==cell adhesion molecules== that catecholamines increase through ==upregulated β2-adrenergic receptors==.
