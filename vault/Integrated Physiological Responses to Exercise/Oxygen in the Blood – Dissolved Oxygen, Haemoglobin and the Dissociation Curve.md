
> Every cell needs a steady supply of oxygen, and working muscles need far more. This note explains how a protein in the blood carries it from the lungs to wherever it is needed.

### 1. Why Dissolved Oxygen Cannot Supply the Body

Oxygen dissolves poorly in water, so plasma, the watery part of blood, can hold very little of it. Only about ==1.5 per cent== of the blood's oxygen travels dissolved. The other ==98.5 per cent== is bound to haemoglobin, a protein in the red blood cells.

How much oxygen dissolves depends on its partial pressure (PO2). This is the pressure exerted by oxygen on its own, measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg) and explained in [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane]]. For every 1 mmHg of PO2, only ==0.003 mL of oxygen== dissolves in 100 mL of blood. Blood leaving the lungs has a PO2 of about 100 mmHg, so each 100 mL holds just 0.3 mL of dissolved oxygen, or 3 mL per litre.

Small as it is, the dissolved oxygen does a job that bound oxygen cannot. Oxygen bound to haemoglobin exerts no pressure, which is why it counts as hidden. So ==the dissolved oxygen alone sets the PO2== in blood and tissue fluid. And what drives diffusion is the difference in PO2 between two places, not the amount of oxygen dissolved.

Dissolved oxygen is also the doorway into and out of haemoglobin. In the lungs, oxygen first dissolves in the plasma and only then enters the red cells. Haemoglobin takes up this dissolved oxygen almost as soon as it arrives, so the plasma never fills up with it. The plasma PO2 therefore stays below the PO2 of the air in the lungs, and oxygen keeps diffusing in. This continues until haemoglobin is nearly full, when the plasma PO2 catches up with that of the air and the flow stops. 

As a way of carrying oxygen, though, the dissolved form falls far short. At rest the heart pumps about 5 L of blood a minute, the cardiac output explained in [[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation]]. At 3 mL per litre, dissolved oxygen alone would deliver only ==15 mL of oxygen a minute==. But the resting body uses about ==250 mL of oxygen a minute==, so the blood needs a carrier.

### 2. Haemoglobin – Carrying Capacity, Structure and the Route of Oxygen

Haemoglobin, an iron-containing protein that binds oxygen loosely, is that carrier. Each gram of it binds about ==1.34 mL of oxygen==. Men have about ==15 to 16 g of haemoglobin== in each 100 mL of blood, enough to carry about 20 mL of oxygen. Compared with the 0.3 mL that dissolves, this raises the blood's ==oxygen-carrying capacity about 70 times==.

Women have 5 to 10 per cent less, about ==14 g per 100 mL==. The difference comes from testosterone, which boosts the production of ==erythropoietin (EPO)==, a hormone made by the kidneys. EPO drives the production of red blood cells in the bone marrow, a process called ==erythropoiesis==, and more red cells carry more oxygen. Training at altitude also raises EPO, as explained in [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training]].

The haemoglobin molecule itself is made of four protein chains called globins, two alpha (α) and two beta (β). Each chain holds a small flat group called a ==haem==, with one iron atom (Fe2+) at its centre. The iron acts like a magnet for one oxygen molecule, so each haemoglobin has four seats for ==four oxygen molecules==. The bond is weak, fully reversible and leaves the haem unchanged, so oxygen can load in the lungs and come off in the tissues.

Each red blood cell holds about ==280 million haemoglobin molecules==. So one red cell can carry ==more than a billion oxygen molecules==.

Haemoglobin also gives blood its colour, bright red when loaded with oxygen and dark red when not. Arterial blood is therefore bright and venous blood dark, except in the pulmonary artery and veins, where it is the other way round.

### 3. Cooperativity – Four Seats That Help Each Other

Haemoglobin's four seats do not fill independently. When the first oxygen binds to empty haemoglobin, called deoxyhaemoglobin, the whole molecule changes shape slightly (a conformational change). The new shape grips oxygen more strongly (a higher affinity). So the second oxygen binds more easily, and the third and fourth more easily still. This teamwork is called ==cooperativity==.

Because binding at one site changes how the other sites behave, the change of shape is called an ==allosteric change==. Cooperativity also works in reverse: once oxyhaemoglobin releases its first oxygen, the others leave more easily. Haemoglobin carries some carbon dioxide too, and as it loads oxygen in the lungs it drives that carbon dioxide off. This is the ==Haldane effect==, and how blood carries carbon dioxide is explained in [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate]].

Above all, cooperativity makes the graph of how much oxygen haemoglobin holds at each PO2 ==S-shaped (sigmoid).==This shape is what lets haemoglobin fill almost completely in the lungs and still release a lot of oxygen in the tissues. Without cooperativity, the graph would simply bend over gradually, and a carrier that loaded well in the lungs would hold on to its oxygen too tightly in the tissues.

### 4. The Oxygen Dissociation Curve and What Its Shape Does

This S-shaped graph is called the ==oxygen dissociation curve==. It plots haemoglobin's ==percentage saturation==, the share of its seats holding oxygen, against the PO2 around it. Saturation ==climbs steeply up to a PO2 of about 50 mmHg==, then more slowly, and then levels off.

![[Pulm2_slide11_oxygen_dissociation_curve.png|368]]

In this graph, PO2 in mmHg runs along the bottom and saturation up the side. The dashed lines, also listed in the table beneath, mark two reference points. In the systemic arteries, which carry blood from the heart to the body, haemoglobin is ==98 per cent saturated at 100 mmHg==. In the systemic veins, which return blood from the tissues, it is still ==75 per cent saturated at 40 mmHg==. The bracket on the right spans the gap between them, which is the oxygen the tissues take at rest.

These two points sit on different parts of the curve. Arterial blood sits on the flat top, which acts as a ==safety margin==. Saturation stays high even when the PO2 in the lungs falls quite a lot: at 60 mmHg it is still about 90 per cent. This protects loading at altitude.

The tissues sit on the steep middle, where ==a small fall in PO2 releases a lot of oxygen==. At rest they take only ==about a quarter== of the load, and the rest returns to the heart as a reserve. Working muscle uses oxygen fast, so its PO2 falls further down the steep part and the blood hands over much more of that reserve. Conditions in working muscle also shift the whole curve so that haemoglobin lets go more easily, as explained in [[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate]].

### Summary

> Oxygen dissolves poorly, so only ==1.5 per cent== of the blood's oxygen travels dissolved, though that share alone sets the PO2 that drives diffusion. The rest rides on ==haemoglobin==, whose four globin chains each hold a haem with an iron atom that binds one oxygen molecule reversibly. With ==1.34 mL of oxygen per gram== and about 15 g per 100 mL of blood in men, haemoglobin raises carrying capacity about 70 times. Each oxygen that binds changes haemoglobin's shape so the next binds more easily, and this ==cooperativity== makes the dissociation curve S-shaped. Haemoglobin is ==98 per cent saturated at 100 mmHg== in the arteries and still 75 per cent at 40 mmHg in the veins. The flat top keeps arterial blood saturated when PO2 falls, and the ==steep middle== releases much more oxygen when working muscle lowers its PO2.
