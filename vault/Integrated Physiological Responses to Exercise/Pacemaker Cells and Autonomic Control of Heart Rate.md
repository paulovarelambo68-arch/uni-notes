
> ==Pacemaker cells== fire by themselves because their voltage ==climbs to threshold== instead of resting. The fastest, in the ==SA node, fire about 100 times a minute== and set the heart rate, which ==sympathetic nerves raise and the vagus lowers==. A ==transplanted heart rests near 100 beats per minute== but still speeds up in exercise.

### 1. The excitation-conduction system

The heart starts its own beats: ==excitation, the electrical signal that triggers each contraction, is generated within the heart== by ==pacemaker cells==, specialised muscle cells that fire with no outside trigger. Ordinary heart muscle cells sit at a steady resting voltage until something excites them ([[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period]]), but ==pacemaker cells have unstable resting potentials==: after each impulse their voltage climbs again until it reaches threshold, the level at which the cell fires.

Pacemaker cells belong to the ==excitation-conduction system==, which spreads each beat so that the chambers contract in a coordinated order. The signal begins in the ==sinoatrial (SA) node== and travels through the ==atrioventricular (AV) node==, the ==bundle of His== and the ==left and right bundle branches== to the ==Purkinje fibres== (the route and the ECG are covered in [[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System]]).

### 2. The SA node action potential

The SA node's cells show how a pacemaker fires by itself.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 16.43.24 1.png|389]]

The graph plots an SA node cell's voltage over time (scale bar 200 ms). Its action potential has ==only three phases, 4, 0 and 3==: a slow climb from about -60 mV (phase 4), a small upstroke to about +20 mV (phase 0) and a fall (phase 3). Unlike a ventricular cell, it has no flat resting level and no plateau.

Phase 4, the climb, is the ==pre-potential or pacemaker potential==. It starts at ==about -60 to -70 mV== and rises for two reasons. First, a special channel lets Na+ leak in, an ==inward Na+ current called if, the "funny" current== (i stands for current). Second, the ==membrane's K+ permeability (GK) gradually falls==, so ==less K+ leaks out (iK falls)== and if increasingly wins. In the ==last third of the climb, Ca2+ starts flowing in too (iCa)==.

At threshold, phase 0 begins. Because ==SA and AV node cells lack working fast Na+ channels==, which give ordinary muscle its sharp upstroke, their upstroke is ==slow and small==: it is ==carried mainly by Ca2+ flowing in (iCa)== as Ca2+ channels open (GCa rises), partly opposed by outward iK. In ==phase 3, iK brings the voltage back down==, and the climb begins again.

### 3. Autonomic control of heart rate

Because the cell fires whenever its climb reaches threshold, ==the steepness of phase 4 sets the heart rate==, and this is what the nerves adjust. Left alone, the SA node fires ==100 times a minute==, but the ==resting rate is 60 to 70== and heavy exercise can reach ==180==. The ==vagus nerve (parasympathetic) slows the node== and ==sympathetic cardiac nerves speed it up==, and a resting rate below 100 shows that the vagus brakes the node constantly: this is ==vagal tone==.

Sympathetic nerves ==steepen the climb==, so ==threshold comes sooner== and the node fires more often. Their ==noradrenaline binds beta1-adrenergic receptors== on the node and raises ==cyclic AMP (cAMP)==, a messenger inside the cell, which acts in two ways.

- cAMP ==boosts the funny current (if) directly==, so more Na+ enters (GNa rises).
- cAMP ==activates protein kinase A (PKA)==, an enzyme that ==phosphorylates (adds a phosphate to) Ca2+ channels==, so more Ca2+ enters (GCa rises).

A faster heart also needs shorter beats, since each must end before the next starts. So in the pumping muscle cells, where the same chain strengthens contraction ([[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation]]), PKA also phosphorylates K+ channels: ==iK rises and the action potential shortens==, so ==its long duration does not limit heart rate==.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 16.44.14 1.png]]

The vagus does the opposite, as this SA node recording shows (voltage against time, scale bar 2 s; the numbers are gaps between beats in ms). At X the vagus is briefly stimulated: the voltage dips (blue, ==hyperpolarisation==), stretching that gap from 700 to 1250 ms, then climbs more gently (red, ==reduced slope==), keeping gaps near 900 ms. Both changes ==delay threshold (dashed line), so the heart slows==.

Behind both changes is ==acetylcholine (ACh), which the vagus releases onto muscarinic M2 receptors==.

- ACh ==opens ACh-sensitive K+ channels (KACh)==, raising GK, so ==more K+ flows out== and the voltage drops lower.
- ACh ==lowers cAMP==, which ==weakens the sympathetic effect and reduces if and iCa==, flattening the climb.

So, in exams, the effect of ACh is an ==increased K+ current in the SA node==: it never raises cAMP, Ca2+ entry or the slope (in either node), and never depolarises the node.

The two nerves side by side:

| | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic (vagus) |
|---|---|---|
| Transmitter | Noradrenaline | Acetylcholine |
| Receptor | beta1-adrenergic | Muscarinic M2 |
| Second messenger (cAMP) | Rises | Falls |
| Currents | More if and iCa | More iK; less if and iCa |
| Slope of the climb | Steeper | Gentler |
| Starting voltage | Unchanged | More negative |
| Time to threshold | Shorter | Longer |
| Heart rate | Faster | Slower |

### 4. Why the SA node is the pacemaker, and the heart without nerves

The nerves act on the SA node because, as the fastest pacemaker, it sets the pace. Every part of the conduction system can fire on its own, but ==the SA node fires about 100 times a minute, the AV node 40 to 60 and the Purkinje fibres 20 to 40==, so SA impulses fire the slower cells before their own climb reaches threshold. If the SA node's impulses cannot reach the ventricles, as in ==complete AV block==, ==a slower pacemaker lower down takes over and the ventricles beat slowly== ([[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation]]).

A transplanted heart has had its nerves cut, so it shows the SA node working alone. With ==no vagal brake, it rests at about 100 beats per minute==. In exercise ==its rate still rises, but later and less==, because only ==adrenaline from the adrenal glands== can reach its beta1 receptors, through the blood, and that takes time. Meanwhile the heart ==pumps more per beat==, because extra returning blood stretches the ventricle and makes it contract harder ([[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling’s Law and Contractility]] ). The baroreflex, which steadies blood pressure through these nerves, ==cannot change its rate== ([[Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex]]).

### Summary

> ==Pacemaker cells== have ==unstable resting potentials==: their voltage climbs to threshold after every impulse, so the heart makes its own beats, led by the fastest pacemaker, the ==SA node (about 100 per minute when left alone)==. Its action potential has ==only phases 4, 0 and 3==: the climb is driven by the ==funny Na+ current (if) and a falling iK==, the ==small upstroke is carried by Ca2+== because ==node cells lack fast Na+ channels==, and iK brings the voltage back down. ==The steepness of the climb sets the heart rate==, and ==vagal tone== holds it at ==60 to 70 at rest==. ==Noradrenaline (beta1 receptors, more cAMP) steepens the climb== by boosting if and iCa. ==Acetylcholine (M2 receptors) increases the K+ current==, which hyperpolarises the node, and ==lowers cAMP==, which flattens the climb. A ==transplanted heart rests near 100 beats per minute== and speeds up in exercise ==later and less, through circulating adrenaline==.
