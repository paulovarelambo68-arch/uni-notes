
> Cardiac muscle contracts ==more strongly when stretched, up to an optimum length==, so ==a larger end-diastolic volume (EDV) gives a larger stroke volume (SV)==, which is ==Starling's law==. Each beat traces an ==anticlockwise pressure–volume loop== with ==the valve events at its corners== and ==the SV as its width==. ==Contractility, the SV for a given EDV==, is raised by sympathetic activity and measured as ==dP/dt max==, the fastest rise of ventricular pressure.

### 1. Length and tension in cardiac muscle

Stroke volume, the volume of blood ejected in a single beat, depends on how far the ventricular muscle is stretched before it contracts. Its force comes from sarcomeres, the contractile units between Z lines (discs anchoring the thin actin filaments), where cross-bridges of the thick myosin filaments pull actin inwards, the sliding-filament mechanism of [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation]]. A strip of cardiac muscle held at fixed lengths and stimulated shows how that force varies with length.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-03 at 18.34.59.png|562]]

The figure plots tension against length. ==Passive tension== (lower solid curve, red), the elastic recoil of the stretched resting muscle, ==rises steeply at long lengths==, while ==active tension== (dashed, orange), the extra force of cross-bridge cycling, ==rises with length to an optimum and then falls==, and ==total tension== (top curve) ==is their sum==. The sarcomeres under dots 1 to 3 explain the peak, because at 2, the ==optimum length==, filament overlap allows the most cross-bridges, whereas at 3 few myosin heads reach actin. ==Resting length sits just below the optimum==, so a slight stretch (purple) raises total tension (blue arrows), which is the text box's point: ==this ability to increase contractile force with a slight increase in length underlies the heart's ability to control stroke volume==. Past the optimum, ==an over-stretched muscle, as in a dilated heart, produces less active tension==, so ==an overly dilated heart is not a good pump==.

### 2. The pressure–volume loop

![[Screenshot 2026-11-03 at 18.35.42.png|463]]

Figure 2.6 takes the left ventricle's pressure and volume traces from the Wiggers diagram in [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds]] and plots one against the other, so each beat traces one ==anticlockwise== loop. This figure usually carries more than one exam question. The loop's two vertical sides are the isovolumetric phases, when ==all valves are shut, so the volume cannot change==. Pressure still changes, because contracting means developing tension, not necessarily shortening. With the blood trapped and incompressible, the fibres stay at the same length, as when you grip a capped bottle hard without your fingers moving, so their rising tension becomes rising pressure. When they relax, tension and pressure fall together, still at constant volume. Ejection cannot begin until about 80 mmHg, because ==the aortic valve opens only when ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure==, and it shuts when ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure.

The passive compliance curve along the bottom is section 1's passive tension seen in the whole ventricle, and compliance means how easily the relaxed ventricle stretches as it fills. 

The left-hand vertical is the end-systolic volume (ESV) and the right-hand one the end-diastolic volume (EDV), so the width of the loop is ==the stroke volume, SV = EDV - ESV==, here 80 mL. ==The area inside the loop is the stroke work== of one beat.

The exam shows this same loop with letters instead of labels,  and asks which letter marks a given event. Going anticlockwise from the bottom left, the letters match Figure 2.6 as follows:

- A is the bottom-left corner, where ==the mitral (AV) valve opens== and filling begins.
- B is a point along the bottom, partway through filling.
- C is the bottom-right corner, where the mitral valve closes and ==systole begins==.
- D is the top of the right-hand side, where ==the aortic valve opens== and ejection begins.
- E is the top of the loop, the highest pressure during ejection.
- F is the top-left corner, where ==the aortic valve closes==.

Two of these answers are easy to get wrong. ==The second heart sound is heard at F==, because it is the sound of the aortic and pulmonary valves closing. Valves make no sound when they open, and the first heart sound, from the AV valves closing, is at C. ==Systole begins at C==, not at D, because the isovolumetric contraction between C and D is already systole. Atrial contraction, at the very end of filling just before C, still counts as diastole.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-03 at 18.36.19.png]]

This second loop comes from Bray's textbook and uses slightly larger volumes. It has its own letters, which are not the exam's: here filling runs from A to B, and the aortic valve opens at C and closes at D. The red arrow is the stroke volume, 130 - 60 = ==70 mL==, which gives an ==ejection fraction (SV divided by EDV)== of about 54%.

Around the loop, two big curves show the range of pressure the ventricle can have at each volume. The lower, diastolic curve is its pressure when relaxed, so filling always runs along it. The upper, maximum systolic curve is the most pressure it can produce if the aortic valve stays shut. At 130 mL that maximum is C*, far above the 80 mmHg needed at C to open the aortic valve.

### 3. Starling's law of the heart

Starling's law applies section 1 to the whole heart. ==Preload is the stretched condition of the heart muscle at the end of diastole==, set by the EDV. The law is a chain: ==more venous return (blood returning to the heart) raises EDV, which raises preload, which raises stroke volume==. On the pressure–volume loop, a larger EDV moves the right-hand side further right, so the loop is wider and ejects more.

Because it needs no nerve or hormone, the law ==keeps the two ventricles' outputs equal beat by beat==: if the right ventricle pumps a little more, the left ventricle's EDV rises and it pumps more too. A transplanted heart, whose nerves have been cut, relies on it first when venous return rises in exercise ([[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate]]).

Preload is the load on the muscle before it contracts, whereas afterload is the load it contracts against: ==the pressure the ventricle must exceed to eject==, in practice aortic diastolic pressure. That is why the aortic valve opens at about 80 mmHg. A higher afterload, as in [[Hypertension – Causes, Mechanisms, Consequences and Treatment]], means the right-hand side of the loop must climb higher before ejection can begin.

Plotting SV against EDV gives the ==ventricular function (Starling) curve==. At rest the heart sits at a ==stroke volume of about 70 mL for an EDV of 130 to 140 mL==. ==An increase in EDV moves the heart up the curve and increases SV, and a decrease moves it down and lowers SV==.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-03 at 18.37.29.png|408]]

The curve rises, then flattens as the fibres near their optimum length. Its two boxes state that ==contractility is the SV for a given EDV== and that ==every point on the Starling curve has the same contractility==.

### 4. Contractility: what it actually means

The two boxes on that curve define contractility, and the idea behind them is simple. There are two ways to make the heart eject more blood per beat. The first is to fill it more, which stretches the muscle and moves the heart ==along the same Starling curve==. The second is to make it contract more strongly with the same filling, which lifts the heart ==onto a higher curve==, and only this second way is a rise in contractility. That is what the boxes mean: the SV for a given EDV measures how strongly the muscle contracts, while moving along one curve changes only the filling, ==whereas a change in contractility moves the heart to a different curve==. Put precisely, ==contractility is the force the muscle develops at a given length, that is, at a given preload==, so it is a property of the muscle itself, independent of how much the heart is filled (preload) and the pressure it pumps against (afterload). For the same reason, more venous return is not an inotropic (contractility-changing) effect: it only fills the heart more, raising SV by stretch along the same curve. On the pressure–volume loop, a stronger contraction empties the ventricle further, to a ==smaller end-systolic volume from the same EDV==, so the loop gets wider to the left.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-03 at 18.38.02.png|421]]

Each curve is one level of sympathetic activity. At the same EDV of 130 mL, the dots show the SV rising from about 50 mL to about 110 mL as sympathetic activity increases. So ==sympathetic activity increases contractility: a higher curve gives a higher SV for the same EDV==.

### 5. Measuring contractility

Because SV also depends on preload and afterload, contractility is measured when neither can interfere. During isovolumetric contraction, ventricular pressure climbs from its filling level to about 80 mmHg, and a stronger heart makes it climb faster. ==The fastest rate of that climb, called dP/dt max== (change in pressure per change in time), ==is a good measure of myocardial contractility.== With all valves shut, fibre length is fixed and no blood is yet being pushed out against aortic pressure, so the speed of the rise mainly reflects how strongly the muscle contracts. On a pressure trace, dP/dt max is the slope of the steepest part of the upstroke, ==steeper in a hyperdynamic (more forceful) heart, as with noradrenaline, and shallower in a failing one==. The inotropic agents and their calcium machinery are in [[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation]], and the sympathetic effect on contractility is one arm of the baroreflex in [[Arterial Blood Pressure – Measurement, Normal Values and the Baroreflex]].

### Summary

> ==Active tension peaks at an optimum length just above resting length==, so a slight stretch raises force but ==an overly dilated heart is not a good pump==. The ==anticlockwise pressure–volume loop== fills from ==40 to 120 mL==, ejects once pressure passes ==about 80 mmHg== and peaks ==near 120 mmHg==, and on the exam's version ==the AV valves open at A, systole begins at C and the aortic valve closes, with the second heart sound, at F==. ==Preload (EDV) moves the heart along a Starling curve==, whose resting point is an ==SV of about 70 mL at an EDV of 130 to 140 mL==. ==Contractility moves it to another curve==, as sympathetic activity does, and is measured as ==dP/dt max==.
