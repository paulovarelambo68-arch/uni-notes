Dr O'Toole's five example questions for the pulmonary lectures (ET1501, Pulmonary Responses to Exercise 1 to 5), in his wording and option order. Each is answered and explained briefly, with why the other options fail and the note and section that cover it. The five show his style: a short stem, four short options, and one trap built from a neighbouring number or the opposite direction of a change.

## 1. Minute ventilation (VE) =

- [ ] PO2 x PCO2
- [x] Breathing rate x Tidal volume
- [ ] FVC x Transit time
- [ ] Residual volume x FEV1

> Minute ventilation is the volume of air moved into or out of the whole respiratory tract in one minute, so it is breaths per minute times the volume of each breath: about 12 x 0.5 L = 6 L/min at rest, rising to about 100 L/min in strenuous exercise and 160 L/min in male endurance athletes. The other three multiply quantities that have nothing to do with each other. See [[Minute and Alveolar Ventilation – Dead Space, the Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio and Shunt#2. Minute ventilation and its numbers]].

## 2. What is the normal pressure of air at sea level?

- [ ] 76 mmHg
- [ ] 159 mmHg
- [x] 760 mmHg
- [ ] 209.3 mL/L

> 760 mmHg is the barometric pressure at sea level, the pressure that supports a 760 mm column of mercury. 159 mmHg is the partial pressure of oxygen in that air (20.9% of 760), 209.3 mL/L is the volume of oxygen in each litre of air, and 76 mmHg is a decimal-shift distractor. See [[The Respiratory Gases – Composition of Air, Partial Pressures and Exchange across the Alveolar Membrane#1. What is in the air and how hard it presses]].

## 3. At rest, the most important stimulus for breathing is...

- [ ] alveolar PO2
- [ ] blood lactate concentration
- [ ] body temperature
- [x] arterial PCO2

> The PCO2 of arterial plasma is the most important respiratory stimulus at rest, sensed mainly by the central chemoreceptors in the medulla (about 80% of the response) with the peripheral chemoreceptors giving the quick remaining 20%. A fall in PO2 only drives breathing strongly in lung disease or at altitude, lactate matters above the lactate threshold, and core temperature has little effect on breathing except in extreme hyperthermia. See [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation#4. The sensors chemoreceptors]].

## 4. Which of the following is a cellular adaptation to altitude?

- [ ] Reduced mitochondria
- [ ] Reduced 2,3-DPG
- [ ] Reduced inflammation
- [x] Increased myoglobin production

> Acclimatisation remodels the muscle: more myoglobin to store oxygen, a small rise in mitochondrial number, new and remodelled capillaries that shorten the diffusion distance, more 2,3-DPG and a slight right shift of the dissociation curve. Two of the distractors reverse the direction of real changes, because mitochondria and 2,3-DPG both increase. See [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training#4. The first weeks acclimatisation]].

## 5. What gives mucus its thick, sticky consistency?

- [x] Mucin proteins
- [ ] Salts
- [ ] LPS
- [ ] Cytokines

> Mucus is 95–97% water, and it is the mucin glycoproteins secreted by the goblet cells that turn that water into a sticky gel; salts, innate immune proteins and lipids are its other constituents. LPS is a bacterial cell-wall molecule and cytokines are signalling proteins, not constituents of mucus. See [[The Lung's First Line of Defence – Nose, Cilia, Mucus and Antimicrobial Peptides#4. Mucus the sticky blanket that traps and kills microbes]].
