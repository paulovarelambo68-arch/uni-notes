## 1. During resistance (strength) exercise, which of the following best describes the acute blood pressure response?

- [ ] Both systolic and diastolic pressures decrease
- [ ] Systolic pressure increases slightly, diastolic remains constant
- [x] Both systolic and diastolic pressures can rise dramatically
- [ ] Only diastolic pressure increases

> Heavy resistance exercise combines a sustained contraction that compresses the arteries inside the working muscle (raising resistance) with a Valsalva-type rise in intrathoracic pressure, so both pressures climb steeply; pressures above 300/200 mmHg have been recorded during heavy lifts. A fall in either pressure, or a change in only one of them, does not describe this response. Not covered in the two uploaded lectures (acute cardiovascular responses lecture); the chronic counterpart, concentric hypertrophy, is the contrast to the endurance heart of Training & Detraining, slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training).

## 2. During steady-rate aerobic exercise, what happens to diastolic blood pressure in healthy individuals?

- [ ] Increases substantially
- [x] Decreases slightly or remains unchanged
- [ ] Decreases significantly
- [ ] Increases in proportion to workload

> In steady-rate (rhythmic) aerobic exercise it is systolic pressure that rises with the rising cardiac output; diastolic pressure stays about the same or falls a little, because vasodilation of the active muscle beds lowers total peripheral resistance. "Increases in proportion to workload" describes the systolic response, and a significant fall would compromise coronary perfusion. Not covered in the two uploaded lectures (acute cardiovascular responses lecture); the training effect on resting pressure in hypertensives is in Training & Detraining, slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training).

## 3. Which structural adaptation occurs in the heart as a result of chronic endurance training?

- [ ] Thicker right ventricular wall
- [ ] Concentric left ventricular hypertrophy
- [x] Eccentric left ventricular hypertrophy
- [ ] Reduced left ventricular chamber size

> The endurance heart adapts to a volume load: the left ventricular cavity enlarges (larger internal ventricular dimensions, greater compliance) with a proportionate wall, which raises end-diastolic volume and stroke volume. Concentric hypertrophy, a thicker wall without a larger cavity, is the response to a pressure load (resistance training, hypertension), and a smaller chamber is the opposite of what happens. See Training & Detraining, slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training: ↑ stroke volume through ↑ LV volume, eccentric hypertrophy) and slide 14 (Aerobic Training Increases Oxygen Delivery to Active Muscles).

## 4. Which of the following best describes the cardiovascular adaptation to resistance (strength) training?

- [ ] Increased capillary density
- [ ] Eccentric hypertrophy of the left ventricle
- [x] Concentric hypertrophy of the left ventricle
- [ ] Decreased left ventricular wall thickness

> The brief, very high arterial pressures of heavy lifting load the ventricle with pressure rather than volume, so the wall thickens without the cavity enlarging. Eccentric hypertrophy and increased capillary density are endurance-training adaptations, and the wall thickens rather than thins. See Training & Detraining, slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training), which gives the endurance pattern this question contrasts with.

## 5. After chronic aerobic training, which of the following contributes to improved venous return?

- [ ] Reduced muscle pump efficiency
- [x] Increased blood volume and capillarization
- [ ] Decreased venous compliance
- [ ] Reduced end-diastolic volume

> In the lecture's scheme a larger plasma volume and red cell mass raise total blood volume, which raises venous return and, with the more compliant ventricle, end-diastolic volume; angiogenesis raises capillary density in the trained muscle. A less efficient muscle pump and a smaller end-diastolic volume are the reverse of training effects, and a fall in venous compliance is not among the listed adaptations (venous compliance and reactivity appear only among the peripheral blood-flow factors that limit VO₂max, slide 11). See Training & Detraining, slide 14 (Aerobic Training Increases Oxygen Delivery to Active Muscles) and slide 15 (Cardiovascular adaptations to endurance training: ↑ blood volume, ↑ capillary density).
