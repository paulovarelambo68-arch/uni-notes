
> A ==cardiac cycle== is one ventricular contraction (==systole, 0.34 s at rest==) and relaxation (==diastole, 0.66 s==), divided into five steps by valves that ==open and close passively==. Each ventricle fills to ==about 130 mL==, empties to ==about 60 mL== and so ejects ==70 mL==, equally on both sides but at lower pressure on the right. Closure of the atrioventricular (AV) valves makes the ==first heart sound (S1)==, and closure of the semilunar valves the ==second (S2)==.

### 1. The pump and its valves

The heart is a muscular organ inside the pericardium, its enclosing sac, and its wall of cardiac muscle cells is the ==myocardium==. It has right and left sides, each with an atrium and a ventricle, and its valves are held in a ==fibrotendinous ring==.

Between each atrium and its ventricle lies an ==atrioventricular (AV) valve==, the ==tricuspid valve on the right== and the ==mitral valve on the left==. The free margins of its cusps are tied by tendinous cords, the ==chordae tendineae==, to the ==papillary muscles==, which are projections of the ventricular muscle. When the ventricle contracts, the papillary muscles contract with it and pull the cords taut, which ==prevents eversion==, in other words stops the cusps being forced back into the atria. As a result, blood can pass ==only from atrium to ventricle==.The ==semilunar valves==, ==aortic== (left ventricle to aorta) and ==pulmonary== (right ventricle to pulmonary artery), let blood into the arteries in systole (ventricular contraction) and ==stop back-flow in diastole== (relaxation). ==All valves open and close passively on the pressure difference across them==, and together they make flow through the heart ==one-way==.

### 2. What a cardiac cycle is

The length of a cycle is simply 60 seconds divided by the heart rate, ==cycle length (s) = 60 / heart rate (beats per minute)==, so 60/60 = 1.0 s and 60/180 = 0.33 s. A rate of 120 per minute therefore gives 60/120 = ==0.5 s==, and a cycle of only 0.25 s would need 240 beats per minute.

Between rest and maximum rate, ==diastole loses 0.53 s but systole only 0.14 s==, because the two phases are timed differently. Systole lasts roughly as long as the ventricular action potential and the contraction it triggers ([[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period]]), and these shorten only modestly as the rate rises. Diastole, by contrast, is simply the time left before the next beat begins, so ==most of the time saved by beating faster comes out of diastole==. Because diastole is when the ventricles fill, this costs filling time. Little is lost at first, because most filling happens quickly in early diastole, but at the highest rates ==the ventricle fills less and so ejects less== with each beat.

### 3. Diastole in three steps

Diastole begins with ==isovolumetric (isometric) ventricular relaxation==, a very short phase (==0.05 s==) in which ==all valves are shut==, so the relaxing ventricle has ==no flow and no change in volume==, and therefore none in fibre length, which is what the two names mean. Its volume is at the minimum, the ==end-systolic volume (ESV)==. Atrial pressure is still ==lower than ventricular==, but returning blood fills the atrium until its pressure ==exceeds ventricular pressure== and the ==AV valve opens passively==, beginning ventricular filling.

Filling lasts ==about 0.5 s at rest== and is ==about 80% passive==, down the pressure gradient. In ==late diastole== the atria depolarise (the ==P wave== of the ECG) and contract, raising atrial pressure to ==about 5 mmHg== and pushing in the ==final 20%==, so volume peaks at the ==end-diastolic volume (EDV), about 130 mL at rest== (CVS6 uses 140 mL). ==Atrial contraction is therefore the last event of diastole, not the start of systole==.

### 4. Systole in two steps

Systole opens as the ventricles depolarise (the ==QRS complex==) and contract, their tension raising ==left ventricular pressure (LVP)== above atrial pressure, so the ==mitral valve rapidly closes==, which gives the ==first heart sound (S1)== and begins ==isovolumetric (isometric) contraction== (==0.05 s==). With ==AV and semilunar valves all closed== volume cannot change, so pressure ==rises steeply, never falls==, until it can overcome aortic pressure.

Ejection (==0.3 s==) starts when ==LVP exceeds aortic pressure (AP)== and the ==aortic valve opens==, the ==opening of the semilunar valves marking the start of ejection==. Blood entering the aorta ==raises arterial volume and pressure==, LVP and AP ==rise in parallel to a maximum at mid-systole==, and ==about two-thirds of the blood leaves in the first third== of ejection (==rapid ejection==). In late systole (==reduced ejection==) both pressures fall, the ventricles repolarise (the ==T wave==) and volume reaches ESV. ==Isovolumetric contraction (0.05 s) is thus far shorter than ejection (0.3 s)==.

### 5. Closing the loop

When ventricular pressure ==falls below aortic==, the ==semilunar valves close==, giving the ==second heart sound (S2)== with ==aortic (A) and pulmonary (P) components==. ==This closure marks the end of systole and the beginning of diastole==, and the cycle re-enters isovolumetric relaxation at an ==ESV of about 60 mL==.

Followed round the whole cycle, ventricular volume is ==maximal at EDV==, falls in ejection to its ==minimum at ESV== and is constant in both isovolumetric phases. Ventricular pressure is ==lowest at the start of diastole== and rises a little with filling and atrial contraction, then ==rises dramatically in isovolumetric contraction== to a ==maximum in ejection==, falls in late systole and ==falls dramatically in isovolumetric relaxation==. Arterial pressure is ==lowest at the end of diastole (the diastolic pressure)== and peaks with ventricular pressure in ejection (==the systolic pressure==). It dips at the ==incisura or dicrotic notch== when the aortic valve closes, a brief dip as back-flow snaps the valve shut, and then falls through diastole to its diastolic minimum.

The two volumes give the ==stroke volume, SV = EDV - ESV = 130 - 60 = 70 mL==, and the ==ejection fraction, EF = SV / EDV, about 54%==.

### 6. The numbers for both sides

The right side of the heart, the right atrium and ventricle that pump blood to the lungs, runs ==essentially the same events as the left==, and the resting numbers show what differs.

| Chamber or vessel                | Diastole   | Systole   |
| -------------------------------- | ---------- | --------- |
| Left ventricle                   | 0 mmHg     | 120 mmHg  |
| Aorta                            | 80 mmHg    | 120 mmHg  |
| Right ventricle                  | 0 mmHg     | 25 mmHg   |
| Pulmonary artery                 | 8 mmHg     | 25 mmHg   |
| Ventricular volume, left = right | EDV 130 mL | ESV 60 mL |

The table gives an arterial ==pulse pressure==, systolic minus diastolic, of ==40 mmHg==. Because the circuits are in series, ==right ventricular volumes equal left==, but ==pulmonary resistance is much lower than total peripheral resistance (TPR)== of the systemic circulation ([[Circulation Overview – Circuits, Vessels and the MAP Equation]]), so ==right ventricular and pulmonary artery pressures are far lower than LVP and AP==. In other words, ==both sides process the same volumes at very different pressures==.

### 7. Heart sounds and the ECG on the same timeline

Each valve closure is audible, so the heart sounds share this timeline. S1 is ==mitral before tricuspid closure (M1 before T1)==, a ==low, long 'lub'==, and S2 is ==aortic before pulmonary (A2 before P2)==, a ==short, high-pitched 'dub'==. The ==third sound (S3)==, from ==passive filling in early diastole==, is ==not heard in adults but may be in the young==, and the ==fourth (S4)==, from ==rapid filling after atrial contraction==, is ==not heard==, both being ==detected by phonocardiography== (microphone recording). On the ECG ==each electrical event precedes its mechanical one==: the ==P wave (atrial depolarisation) precedes atrial contraction==, the ==QRS complex (ventricular depolarisation) precedes ventricular contraction==, and the ==T wave is ventricular repolarisation== ([[The ECG – Conduction, Leads and the Hexaxial System]]).

### 8. Murmurs

Between these sounds healthy valves are silent, because ==normal flow is laminar (stream-lined)==, whereas ==turbulent flow is heard as a murmur==, an ==important sign of valve lesions==: narrowing (==stenosis==) or reversed flow (==insufficiency or incompetence==). A stenosis murmur sounds while its valve should be open, an incompetence murmur while it should be shut.

- In ==aortic stenosis== the murmur is ==systolic, between S1 and S2, peaking at mid-systole==.
- In ==mitral incompetence== the murmur lasts ==throughout systole==.
- In ==aortic incompetence== the murmur comes in ==early diastole==.
- In ==mitral stenosis== the murmur is ==low-pitched and mid-diastolic==.

### Summary

> Passive valves divide the cycle into ==diastole== (isovolumetric relaxation, ==80% passive filling== and ==atrial contraction adding the last 20%==) and ==systole== (==isovolumetric contraction, 0.05 s, starting with S1==, then ==ejection, 0.3 s==). Each ventricle swings between an ==EDV of about 130 mL== and an ==ESV of about 60 mL==, ejecting ==70 mL== on both sides, but at ==120/80 mmHg in the aorta== against ==25/8 mmHg in the pulmonary artery==. ==Semilunar closure gives S2 and the dicrotic notch and ends systole==. Faster rates ==shorten diastole most==, so at ==about 180 per minute it is shorter than systole==. Murmurs are ==turbulent flow== through a stenosed or incompetent valve.
