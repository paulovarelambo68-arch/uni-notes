
> Each heartbeat starts as an electrical signal that travels a fixed route through the heart, ==pausing between the upper and lower chambers==. The electrocardiogram (ECG) records this activity from the skin as waves. A wave points up or down depending on whether the activity travels ==towards or away from a positive electrode==, and each electrode pair, called a lead, ==sees the heart from its own angle==. The paper's grid turns the waves into ==timings and a heart rate==.

### 1. How excitation spreads

A heartbeat starts with an action potential: ==the inside of a heart cell flips from negative to positive==, triggering contraction ([[Cardiac Action Potential – Resting Potential, Phases and Refractory Period]]). It spreads by ==local currents==. Positive charge flows ==through the gap junctions== (channels linking neighbouring cells) into the still-negative neighbour and excites it, while outside the cells charge flows back (from the resting cell's positive surface towards the excited cell's now-negative one), reducing the positive charge outside the resting cell and helping it fire.

The signal starts in the SA node, the heart's pacemaker ([[Pacemaker Cells and Autonomic Control of Heart Rate]]), and changes speed along its route:

| Route | Speed |
|---|---|
| Atrial muscle | 0.5 m/s |
| AV node, the only bridge from atria to ventricles | ==0.05 m/s (slowest)== |
| Bundle of His and branches, down the septum (wall between the ventricles) | 1.0 m/s |
| Purkinje fibres, through the ventricle walls | ==5.0 m/s (fastest)== |
| Ventricular muscle | 0.5 m/s |

The slow AV node holds the signal back about 0.1 s, so ==the atria empty before the ventricles contract==. The fast Purkinje fibres reach all ventricular muscle almost at once, so ==both ventricles contract together, from the apex (tip) up==.

### 2. What an ECG records

These currents also flow through the salty extracellular fluid, so they reach the skin. The ECG is ==a recording of voltage changes at the skin caused by heart muscle depolarising and repolarising== (being excited, then recovering). A sensitive voltmeter, wired to metal skin electrodes, records these ==tiny voltages (about 1 mV)== on moving paper or a screen. Each recording compares a positive electrode with a negative one, a pairing called a ==lead== (section 5). ==Signal size depends on how much muscle is active==, so ==the small SA and AV nodes do not show==.

### 3. The vector rules

Spreading excitation is a ==vector==: it has a size and a direction. ==Its direction relative to the positive electrode decides whether the trace goes up or down==. Recovery (repolarisation) is the reverse change, so its rules are reversed:

| The wave | Trace |
|---|---|
| Depolarisation towards the + electrode | Up |
| Depolarisation away | Down |
| Repolarisation away | ==Up== |
| Repolarisation towards | Down |
| No electrical activity | Flat baseline (isoelectric line) |
| Moving at 90° to the lead | ==Flat== |

To remember it, start from "depolarisation towards = up": flip the wave type or the direction and the trace flips; flip both and it is up again.

### 4. The origin of the waves in lead II

Lead II runs from the right arm (negative) to the ==left leg (positive), directly below the heart==, so "towards" means down and to the patient's left.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 19.40.19.png|453]]

In these snapshots of one beat, the red + is the left-leg electrode, each small triangle's arrow shows the direction of activity, and the trace grows alongside.

- The SA node fires by itself and excitation spreads down the atria, towards the electrode: an upward ==P wave==.
- The septum is excited first, and its overall direction is away from the electrode: a small downward ==q wave==.
- Most ventricular muscle is excited from the inside outwards, towards the electrode: the tall upward ==R wave==.
- The last part, near the top, is excited from the inside outwards but away from the electrode: a small downward ==s wave==.
- The ventricles recover from the outside inwards, from the apex up, so recovery moves away from the electrode: an upward ==T wave==.

The atria recover too, but ==atrial repolarisation is not seen==: it happens during the much larger QRS. What each wave triggers is in [[The Cardiac Cycle – Phases, Pressures, Volumes and Heart Sounds]].

### 5. The twelve leads: limb leads, the hexaxial system and chest leads

A standard ECG looks at the heart through ==twelve leads==, each viewing it from a different direction. ==Six are limb leads==, made from electrodes on the arms and legs: Einthoven's three bipolar leads and three augmented leads, which together form one picture, the ==hexaxial reference system==. ==The other six are chest leads==. Lead II, from section 4, is one of Einthoven's leads.

The first set, ==Einthoven's bipolar leads==, uses electrodes on the ==left arm (LA), right arm (RA) and left leg (LL)==, while the ==right leg carries only an electrical earth==. Pairing them gives ==leads I, II and III==, which form ==Einthoven's triangle== in the ==frontal plane== (the body seen from the front).

The second set, the ==augmented leads==, uses ==the same three electrodes, with no new ones added==; only the way the machine combines them changes. Picture the electrodes as the three corners of Einthoven's triangle. Each bipolar lead runs along one side, from corner to corner. Each augmented lead instead runs from the middle of one side to the opposite corner: the machine ==joins two electrodes together as the negative end, which acts like a point halfway between them, and makes the third electrode positive==. For aVF, for example, the two arm electrodes are joined, giving a point midway between the shoulders, so aVF runs straight down from there to the foot. Each lead looks at the heart from its positive limb, which gives its name: ==aVR (right arm), aVL (left arm), aVF (foot)==. ==aV stands for augmented voltage==, because this wiring makes the signal bigger, and the leads are called unipolar because only one electrode does the looking.

Though wired differently, the two sets form one system: all six limb leads lie in the frontal plane, so they are drawn through one centre as six lines 30° apart, the ==hexaxial reference system==. Read it like a clock on the patient's chest as you face them: ==0° is 3 o'clock (the patient's left)==, each hour adds 30°, and ==+90° is 6 o'clock (straight down)==. With the leg at the triangle's bottom corner, each lead points towards its positive end:

| Lead | Positive | Negative | Angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | LA | RA | ==0°== |
| II | LL | RA | ==+60°== |
| III | LL | LA | ==+120°== |
| aVF | LL | RA + LA | ==+90°== |
| aVL | LA | RA + LL | ==−30°== |
| aVR | RA | LA + LL | ==−150°== |

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 19.40.55 2.png]]


On the left is Einthoven's triangle (LF = left foot); on the right, the six leads share one centre, each spoke with its angle in red and a typical QRS. Because each lead sees only the part of a wave running along its line, ==a wave looks tallest in the lead pointing the same way==. The ==mean QRS axis==, the average direction of ventricular excitation, normally points ==about +60°== (range −30° to +90°), so lead II is tallest and ==aVR, looking from almost the opposite side, is the only mainly downward lead==. Likewise, a wave at +120° is tallest in lead III, smaller in aVF (30° off), flat in aVR (90° off) and downward in aVL (150° off).

The ==chest leads (V1–V6)== are six electrodes across the chest: ==V1 and V2 either side of the breastbone, V3 to V6 curving round the left side towards the armpit==. Each is the positive end of its own lead. The ==negative end is shared: the three limb electrodes joined together==, which average out to one point in the middle of the body, roughly at the centre of the back (the right leg stays as the earth). Each lead therefore runs from the back, through the heart, to its electrode, so together they show the ==horizontal plane== (a slice across the chest), which the limb leads miss. Sitting close to the heart, they draw ==large deflections==, meaning big waves. The thick left ventricle pulls the wave leftwards, so ==V1–V2 (over the right ventricle) show a deep S, V3–V4 (over the septum) an R and an S, and V5–V6 (over the left ventricle) a tall R==.

### 6. Reading the paper: calibration, intervals and heart rate

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 19.44.08.png|358]]

The square bump is a 1 mV test pulse, drawn ==10 mm tall (two large boxes, ten small squares)==. The red arrow spans one second at ==25 mm/s (five large boxes, 25 small squares)==, so ==a large box is 0.2 s and a small square 0.04 s==.

The trace has ==waves== (P, Q, R, S, T), ==segments==, the flat stretches between waves (the ==ST segment runs from the end of S to the start of T==), and ==intervals==, which include both.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-04 at 19.44.48.png|317]]

The bracket marks the ==PR interval==, from the start of P to the start of the QRS. The red box frames the P wave and the flat line where the signal waits in the AV node. So the PR interval is the time ==from the start of atrial to the start of ventricular excitation==, reflects ==AV node conduction==, and is ==normally 0.12–0.20 s== (three to five small squares). Longer means slowed AV conduction, first-degree block ([[Arrhythmias – Sinus Arrhythmia, AV Block, Ectopic Beats and Fibrillation]]).

At five large boxes per second, a minute holds 300 large boxes, so ==heart rate = 300 ÷ large boxes between two R waves==: four boxes give 75 per minute.

### Summary

> Excitation spreads cell to cell by local currents, ==slowed in the AV node (0.05 m/s)== so the atria empty first, and ==sped through the Purkinje fibres (5.0 m/s)== so both ventricles contract together. The ECG records about 1 mV at the skin: ==depolarisation moving towards the positive electrode draws an upward wave==, and repolarisation follows the reverse rules. In lead II this gives upward P, R and T waves, while atrial recovery stays hidden in the QRS. Each lead looks from its positive end at a fixed angle (==I 0°, II +60°, III +120°, aVF +90°, aVL −30°, aVR −150°==), a wave is tallest in the lead matching its direction, and six chest leads complete the twelve-lead ECG. On standard paper, the ==PR interval is 0.12–0.20 s== and ==heart rate = 300 ÷ large boxes between R waves==.
