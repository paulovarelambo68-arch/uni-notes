
> The airways clean the air we breathe before it can harm the lung. This note follows a particle on its way in, from the nose to a moving layer of mucus and the molecules that kill microbes.

### 1. Why the lung needs its own defences

The lung needs defences of its own because it is open to the outside air. We breathe in roughly 10,000 L of it a day, laden with dust, pollen and microbes. Physical barriers stop most of them, and molecules dissolved in the airway lining attack the microbes. Only the survivors meet the lung's immune cells, described in [[Lung Immune Cells – Toll-like Receptors, Macrophages and Neutrophils]].

All these defences belong to innate immunity, the inborn system that ==acts within hours==. Adaptive immunity, which targets one particular microbe, ==takes days==, as [[Immune System Overview – Functions and the Two Branches]] explains.

### 2. The nose: a filter that sorts particles by size

The nose is the first filter. Its lining swells or shrinks to suit conditions such as exercise, a change of climate or sleep.

Air entering the nose soon reaches its narrowest point, the ==nasal valve==, about 1.5 cm inside the nostril. Squeezing through this gap, the air can reach ==18 m/s==, the speed of a gale. Nowhere else in the respiratory tract does air move faster.

That speed matters, because how far a particle gets depends on four things: its ==size, density, time in the airway and speed==. Heavy, fast particles cannot follow the air around bends, so they crash into the wet wall and stick.

In practice, the nasal mucosa, the moist lining of the nose, traps ==particles of 0.5 to 3 micrometres (µm)==, about the size of bacteria. Cilia, tiny beating hairs on the lining, sweep them to the back of the nose, where most are swallowed. Particles ==smaller than 0.5 µm== follow the air around bends and pass through too quickly to settle. The nose therefore filters them poorly, and they can reach the alveoli, the air sacs where oxygen enters the blood.

### 3. Cilia: the escalator that carries mucus out of the airway

Cilia keep the airways clean from the nose deep into the lung. A single lining cell can carry ==200 to 300== of these hair-like projections. They resemble the whip-like tails (flagella) of some bacteria, but the two are unrelated. Each cilium bends because motor proteins called ==dyneins== slide its inner rods past one another. The dyneins run on energy released by splitting ATP, the cell's energy currency.

Each beat has a stiff forward stroke that pushes the mucus along, then a limp return stroke that slips back beneath it. Neighbouring cilia beat slightly out of step, so waves ripple along the lining like wind across a wheat field. These waves carry the mucus, and everything stuck in it, upwards and out of the airway. This moving belt is the ==mucociliary escalator==, and it also keeps particles from touching the cells beneath.

The escalator fails in two inherited diseases. In ==cystic fibrosis==, the mucus is too thick for the cilia to move. In ==primary ciliary dyskinesia==, the cilia themselves are faulty. The metals cadmium, copper and magnesium can also interfere with cilia. So can smoking, as [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold]] explains.

### 4. Mucus: the sticky blanket that traps and kills microbes

The mucus that rides on the cilia is made by ==goblet cells==, cup-shaped cells in the airway lining. It is ==95 to 97 per cent water==. Most of the rest is mucins, sugar-coated proteins that turn the water into a sticky gel. Mucus also carries salts, fats (lipids) and innate immune proteins that attack microbes directly.

These ingredients give mucus five jobs. It is a ==physical barrier== and a lubricant, and it keeps the cells beneath it moist and working. It also destroys microbes and, riding on the cilia, removes them. Mucus is like flypaper laid on a conveyor belt, and the cilia are the belt.

### 5. Molecules that attack microbes: antimicrobial peptides, lysozyme and lactoferrin

Many of the microbe-killing proteins in mucus are antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), short chains of amino acids. They have ==broad activity==, which means each attacks whole classes of microbes rather than one species. The airway makes more of them during an infection. Most kill by breaking a bacterium's rigid wall or the thin, fatty membrane that holds the cell together. A hole in the membrane lets the contents leak out, and the bacterium dies.

Two families of AMPs kill in this way. Defensins are very small, only ==18 to 45 amino acids== long, and they break up the membrane. Alpha-defensins are made by ==neutrophils and natural killer cells==, white blood cells described in [[Cells and Tissues of the Immune System]] and [[Complement and Natural Killer Cells]]. Beta-defensins come from white blood cells in general (leukocytes) and from the ==epithelial cells== that line the airway.

Cathelicidins are also small, ==12 to 80 amino acids== long, and the human one, LL-37, has 37. They ==punch pores==, or holes, right through the membrane. LL-37 also signals to receptors on other cells, helping to call in immune cells. Both families are either ==secreted or used inside the phagosome==. The phagosome is the bubble in which a cell digests a microbe it has swallowed, as [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis]] explains.

In these four cartoons, the dark corkscrews are peptides and the double row of fat molecules is the membrane they attack. In the barrel-stave and toroidal pore models, the peptides stand upright and line a hole. In the carpet model, they lie flat and coat the surface until it dissolves, like grease in washing-up liquid. In the aggregate model, they clump with fat molecules into knots that span the membrane. Whichever way it happens, the membrane leaks.

Lysozyme, one of the chemical barriers named in [[Immune System Overview – Functions and the Two Branches]], attacks the bacterial wall rather than the membrane. The wall is built from peptidoglycan, a mesh of sugar chains described in [[Innate Immunity – Pathogen Recognition and Phagocytosis]]. Lysozyme is an enzyme that splits (hydrolyses) the ==beta-1,4 links== between these sugars. The mesh falls apart, so the bacterium swells with water and bursts.

Two other molecules starve bacteria instead of breaking them. Bacteria need iron to make energy and copy their DNA, so ==without iron they cannot multiply==. Hepcidin, a third kind of AMP, is an iron-binding (iron-chelating) protein made ==mostly by the liver==. It works strongly against some microbes and not at all against others. Lactoferrin, a mucus protein often classed with lysozyme as an enzyme, likewise ==sequesters iron==, locking it away from bacteria.

### Summary

> Air speeds through the narrow nasal valve at ==up to 18 m/s==, so heavy, fast particles crash into the wet lining of the nose. That lining traps ==particles of 0.5 to 3 µm==, while smaller ones are filtered poorly and can reach the alveoli. Each lining cell can carry 200 to 300 dynein-driven cilia, whose waves move mucus upwards and out of the airway as the ==mucociliary escalator==. Mucus from goblet cells is ==95 to 97 per cent water== thickened by mucins, and it traps, kills and removes microbes. Most antimicrobial peptides, such as defensins and cathelicidin (LL-37), kill by ==breaking bacterial membranes==. Lysozyme splits the ==beta-1,4 links of peptidoglycan== in the bacterial wall, while hepcidin and lactoferrin starve bacteria by locking iron away.
