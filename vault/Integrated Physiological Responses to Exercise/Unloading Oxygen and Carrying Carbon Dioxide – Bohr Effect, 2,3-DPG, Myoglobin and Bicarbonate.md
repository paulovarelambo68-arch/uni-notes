
> Working muscle needs extra oxygen and must clear extra carbon dioxide. This note explains how it makes the blood release more oxygen, keeps a small store of its own and sends carbon dioxide back to the lungs.

### 1. Muscle tells haemoglobin to let go: the Bohr effect

Working muscle changes its blood in three ways, and each makes haemoglobin, the oxygen carrier in red blood cells, release more oxygen. First, the muscle is hotter, because breaking down fuel releases heat. Second, that same breakdown produces more carbon dioxide. Third, the muscle is more acidic, meaning it has more free hydrogen ions and so a lower pH. These hydrogen ions come partly from that extra carbon dioxide, which forms an acid in water, and partly from the lactate that builds up in hard exercise.

Each change ==shifts the dissociation curve down and to the right==. This curve, explained in [[Oxygen in the Blood – Dissolved Oxygen, Haemoglobin and the Dissociation Curve]], plots how much oxygen haemoglobin holds at each oxygen pressure (PO₂). After a right shift, haemoglobin holds less oxygen at any given pressure, so more is released. In other words, its ==affinity== for oxygen, meaning how tightly it holds on, has fallen. Picture haemoglobin as a magnet that grips oxygen less tightly when it is warm and in acid.

Hydrogen ions and carbon dioxide loosen the grip by attaching to haemoglobin and changing its shape. Heat loosens it too. The released oxygen diffuses, or spreads, into the muscle fibres, where its pressure is lower. This loosening of haemoglobin's grip is called the ==Bohr effect==. It ==predominates in intense exercise==, when heat, carbon dioxide and lactate all climb.

The middle curve in each panel is normal blood. The curve moves right with high carbon dioxide, labelled "High PCO₂", with more acid and with more heat. The left-hand curves, labelled "increased affinity" at pH 7.60 and 20°C, show the opposite, a ==left shift==, in which haemoglobin holds on more tightly. This happens in the cold, covered in [[Limits to Ventilation – Work of Breathing, Breathing Reserve, Exercise-Induced Hypoxaemia, Smoking and Cold]]. It also happens in the alkaline, or less acidic, blood of the first days at altitude, covered in [[Altitude – Less Oxygen, Acclimatisation, Performance and Altitude Training]].

### 2. The red cell's own release signal: 2,3-DPG

The red cell also makes a release signal of its own, a small molecule called ==2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG)==, also known as 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG). It ==binds to deoxyhaemoglobin==, haemoglobin that has given up its oxygen, and holds it in that unloaded shape. So oxygen is released more easily, another right shift of the curve that weakens the magnet further.

2,3-DPG is a by-product of the way red cells make energy. They have ==no mitochondria==, so they rely entirely on ==anaerobic glycolysis==, the breakdown of glucose without oxygen. The enzyme that makes 2,3-DPG along this pathway is normally held back by oxyhaemoglobin, which is haemoglobin carrying oxygen.

So when there is less oxyhaemoglobin, more 2,3-DPG is made. This happens ==at high altitude and in anaemia==, a shortage of haemoglobin. The blood then unloads oxygen more readily, which offsets the poorer supply. In pregnancy, a rise in 2,3-DPG helps oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the placenta.

### 3. The muscle's own oxygen store: myoglobin

Muscle fibres also keep a small oxygen store of their own, in a protein called ==myoglobin==. This ball-shaped (globular) protein contains iron and is found in skeletal and heart muscle. Like haemoglobin, it binds oxygen reversibly, picking it up and releasing it again. But each molecule has only ==one iron atom==, so it holds just one oxygen molecule.

![[Pulm2_slide16_myoglobin_vs_haemoglobin.png|301]]

 In haemoglobin, each oxygen that binds makes the next bind more easily (cooperativity), which bends its curve into an S. Myoglobin's single seat allows no such teamwork. So its curve rises steeply from the lowest pressures and then flattens, a shape called a ==hyperbola==. In short, the S shape suits a deliverer and the hyperbola suits a keeper.

Myoglobin also has a ==higher affinity== for oxygen than haemoglobin, so its curve lies further left. It keeps its oxygen at pressures where haemoglobin has already let go, and stays almost fully loaded at rest and in moderate exercise. In hard exercise, the oxygen pressure inside the fibre falls, and myoglobin hands its oxygen to the mitochondria. It releases most of it only when that pressure drops ==below 5 mmHg==. It is a reserve tank that opens only when the main supply runs low.

Unlike haemoglobin, myoglobin is ==not subject to the Bohr effect==, so heat and acid cannot loosen this stronger magnet early. ==Slow-twitch fibres==, which contract slowly and resist fatigue, contain more of it. Myoglobin is a red pigment, so these fibres look redder. 

### 4. The return journey: carrying carbon dioxide

Working muscle also hands the blood carbon dioxide to carry back to the lungs. It travels in three forms:

- About ==7 per cent== is dissolved in the plasma, the liquid part of the blood. Only these free molecules exert gas pressure, so they set the blood's carbon dioxide pressure (PCO₂).
- About ==23 per cent== rides loosely on haemoglobin's protein chains, a form called carbaminohaemoglobin.
- About ==70 per cent== travels as bicarbonate, made inside the red cell.

Bicarbonate forms when carbon dioxide (CO₂) joins water (H₂O) to make carbonic acid, which then splits into bicarbonate and a hydrogen ion. In symbols, ==CO₂ + H₂O ↔ H₂CO₃ ↔ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺==, where H₂CO₃ is carbonic acid, HCO₃⁻ is bicarbonate and H⁺ is a hydrogen ion. The double arrows mean that each step can run either way. On its own the first step is slow, but the enzyme ==carbonic anhydrase== in the red cell speeds it up thousands of times.

Bicarbonate is the main route because it ==dissolves far more easily in water== than carbon dioxide gas does. In the lungs, the reaction runs backwards. As carbon dioxide diffuses out into the alveoli, the lungs' air sacs, the blood's PCO₂ falls. Bicarbonate and hydrogen ions then rejoin as carbonic acid, which splits back into water and carbon dioxide, and the carbon dioxide is breathed out.

The hydrogen ion from this reaction is why carbon dioxide makes the blood more acidic, which helps weaken the haemoglobin magnet in working muscle. The same hydrogen ion is what chemoreceptors detect when carbon dioxide rises. These chemical sensors tell the brain's breathing centres to increase breathing, as [[Control of Breathing – Brainstem Centres, Chemoreceptors and the Three Phases of Exercise Ventilation]] explains. Haemoglobin mops up many of these hydrogen ions, a buffering job explained in [[Acid–Base Balance in Exercise – Buffers, Ventilation and the Kidney]].

### Summary

> Heat, carbon dioxide and acid in working muscle lower haemoglobin's affinity and shift its curve right, releasing more oxygen: the ==Bohr effect==, strongest in intense exercise. Red cells, which lack mitochondria, make ==2,3-DPG== by anaerobic glycolysis, and it binds deoxyhaemoglobin to shift the curve right as well. Oxyhaemoglobin holds back the enzyme that makes 2,3-DPG, so its level rises when oxyhaemoglobin falls, as ==at altitude and in anaemia==. Myoglobin, the muscle's own oxygen store, is richest in slow-twitch fibres and holds ==one oxygen per molecule==, so its curve is a hyperbola rather than an S. It has a higher affinity than haemoglobin, shows no Bohr effect and gives up most of its oxygen only ==below 5 mmHg==. Carbon dioxide travels about 7 per cent dissolved, 23 per cent on haemoglobin and ==70 per cent as bicarbonate==, made by carbonic anhydrase and reversed in the lungs.
