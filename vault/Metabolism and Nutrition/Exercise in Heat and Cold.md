
> Exercise in the heat asks one supply of blood to feed the muscles and to carry heat out to the skin. This note follows what happens when that blood runs short, up to the illnesses heat can cause. It then turns to the cold, where the body protects its core at the expense of the muscles.

### 1. Skin and muscle compete for blood

In the heat, the skin vessels widen (dilate) to carry heat from the core to the surface ([[Coronary and Cutaneous Blood Flow]]). The skin becomes engorged with a large volume of blood, which ==pools in the skin== instead of flowing straight back. So less blood returns to the heart, which means venous return falls. The central blood volume, held in the large veins and the heart, falls with it. A heart that fills less ejects less with each beat, so stroke volume falls ([[Pressure–Volume Loop, Starling's Law and Contractility]]). This is why ==the major cardiovascular burden of heat stress results from impaired venous return==.

The body compensates in two ways. It constricts the vessels of the kidneys, gut and liver, called the ==renal and splanchnic beds==. This pushes their blood back toward the heart and diverts cardiac output to the skin and the working muscles. It also raises heart rate. Cardiac output (CO) is stroke volume (SV) times heart rate (HR), ==CO = SV × HR==. So a faster heart can make up for a smaller stroke volume, but only up to a point.

### 2. Hormones that save water and salt

Sweating costs water and sodium, and exercise with a loss of body water triggers two hormones. Aldosterone, from the cortex of the adrenal gland on top of the kidney, makes the kidneys ==retain sodium==. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, also called vasopressin), from the posterior pituitary gland at the base of the brain, makes the kidneys ==retain water==.

### 3. Heat illnesses in order of severity

All four heat illnesses result from strenuous exercise combined with environmental heat, and they form a scale of increasing severity.

| Illness | Severity | Core temperature | What defines it | Warning signs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heat cramps | Mildest | Not defined by a temperature | Severe, painful cramping of large muscles, triggered by sodium loss and dehydration. Most common in heavy sweaters. Prevented by a liberal intake of sodium and water. | Painful large muscle cramps, profuse sweating, excessive thirst |
| Heat exhaustion | Mild to moderate | ==From 38.5 up to about 40 °C== | Inability to sustain cardiac output, often with hot skin and dehydration | Fatigue, headache, chills and goose bumps, nausea |
| Heat injury | Moderate to severe | ==Above 40 °C== | Injury to organs or tissues | No separate signs given |
| Heat stroke | Severe | High, ==above 40 °C== | Profound dysfunction of the central nervous system with organ or tissue injury. Can leave profound neuropsychiatric impairment. Can be complicated by liver damage and rhabdomyolysis, the breakdown of damaged muscle fibres that release their contents into the blood ([[McArdle Disease Cause and Mechanism]]). | ==Cessation of sweating==, confusion, loss of consciousness |

The change from profuse sweating to ==no sweating, together with confusion,== marks the emergency.

### 4. From heat strain to heat stroke

Heat strain damages organs by two routes that feed each other. In the first, blood goes to the skin and muscles while the gut vessels constrict, so the gut wall becomes ==ischaemic==, meaning short of blood. Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS and RNS), unstable molecules that attack cells, then make the wall leaky. Bacterial toxins cross into the blood, a state called ==endotoxaemia==. They set off an exaggerated acute phase response, an inflammatory reaction of the whole body ([[Inflammation – Acute, Chronic and CRP]]). Through nitric oxide (NO), this response worsens the ischaemia.

In the second route, heat shock and ischaemia strike cells directly, in the brain, vessel lining, intestine, liver, kidneys, heart and muscles. Some cells are injured, with clotting disorders and inflammation. Some die, by planned self destruction or by bursting (apoptosis and necrosis). Others become tolerant by making ==heat shock proteins== (HSP), which protect other proteins from heat damage.

Both routes end in exertional heat injury and heat stroke. These damage the brain and organs through fever, shock, haemorrhage, stroke, rhabdomyolysis and ==DIC== (disseminated intravascular coagulation, widespread clotting inside small vessels).

### 5. Acclimation to heat

Repeated exercise in the heat over several days brings three groups of changes. First, plasma volume rises, because more protein in the plasma raises its ==oncotic pressure==, the pull that holds water inside the vessels. This rise is temporary, back to normal after about ==10 days==, but it buys time for the other adaptations.

Second, heart rate is lower and cardiac output higher for the same work. This supports a greater skin blood flow, so more heat is lost and core temperature stays lower. Third, sweating starts earlier, spreads over more of the body and is ==more dilute==. This improves evaporative heat loss and prevents a dangerous loss of sodium. The set point, the temperature the brain defends, also falls ([[Thermoregulation]]).

### 6. Defending heat in the cold

Cold stress is ==any environmental condition that causes a loss of body heat==. A fall in core or skin temperature triggers the defences described in [[Thermoregulation]]. The POAH (preoptic anterior hypothalamus, the brain's thermostat) brings on peripheral vasoconstriction, nonshivering thermogenesis and shivering, and the cerebral cortex brings on behavioural defences.

Dangerous conditions are hard to define, because heat loss depends on the body as well as the weather. More subcutaneous fat, the fat under the skin, means ==more insulation==, and so does more inactive muscle in the limbs. A ==smaller ratio of body surface area to mass means less heat loss==. That is why a child, with a large surface for its mass, cools faster than an adult. Women have more subcutaneous fat, which is an advantage, but less active muscle to make heat, which is a disadvantage.

### 7. Muscle and fuel in the cold

Cold also changes how muscle works and which fuel it burns. A cooled muscle recruits its fibres differently, so ==contractile force falls==, and its shortening velocity and power fall too. The superficial muscles are affected while the deep ones are spared. As fatigue rises, metabolic heat production falls. So in long endurance exercise, ==running down energy reserves opens the way to hypothermia==, a dangerous fall in core temperature.

Normally a rise in catecholamines, epinephrine (adrenaline) and norepinephrine, raises the use of free fatty acids ([[Fat Catabolism]]). In the cold, catecholamine secretion rises but free fatty acid use does not. The reason is that ==vasoconstriction in subcutaneous fat reduces free fatty acid mobilisation==. Less blood flows through the fat, so fewer fatty acids are released and carried away. Blood glucose is well maintained, but ==muscle glycogen is used faster== ([[Glycogen and Blood Glucose]]). This matters because hypoglycaemia, meaning low blood glucose, ==suppresses shivering==. So running out of carbohydrate removes a key defence against the cold.

### 8. Three patterns of cold adaptation

Repeated cold stress leads to one of three patterns, and two questions decide which. If the cold does not actually cause a loss of body heat, the result is ==habituation==, in which shivering and skin vasoconstriction become blunted. If heat is lost and extra metabolic heat is enough to defend body temperature, the result is ==metabolic acclimatisation==. Shivering thermogenesis is enhanced, and possibly nonshivering thermogenesis too. If heat is lost and extra heat production is not enough, the result is ==insulative acclimatisation==. Skin vasoconstriction is enhanced, and improved muscle blood flow redistributes body heat toward the subcutaneous shell.

The decision tree asks first about Body heat loss, then whether increased metabolic heat production is sufficient. It works like heating a house. If no heat escapes, you turn the heating down. If the boiler keeps up, you run it harder, and if it cannot, you add insulation.

### Summary

> In the heat, ==blood pools in the dilated skin==, so venous return and stroke volume fall. The body compensates by ==constricting the renal and splanchnic beds and raising heart rate==, which reaches about 197 beats per minute at 43 °C against 157 at 26 °C, until cardiac output falls behind. ==Aldosterone retains sodium and ADH retains water==. Heat illnesses run from heat cramps through heat exhaustion (==38.5 to about 40 °C==) and heat injury to heat stroke above 40 °C, marked by ==no sweating and confusion==. Heat stroke follows two linked routes, gut ischaemia with ==endotoxaemia== and direct heat shock to cells, while heat acclimation ==raises plasma volume, lowers heart rate and makes sweat earlier and more dilute==. In the cold, muscle loses force, ==free fatty acid mobilisation falls== and glycogen is used faster, and repeated cold leads to ==habituation, metabolic acclimatisation or insulative acclimatisation==.
