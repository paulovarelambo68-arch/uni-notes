
> The body keeps a small reserve of sugar in the liver and the muscles for when food is not arriving or muscles work hard. This note explains how the reserve is filled and emptied, and how it keeps blood sugar steady. It is the normal system that McArdle disease breaks.

### 1. Glycogen, the store of glucose

The body stores glucose as glycogen, and two processes fill and empty this store. ==Glycogen== is a large, branched molecule built from many glucose units. It is kept mainly in the ==liver and skeletal muscle==. The whole store is small, ==about 0.5 kg or roughly 2,000 kcal==.

The store is filled by ==glycogenesis==, the synthesis of glycogen from surplus glucose. It happens when the cell is not very active or its reserves are depleted, as after a meal or after exercise. The enzyme that adds each glucose unit is ==glycogen synthase==, also called glycogen synthetase. The store is emptied by ==glycogenolysis==, the breakdown of glycogen. This happens when the cell is very active and glucose is running short.

The names follow a simple pattern. Names of breakdown end in ==lysis==, meaning splitting, as in glycogenolysis, glycolysis and lipolysis. Names of synthesis end in ==genesis==, meaning making, as in glycogenesis, gluconeogenesis and lipogenesis.

### 2. Breaking glycogen down

Glycogen is broken down by one key enzyme, ==glycogen phosphorylase==. It removes glucose units one at a time from the ends of the glycogen branches, so each cut leaves the glycogen one unit shorter. To make the cut it uses inorganic phosphate (Pi). The unit therefore comes off already carrying a phosphate, as ==glucose 1-phosphate==.

A second enzyme, ==phosphoglucomutase==, moves this phosphate from carbon 1 to carbon 6 of the glucose, giving glucose 6-phosphate. This is the same molecule made by the first step of [[Glycolysis]], the pathway that splits glucose into pyruvate. Glycolysis normally spends 2 ATP and makes 4, so it nets 2. The first of those 2 ATP is spent putting a phosphate on free glucose. Glucose from glycogen arrives with its phosphate already attached, so it ==nets 3 ATP in glycolysis instead of 2==.

Different tissues make their own versions of glycogen phosphorylase, each from a different gene. The muscle version is called ==myophosphorylase==, and the liver and the brain each have their own. A faulty gene can therefore disable only one version, and the muscle version is the one missing in McArdle disease ([[McArdle Disease Cause and Mechanism]]).

### 3. Liver and muscle use it differently

Glucose 6-phosphate stands at a crossroads with three exits, and the tissue decides which it takes.

- In muscle and brain, it enters glycolysis and becomes pyruvate, which goes on to lactate or to carbon dioxide and water.
- In the liver, the enzyme ==glucose 6-phosphatase== removes the phosphate, giving free glucose that leaves the cell into the blood for other tissues.
- In the pentose phosphate pathway, it makes ribose, a sugar used to build nucleotides, and NADPH, a hydrogen carrier used in building molecules.

The phosphate decides whether glucose can leave a cell, because glucose carrying a phosphate cannot cross the cell membrane. Muscle ==has no glucose 6-phosphatase==, so the glucose of muscle glycogen can never enter the blood. It can only be burned in that muscle or leave as [[Lactate]], which glycolysis forms when pyruvate builds up faster than it is oxidised. In other words, muscle glycogen is a private store for the muscle that holds it. Liver glycogen is a public store for the blood, and ==the only glycogen that can raise blood glucose==.

### 4. Hormones switch the enzymes

The body controls the store by switching its two enzymes on and off. The switch is a phosphate group added to or removed from the enzyme itself, and the two enzymes respond in opposite ways. ==Glycogen phosphorylase is active with the phosphate==, and glycogen synthase is active without it. So one signal can turn breakdown on and synthesis off at the same moment.

The first signal is ==epinephrine== (adrenaline), released in exercise and stress. It binds to a receptor on the cell membrane and activates the enzyme ==adenylate cyclase==, a different enzyme from the adenylate kinase of [[ATP and Phosphocreatine]]. Adenylate cyclase converts ATP into ==cyclic AMP (cAMP)==, a messenger that carries the hormone's signal from the membrane to the enzymes inside the cell. cAMP sets off a series of enzymes that add phosphates to proteins. The result is active glycogen phosphorylase, so breakdown accelerates, and inactive glycogen synthase, so synthesis stops. In the liver, the hormone glucagon acts in the same way.

The second signal is ==insulin==, released after a meal, and it does the opposite. Through its receptor and the insulin receptor substrates, proteins that relay its signal, it switches on protein kinases, enzymes that add phosphates to other proteins. These kinases inactivate ==glycogen synthase kinase==, the enzyme that keeps glycogen synthase switched off. Then ==protein phosphatase 1== removes the phosphate from glycogen synthase. The synthase becomes active, so glycogen synthesis accelerates and the reserves are restored.

### 5. Making new glucose

When liver glycogen runs low, the liver, and to a smaller extent the kidneys, make new glucose from molecules that are not carbohydrates. This is ==gluconeogenesis==, meaning the making of new glucose.

It has three main raw materials. The first is ==lactate==, which returns from muscle to the liver in the Cori cycle, the loop that rebuilds lactate into glucose. The second is ==glycerol==, the backbone released when stored fat is broken down ([[Fat Catabolism]]). The third is the ==glucogenic amino acids==, those whose carbon can become pyruvate once their nitrogen is removed ([[Protein and the Metabolic Mill]]). Fatty acids cannot be used, because their carbon arrives as acetyl CoA, which cannot be turned back into pyruvate.

Gluconeogenesis runs mostly as glycolysis in reverse. Three steps of glycolysis cannot run backwards, so the liver uses different enzymes to get round them. Like pushing a ball back up a slope, this costs energy. Making one glucose ==costs 6 ATP==.

### 6. Keeping blood glucose steady

All these processes keep blood glucose steady, above all for the brain, which takes glucose from the blood all the time and burns it to carbon dioxide.

After a meal, starch and glucose are absorbed from the small intestine and blood glucose rises. The rise makes the pancreas release ==insulin==. Insulin makes the liver store glucose as glycogen. It makes muscle take up glucose and store it as glycogen or burn it, and makes adipose tissue (fat tissue) take up glucose.

Between meals and during exercise the flow reverses. The liver ==breaks down its glycogen and releases glucose into the blood==. It also turns lactate returning from muscle and other tissues back into glucose. Because the liver's store is small, long exercise without food can let blood glucose fall.

### Summary

> ==Glycogen== is the body's small store of glucose, about 0.5 kg or 2,000 kcal, kept in liver and muscle. It is built by glycogen synthase in glycogenesis and broken down by ==glycogen phosphorylase== in glycogenolysis. Phosphorylase uses Pi to release ==glucose 1-phosphate==, which becomes glucose 6-phosphate, so a unit from glycogen nets 3 ATP in glycolysis instead of 2. Only the liver has ==glucose 6-phosphatase==, so muscle glycogen is a private store burned in place, while liver glycogen alone can raise blood glucose. ==Epinephrine acts through cAMP== to switch phosphorylase on and synthase off, and insulin reverses this through protein phosphatase 1 so glycogen is rebuilt after a meal. When liver glycogen runs low, ==gluconeogenesis== makes new glucose from lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acids at a cost of 6 ATP, keeping the brain supplied between meals.
