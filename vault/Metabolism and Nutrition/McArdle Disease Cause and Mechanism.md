
> This note follows one missing enzyme through the energy pathways of muscle. It shows how a muscle full of fuel that it cannot open ends up tired, cramped and stiff, and at worst harms the kidneys.

### 1. What McArdle disease is

McArdle disease is an inherited disorder in which skeletal muscle cannot break down glycogen, its store of linked glucose units. Its formal name is ==glycogen storage disease type V==, and it is also called muscle phosphorylase deficiency. Brian McArdle described it after a young man's blood lactate failed to rise with exercise. He concluded that the man's muscle could not break down its glycogen (McArdle, 1951). The disease is rare, affecting about ==1 person in 100,000==.

The cause is a fault in ==the PYGM gene on chromosome 11==. This gene encodes ==myophosphorylase==, the muscle version of glycogen phosphorylase, which is the enzyme that releases glucose from glycogen ([[Glycogen and Blood Glucose]]). About 100 different faults in the gene had been described by 2012 (Leite et al., 2012). The disease is ==autosomal recessive==. Autosomal means the gene lies on an ordinary chromosome, not a sex chromosome. Recessive means a person is affected only when both copies of the gene, one from each parent, are faulty. Parents with one faulty copy are healthy carriers, so there is often no family history.

The liver and heart versions of the enzyme come from other genes and are unaffected. So ==liver glycogen metabolism is normal== and only skeletal muscle suffers (Leite et al., 2012). The main effect is ==painful muscle cramps on exertion==. They arise because the muscle cannot get enough fuel for [[Glycolysis]], the fast pathway that splits glucose to make ATP.

### 2. A patient's story

A 54 year old man came to physical therapy with lifelong fatigue that worsened on exertion. As a child he struggled uphill, lagged behind his friends and was called lazy. Several doctors could not explain his symptoms, which deepened his unhappiness and led to depression. Climbing stairs brought on early fatigue and contractures, and after walking for a while his legs became stiff like a board until he rested. He was worse when hungry and better after meals. No relative had a neuromuscular disease, and he was diagnosed about ==40 years== after his symptoms began (Leite et al., 2012).

His story holds three clues. Effort brings the symptoms on, rest relieves them, and food eases them.

### 3. Where the pathway is blocked

The missing enzyme acts at the very first step of glycogen breakdown. Normally myophosphorylase snips glucose units off glycogen as glucose 1-phosphate. This is converted to glucose 6-phosphate, the first molecule of glycolysis. Without the enzyme, that first step stops. Glycogen synthase, the enzyme that builds glycogen, still works, so ==glycogen piles up in the fibres==.

Glycolysis itself is intact, but it is ==blocked upstream== and starved of glycogen, its main fuel in hard exercise. One route stays open. The muscle can still take up ==glucose from the blood== and convert it to glucose 6-phosphate, which enters glycolysis below the block (Leite et al., 2012). This is why food helps. After a meal more glucose arrives through this side entrance, and when the patient is hungry the muscle has little to burn. This open route underlies every treatment ([[McArdle Disease Diagnosis and Management]]).

Two results follow further down the pathway. Little pyruvate is made, so the muscle produces ==almost no lactate== during exercise ([[Lactate]]). Less pyruvate also means less acetyl CoA, the two carbon fuel of the [[Krebs Cycle]], and fewer of the cycle's own intermediates. The cycle then hands fewer electron carriers, NADH and FADH2, to [[Oxidative Phosphorylation]], the oxygen dependent process that makes most ATP. So the disease combines ==glycogen overload, blocked glycolysis and a substrate deficit in oxidative phosphorylation== (Santalla et al., 2014).

Fat cannot fill the gap either. Fats burn in a carbohydrate flame, because burning fat needs the Krebs cycle intermediates that carbohydrate supplies ([[Protein and the Metabolic Mill]]). So a muscle that cannot use its glycogen ==cannot make up the difference by burning fat==.

### 4. The energy crisis of the first minutes

The block matters most at the start of exercise and in any intense effort. Muscle then relies first on phosphocreatine (PCr), a store of high energy phosphate that remakes ATP within seconds ([[ATP and Phosphocreatine]]), and then on glycogen. Symptoms typically begin within about ==10 seconds== of strenuous exercise, just when glycogen should take over. ATP cannot be remade as fast as it is spent, glycolytic activity slows and ==ADP accumulates==.

This build up has been measured with ==phosphorus NMR spectroscopy (31P NMR)==. This scan measures phosphorus compounds such as ATP, ADP and phosphocreatine inside living muscle. In healthy muscle, ADP rises from a very low resting level to about ==50 micromolar== (millionths of a mole per litre) in light or heavy exercise. In a patient, light exercise that leads to cramps drives it to about ==240 micromolar==.

The excess ADP sets off a chain of reactions. The enzyme adenylate kinase turns two ADP into ATP and adenosine monophosphate (AMP), ==2 ADP → ATP + AMP==. This squeezes out a little more ATP. The AMP is then broken down to IMP (inosine monophosphate) and ammonia. That is why ==ammonia rises== in these patients, and these breakdown products add to cell damage.

The muscle also turns alkaline. Remaking ATP from phosphocreatine uses up hydrogen ions, which makes the cell less acid. In healthy muscle the lactic acid of fast glycolysis outweighs this, so a hard working muscle turns more acid. In McArdle disease no lactic acid is formed, so the muscle becomes ==more alkaline during strenuous exercise== instead.

### 5. From low ATP to symptoms

Low ATP becomes symptoms because four processes in the muscle fibre need ATP, and each failure can be felt (Santalla et al., 2014). The first is the ==sodium potassium pump== of the sarcolemma, the membrane around the fibre. It uses ATP to bring back the potassium that leaks out with each electrical impulse. When it slows, potassium builds up outside the fibre (hyperkalaemia). The membrane becomes ==less excitable==, so the muscle responds weakly and tires early.

The second is ==SERCA1==, the calcium pump of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, the fibre's internal calcium store. It uses ATP to return calcium to the store after each contraction, which lets the muscle relax ([[Excitation–Contraction Coupling – Calcium Cycle and Sympathetic Stimulation]]). When it slows, calcium lingers and ==relaxation is slow==. The muscle stays shortened and hard. This is a ==contracture==, a muscle that cannot relax for lack of ATP, and it explains the legs stiff like a board.

The third is the sarcomere, the contractile unit of the fibre. Its filaments are linked by cross bridges, which need ATP to let go and pull again. With less ATP ==contractility falls==, so more fibres must be recruited for the same force and every task feels harder.

The fourth is the breakdown of surplus ADP through AMP to IMP. Besides ammonia, this route releases reactive oxygen species (ROS), unstable molecules that cause ==oxidative damage== to the fibre.

Rest relieves all four. At rest the use of ATP falls, so the slow supply catches up, ADP falls, the pumps recover and the muscle relaxes.

### 6. Symptoms and complications

Together these mechanisms produce a recognisable clinical picture. The earliest and most common signs are ==exercise intolerance and fatigability==, usually present since childhood. Others are muscle pain (myalgia), stiffness, cramping and contractures. There is also the ==second wind==, a striking improvement after some minutes of gentle exercise ([[McArdle Disease Diagnosis and Management]]). Strenuous activity brings abrupt exhaustion and a fast rise in heart rate, as the body tries to deliver more fuel in the blood. This is called ==premature exhaustion==.

Under extreme stress the muscle fails. It can no longer contract whatever the effort, it cramps and it can be damaged. Damaged fibres break open because their energy supply has failed, which is ==rhabdomyolysis==. They spill their contents into the blood. Among them are the enzyme creatine kinase and ==myoglobin==, the oxygen binding protein of muscle ([[Unloading Oxygen and Carrying Carbon Dioxide – Bohr Effect, 2,3-DPG, Myoglobin and Bicarbonate]]). The kidneys filter myoglobin into the urine and colour it red, which is ==myoglobinuria==, and myoglobin can injure the kidneys.

Myoglobinuria occurs in ==about half of patients== after intense exercise. About half of those develop ==acute kidney failure==, which is almost always reversible but needs emergency treatment (Leite et al., 2012). The dangerous efforts are ==static contractions==, such as carrying a heavy load, and intense dynamic exercise (Leite et al., 2012). Repeated damage can leave a ==fixed weakness==, a persistent weakness that makes strength hard to gain. Every one of these problems traces back to a muscle full of fuel that it cannot open.

### Summary

> McArdle disease is an ==autosomal recessive== fault in the PYGM gene on chromosome 11, so skeletal muscle lacks myophosphorylase while the liver enzyme works normally. Muscle cannot release its glycogen, so glycogen piles up and ==glycolysis is blocked upstream==. Only blood glucose can still enter glycolysis, at ==glucose 6-phosphate==, which is why food eases the symptoms. Little pyruvate means ==almost no lactate==, and too little acetyl CoA starves the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, a gap that fat cannot fill. Within about ==10 seconds== of hard effort ATP cannot keep up, so ADP climbs to about 240 micromolar, ammonia rises and the cell turns alkaline. Low ATP slows the sodium potassium pump and ==SERCA1==, causing weakness and contracture, while weak cross bridges force more recruitment and the ADP breakdown route causes oxidative damage. Rest lets the supply catch up. Extreme effort, however, causes ==rhabdomyolysis== and myoglobinuria, which can lead to acute kidney failure and, after repeated damage, fixed weakness.
