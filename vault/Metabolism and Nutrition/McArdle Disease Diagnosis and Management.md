
> McArdle disease is a muscle disorder in which exercise quickly becomes painful and exhausting. This note follows how doctors recognise and confirm it, how patients learn to fuel their muscles another way, and what future treatments may offer.

### 1. Suspecting the disease

The diagnosis begins with a pattern of four features (Leite et al., 2012).

- Exercise intolerance, meaning early fatigue, pain and stiffness during effort, has usually been present since childhood, and it is ==partly relieved by glucose==.
- The blood level of creatine kinase, an enzyme that belongs inside muscle, is ==high even at rest==.
- There have been episodes of ==very high creatine kinase after intense exercise==.
- The patient shows the ==second wind==, a sudden easing of exercise after about 10 minutes of gentle effort.

Because these complaints sound like laziness or poor fitness, the diagnosis is often delayed for decades. All four features come from one fault, explained in [[McArdle Disease Cause and Mechanism]]. The muscle lacks ==myophosphorylase==, the enzyme that releases glucose from glycogen, its internal store of glucose. Glucose from the blood enters glycolysis, the pathway that splits glucose for energy, below the blocked step. That is why sugar helps.

### 2. The creatine kinase test

The first test is a simple blood test. Creatine kinase (CK, also called creatine phosphokinase) is the enzyme that uses phosphocreatine to rebuild ATP inside muscle fibres ([[ATP and Phosphocreatine]]). It belongs inside the fibres, so little of it normally reaches the blood. When fibres are damaged, their membranes break and the enzyme leaks out. A high blood level therefore reports ==muscle damage==.

A normal value is below about 200 units per litre. In one reported patient it was ==7,924 units per litre==, and myoglobin, the oxygen storing protein of muscle, had leaked out as well (Leite et al., 2012). The test shows that muscle is being damaged, but not why.

### 3. The forearm exercise test

The forearm test asks whether the muscle can turn its own glycogen into lactate. In the ==ischaemic forearm test==, a blood pressure cuff on the upper arm is inflated to cut off the blood supply. Ischaemic means deprived of blood flow. The patient then squeezes repeatedly with the hand. With no blood arriving, the forearm muscles get no oxygen and no outside fuel. They must work on their own glycogen, which glycolysis turns into lactate when oxygen is lacking ([[Lactate]]).

Once the cuff is released, blood is taken from a vein of that arm. In healthy people its lactate rises steeply, from about 15 to about 45 mg per 100 mL, and returns towards its starting level over the next 15 to 20 minutes. In a patient lactate ==stays flat==, because glycogen cannot feed glycolysis.

Ammonia does rise, however, because a muscle short of energy breaks down part of its surplus ADP, which releases ammonia. This proves that the muscle really worked, so the flat lactate is not due to weak effort. The test is painful, and it can give false negative and false positive results (Leite et al., 2012).

A related measurement records the fatigue itself. During repeated contractions, both force and the ==M wave== are followed. The M wave is the electrical response of the muscle to a stimulus, so it shows how excitable the muscle membrane is. In patients both fall to about half within about a minute and a half. Healthy controls take about ==twice as long==.

### 4. Biopsy and genetic testing

Two further tests look directly at the stored glycogen and at the faulty gene. In a ==needle biopsy==, a hollow needle takes a small sample of muscle, usually from the thigh or the upper arm. Under the microscope the fibres show ==lakes of glycogen== just under their membrane. A stain that colours myophosphorylase stays completely negative, which confirms that the enzyme is absent (Leite et al., 2012).

![[McA_slide11_biopsy_glycogen_lakes.png]]

The picture shows muscle fibres cut across and stained pink. In healthy muscle each fibre would be evenly filled. Here pale, empty looking spaces lie at the edges of several fibres, just under the membrane. These are the lakes of glycogen, fuel that is stored but cannot be released, like grain in a silo with no door.

Genetic testing looks at the cause itself. Myophosphorylase is built from the instructions in the ==PYGM gene==, and a patient carries faults in both copies. Finding them settles the question, so the diagnosis now rests in the end on genetic study (Lucia et al., 2021).

### 5. The second wind

The second wind shows how the muscle gets round the block. It is a sudden, marked improvement in the tolerance of gentle aerobic exercise after ==about 10 minutes==. The fatigue, breathlessness and racing heart of the first minutes ease, and the exercise becomes comfortable. It is ==found only in McArdle disease== (Leite et al., 2012) and was first described by Pearson and colleagues (Pearson et al., 1961).

A recording of one patient shows it clearly (Haller and Vissing, 2002). A 29 year old woman with no myophosphorylase cycled at a light load of about 30 watts. Her heart rate climbed to about 170 beats per minute in the first minutes. By 12 minutes it had fallen to about 125, although the load had not changed. That fall is the spontaneous second wind. Raising the load to 55 watts made her heart rate climb again. Glucose given into a vein at 22 minutes brought it down once more, a ==second second wind==, until the load was raised to 70 watts.

![[McA_slide13_second_wind.png]]

The upper trace is heart rate in beats per minute (left axis) and the lower trace is work in watts (right axis), both against time in minutes. Open squares mark the initial period, half filled squares the spontaneous second wind, and filled squares the period after glucose. Read the upper trace against the lower one. Heart rate falls at about 10 minutes while the work stays low, rises with each step up in work, and falls after glucose. Here heart rate acts as a gauge of how hard the muscle is struggling.

The mechanism is a switch of fuel. The muscle moves from the blocked glycolytic route to the ==oxidative phosphorylation of fuels that arrive in the blood==, the oxygen using process that makes most of the cell's ATP ([[Oxidative Phosphorylation]]). After some minutes of exercise, blood flow to the muscle has risen. Free fatty acids released from fat tissue are arriving ([[Fat Catabolism]]), and more glucose is taken up from the blood (Leite et al., 2012). The muscle burns these fuels, so its ADP falls and its membrane pumps recover.

The glucose experiment proves the point, because fuel supplied through the blood gave the same relief at once. Patients are therefore taught to start every activity gently for about 10 minutes, so that they reach their second wind before asking more of the muscle.

### 6. Treatment today

There is ==no cure==, so treatment feeds the muscle through the blood and trains it to use that fuel.

Sugar before exercise is the quickest help. Sucrose, which is table sugar, raises blood glucose, and that glucose enters glycolysis below the block. In 12 patients, 75 g of sucrose in a drink taken 30 to 40 minutes before 15 minutes of cycling lowered heart rate by ==up to 34 beats per minute==. Exercise tolerance improved markedly in every patient (Vissing and Haller, 2003).

A later study tried a smaller dose taken closer to exercise (Andersen et al., 2008). Six patients cycled for 15 minutes after an overnight fast. With a placebo, a dummy drink, heart rate climbed to about 147 beats per minute at 7 minutes. Perceived exertion reached about 16 on the ==Borg scale==, which runs from 6, least effort, to 20, most effort. Both then fell as the second wind arrived.

With ==37 g of sucrose taken 5 minutes before== exercise, heart rate stayed near 110 beats per minute and exertion near 9. This effect lasted longer than that of 75 g taken 40 minutes before. The smaller dose is also more convenient and adds fewer calories, which matters because sugar before every activity can cause weight gain.

Filled squares are the placebo, filled circles sucrose taken 5 minutes before, and open circles sucrose taken 40 minutes before. The placebo line makes a hump peaking at about 7 minutes, which is the struggle before the second wind. Both sucrose lines stay low and flat, because the sugar supplies in advance the fuel that the second wind would bring late. It is like filling the tank before setting off.

Diet does the same job across the whole day. A diet high in complex carbohydrate, such as bread, rice and pasta, at about ==65 per cent of energy==, and low in fat, at about 20 per cent, keeps blood glucose available (Leite et al., 2012).

Exercise is the other half of treatment. Regular, supervised ==aerobic exercise of low to moderate intensity== is safe and recommended. Heavy static contractions and exercise that causes severe muscle pain should be avoided (Leite et al., 2012). A sustained contraction squeezes the muscle's own blood vessels and so cuts off the supply the patient depends on. Patients were once told to avoid exercise, but careful exercise prescription is now regarded as their ==best ally== (Santalla et al., 2014). Training raises the capacity of muscle to burn fuel from the blood, so daily tasks sit further below its limit.

Physical therapy teaches patients to apply this safely. It covers the long gentle start, pacing, and stopping at the first pain or stiffness. It also teaches them to seek ==urgent care if the urine turns dark== after exercise, a sign that damaged muscle is releasing myoglobin.

Supplements have been tried as well, but the benefits of ==creatine, vitamin B6 and ACE inhibitors== (blood pressure drugs) remain unproven (Leite et al., 2012).

### 7. Future approaches

Every treatment so far works around the block, but two experimental ideas aim at the cause. The first is to induce the expression of glycogen phosphorylase in muscle, which means switching on another version of the enzyme there. The body makes separate versions of phosphorylase for different tissues ([[Glycogen and Blood Glucose]]), and regenerating muscle fibres can make the brain version. The drug ==sodium valproate== increased it in animal studies. In an open label study of 16 patients treated for six months, however, it showed ==no benefit== (Scalco et al., 2020). Open label means that everyone knew which treatment was given.

The second idea is ==gene therapy==, which delivers a working copy of the gene to muscle inside a harmless virus. In a mouse model of the disease this restored the enzyme, reduced the build up of glycogen and returned voluntary running to normal (McNamara et al., 2020). Both approaches are still experimental. Until one succeeds, diet, sugar before effort and well judged exercise remain the treatment.

### Summary

> McArdle disease is suspected when lifelong exercise intolerance that ==glucose partly relieves== comes with a high creatine kinase at rest, spikes after hard effort and a second wind. Every feature follows from the lack of ==myophosphorylase==, which leaves muscle unable to release glucose from its own glycogen. A high creatine kinase in the blood reports ==muscle damage==. In the ischaemic forearm test lactate ==stays flat== while ammonia rises, and force and the M wave fail about twice as fast as normal. A needle biopsy shows ==lakes of glycogen== and no stain for the enzyme, and faults in both copies of the PYGM gene settle the diagnosis. After about 10 minutes of gentle exercise the ==second wind== arrives, because rising blood flow brings fatty acids and glucose that the muscle burns by oxidative phosphorylation. Treatment uses this open route through the blood: ==37 g of sucrose 5 minutes before exercise==, a diet with about 65 per cent of energy from complex carbohydrate, and regular low to moderate aerobic training. Creatine, vitamin B6 and ACE inhibitors remain unproven, sodium valproate showed ==no benefit== and gene therapy has worked only in mice, so fuel through the blood remains the basis of care.
