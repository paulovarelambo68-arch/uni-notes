
> The **aphasias** are ==language disorders that typically follow a stroke or lesion in the relevant area==. Those same areas are the stations of the two speaking pathways. Whether repeating a heard word or reading a written word aloud, speech runs through them as an ordered sequence that ends at the motor cortex.

### Aphasias

**Broca's aphasia** follows a lesion of Broca's area: ==laboured, slow speech in monotone with impaired articulation==, while comprehension survives. **Wernicke's aphasia** follows a lesion of Wernicke's area and reverses the pattern: ==fluent, melodic speech, but with errors in word choice and difficulty in comprehending==. **Conduction aphasia** reflects damage to the connection between the two areas rather than to either area itself, so ==simple speech is intelligible and comprehension remains intact==, but ==repeating sentences is difficult== because repetition must relay directly from Wernicke's area to Broca's area.

### Speaking a heard word

![[Screenshot 2026-10-02 at 20.31.14 2.png]]

The sound of the word is received by the auditory cortex (1), passed to Wernicke's area (2) to be understood, relayed to Broca's area (3) to plan the utterance, and delivered to the motor cortex (4) for execution: ==auditory cortex → Wernicke's area → Broca's area → motor cortex==. The function of each area is set out in [[Language and Hemisphere Dominance]].

### Speaking a written word

![[Screenshot 2026-10-02 at 20.31.29 2.png]]

Reading aloud adds two steps at the front of the same route. The written word is received by the visual cortex (1) and converted by the angular gyrus (2) into its spoken form, from which point it follows the heard word sequence exactly, through Wernicke's area (3) and Broca's area (4) to the motor cortex (5): ==visual cortex → angular gyrus → Wernicke's area → Broca's area → motor cortex==. The auditory cortex isn't needed in this case.

### Summary

> Aphasias are language disorders that follow a stroke or lesion of the relevant area: Broca's aphasia gives laboured, slow speech in monotone, Wernicke's aphasia gives fluent, melodic speech with errors in word choice and poor comprehension, and conduction aphasia impairs repetition alone. Speaking a heard word runs auditory cortex → Wernicke's area → Broca's area → motor cortex; speaking a written word begins at the visual cortex, passes through the angular gyrus and then joins the same route.
