
> The **auditory pathway** is the chain of stations that carries the signal generated in the cochlea to the cerebral cortex: ==from the auditory nerve to the brain stem, then to the medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and on to the auditory cortex==. Because most fibres cross in the brain stem, the representation of each ear is ==mainly contralateral==, though some fibres from each ear also reach both sides of the brain.

### From the cochlea to the cortex

 ==The inner hair cells synapse on more than 90% of the ganglion cells== (The remaining ganglion cell afferents synapse with the **outer hair cells**, whose connections are almost entirely [[Hair Cells and Cochlear Transduction | efferent]]), so virtually all auditory information reaching the brain comes from this one receptor population. The ganglion cell axons form the **auditory nerve** then carry the signal to the **brain stem**, from where it ascends to the **medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus** and on to the **auditory cortex**. The medial geniculate nucleus is the final thalamic relay for hearing, the equivalent of the [[The Lateral Geniculate Nucleus|lateral geniculate nucleus]] in vision.

### Decussation, collaterals and retrograde fibres

Within the brain stem, ==the majority of fibres decussate==, meaning they cross to the opposite side, so the signal carried onward is ==contralateral in the main== and each hemisphere deals chiefly with the opposite ear. Yet the crossing is not complete: ==some fibres from each ear go to both sides of the brain==, so every station above the brain stem receives input from both ears.

Along the way, the ascending fibres give off side branches: ==collaterals innervate the reticular activating system and the vermis of the cerebellum==. These mediate ==general sensory and motor responses to sound== rather than perception: the **reticular activating system** produces arousal, the alerting reaction to a sound, while the **vermis of the cerebellum** handles the motor side, coordinating the body's response to it.

The pathway is not one way: ==inhibitory retrograde fibres run from each level of the auditory system back to the cochlea==, so each level can dampen the levels below it, right down to the receptor itself. Frequency is mapped in order along the cochlea, so the descending fibres act on selected frequency channels rather than the whole cochlea: at the cochlear end they end on the **outer hair cells** of their own region, turning down the cochlear amplifier for just those frequencies. Suppressing the unwanted bands raises the attended sound above the background, ==allowing tuning in to a specific sound==. It is the same general strategy as [[Lateral Inhibition and Adaptation|lateral inhibition]] in the somatosensory system, improving the quality of sensory information by silencing what surrounds the signal, except that here the inhibition is not fixed between neighbouring pathways but directed from higher levels and shifted as needed.

### The auditory cortex

The figure shows the brain in lateral view, with a region of the temporal lobe boxed and magnified. In the magnified strip the **primary auditory cortex** appears in blue, flanked in red by the **secondary auditory cortex**, labelled belt areas because they ring the primary cortex like a belt. Across the blue region the represented frequencies are laid out in sequence, ==500, 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000 and 16,000 Hz==, the low frequency end ==corresponding to the apex of the cochlea== and the high frequency end ==corresponding to the base of the cochlea==: the frequency map of the cochlea is redrawn on the cortex.

The **primary auditory cortex** therefore has a **tonotopic organisation**: ==its neurones respond to specific frequencies of sound==, and neighbouring positions represent neighbouring frequencies. Like the [[The Primary Visual Cortex|visual cortex]], the sheet is also divided into columns, and here the columns alternate in how the two ears drive them: one is excited by both ears, the next is excited by one ear and inhibited by the other, so ==alternating columns are responsive to input from both ears==. Comparing the two ears column by column gives the cortex the differences between them, and these support ==sound localisation==.

The surrounding areas, the secondary auditory cortex, are involved with the ==elaboration of other aspects of sound== beyond frequency, and their output divides into two pathways: a ==parietal pathway== for ==direction==, where the sound comes from, and a ==temporal pathway== for ==meaning==, what the sound is.

### Summary

> Almost all auditory information reaching the brain originates at the inner hair cells, which synapse on more than 90% of the ganglion cells, and the signal ascends from the auditory nerve through the brain stem and the medial geniculate nucleus to the auditory cortex, mostly crossing in the brain stem yet with some fibres from each ear reaching both sides. Collaterals to the reticular activating system and the cerebellar vermis give general sensory and motor responses to sound, while inhibitory retrograde fibres from every level back to the cochlea let the system tune in to a specific sound. The primary auditory cortex keeps the cochlear frequency map in tonotopic form and compares the two ears in alternating columns for sound localisation, and the surrounding areas elaborate sound further along a parietal direction pathway and a temporal meaning pathway.
