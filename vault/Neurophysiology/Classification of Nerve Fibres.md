
## What the classification is based on

Three linked properties determine how fast a fibre conducts:

- **Diameter:** the larger the axon, the lower its internal resistance, so the faster the conduction.
- **Myelination:** myelin insulates the axon and forces the action potential to jump between nodes of Ranvier (saltatory conduction), which is much faster than the continuous conduction of unmyelinated fibres.
- **Conduction velocity:** the measurable result of the two above. Rule of thumb for myelinated fibres: velocity (m/s) is roughly 6 times the diameter (µm).

==Key logic to remember:== **thick + myelin = fastest, thin + no myelin = slowest, and between those extremes myelin tips the balance toward faster.**

## The two naming systems

- **General classification (Erlanger and Gasser):** A, B, C. Applies to both motor and sensory fibres. A is subdivided into α, β, γ, δ.
- **Sensory classification (Lloyd and Hunt):** I, II, III, IV. Applies only to afferent (sensory) fibres.

They describe the same fibres with different labels: **I ≈ Aα afferents, II ≈ Aβ, III ≈ Aδ, IV ≈ C.**

## The fibre types

**Aα (alpha) — the largest and fastest**
- Myelinated, 12 to 20 µm, 70 to 120 m/s.
- Motor arm: alpha motor neurons supplying **extrafusal skeletal muscle fibres** (the fibres that actually produce force).
- Sensory arm (= Group I): **Ia** from [[muscle spindle]] primary endings (signal muscle length and speed of stretch) and **Ib** from Golgi tendon organs (signal muscle tension).
- Role: movement and proprioception.

**Aβ (beta)**
- Myelinated, 5 to 12 µm, 30 to 70 m/s.
- Afferents for **touch and pressure** from skin mechanoreceptors, plus secondary spindle endings.
- Equivalent to sensory **Group II**.

**Aγ (gamma)**
- Myelinated, 2 to 8 µm, 10 to 50 m/s.
- Motor only: gamma motor neurons to **intrafusal muscle fibres** of the spindle. They do not produce movement; they **adjust spindle sensitivity** so it keeps working at all muscle lengths.

**Aδ (delta)**
- Thinly myelinated, 1 to 5 µm, 3 to 30 m/s.
- Carries **fast pain** (sharp, pricking, well localised, the "first pain" you feel immediately after injury) and **cold**.
- Equivalent to sensory **Group III**.

**B**
- Myelinated but thin, 1 to 3 µm, 5 to 15 m/s.
- **Preganglionic autonomic** fibres. Classic exam point: although myelinated, they are slower than most A fibres, showing that classification reflects function as well as speed.

**C — the smallest and slowest**
- **Unmyelinated**, under 1 µm, under 2 m/s.
- **Postganglionic autonomic** fibres, plus afferents for **slow pain** (dull, burning, poorly localised "second pain"), warmth and temperature in general.
- Equivalent to sensory **Group IV** (sometimes labelled IV directly).

## Points you are likely to be asked

- **Why fast fibres are fast:** large diameter lowers internal resistance; myelin enables saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.
- **Fast vs slow pain:** Aδ gives immediate, sharp, localised pain; C gives delayed, dull, diffuse pain. Same stimulus, two fibre types.
- **Motor vs sensory division within A:** Aα and Aγ are the motor types (extrafusal vs intrafusal fibres); Aδ is sensory (pain, cold); Aβ is sensory (touch, pressure).
- **Autonomic fibres:** B before the ganglion (myelinated), C after it (unmyelinated).
- **Clinical relevance:** local anaesthetics block small fibres first, so pain disappears before touch; conversely, loss of large fibre function (Aα, Aβ) impairs proprioception, vibration sense and reflexes, which matters for movement control in clinical exercise settings.
