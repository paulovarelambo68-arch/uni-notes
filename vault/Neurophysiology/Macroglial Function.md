
>Macroglia are the glial cells derived from neural stem cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells, ependymal cells and radial glia). They are not passive support: they build the brain during development, maintain its environment, and actively take part in signalling between neurons.

### 1. Support and insulation

- **==Structural support:==** macroglia form the physical framework that holds neurons in place.
- **==Insulate axons:==** they wrap axons with myelin (Schwann cells in the PNS, oligodendrocytes in the CNS), which isolates the axon and speeds up conduction.

### 2. Blood brain barrier

The blood brain barrier is the selective wall between the blood and the brain's tissue: the endothelial cells of brain capillaries are sealed together by **==tight junctions==**, so most substances cannot pass between them and the brain is shielded from toxins and fluctuations in the blood. The macroglial contribution comes from astrocytes: the terminal swellings of their processes, the **==astrocytic end feet==**, wrap around the outside of these capillaries and are required for the **==formation and maintenance==** of the barrier.
### 3. Nourishing and protecting neurons

Macroglia release [[growth factors]] that nourish and protect neurons. These are the **==neurotrophins==**, for example **==GDNF==** (glial derived neurotrophic factor).

### 4. Role in the anatomical development of the brain

- **==Guide migrating neurones and axon outgrowth:==** 

  1. **Neuronal migration.** **==Radial glia==** stretch long processes across the whole thickness of the developing brain, from the ventricular zone (where new neurons are born) up to the [[pial surface]], forming a scaffold. Migrating neurons press against these processes and climb along them to reach their final position, for example in the cortex or hippocampus (in the cerebellum the same job is done by Bergmann glia).
  
 2. **Axon guidance.** A growing axon ends in an exploratory tip called the **==growth cone==**, which probes the environment with fine processes and reads molecular signals that can be adhesive, attractive or repulsive. This is what the image shows: the pink structure is the growth cone, and the glial cell beside it is releasing attractive signals (the coloured dots). The growth cone detects them and extends toward their source, so glia actively steer axons toward their correct targets. ### 5. Promoting efficient signalling between neurons

Macroglia make synaptic transmission work cleanly, in two ways:

1. **==Clear neurotransmitter from synapses==**, for example glutamate. Removing transmitter from the cleft terminates the signal and prevents it from accumulating around neurons.
2. **==Respond to neuronal signalling by releasing glial factors==**. Glia detect what neurons are doing and answer back chemically, so communication at the synapse is two way. There are three diffuse modulatory systems: **==NAdr (noradrenaline):==**, **==Ach (acetylcholine):==** and **==DA (dopamine):==**, whose widespread projections through the brain are sensed by glia.
![[Screenshot 2026-09-20 at 14.24.49.png]]

The image shows three transmitter systems that share the same design: a small cluster of neurones in the brainstem or basal forebrain sends axons that fan out and branch widely across the whole brain. Instead of carrying precise point to point messages, they bathe large regions in their transmitter and tune the overall state of the brain, and glia sense this activity and respond by releasing glial factors.

The molecules glia release are called **==gliotransmitters==**, because they are the very same molecules neurons use as neurotransmitters, mainly **==glutamate==**, **==GABA==** and **==ATP==** (D serine is another classic example).

### Summary

>Macroglia support and insulate neurons, form the blood brain barrier, nourish neurons with neurotrophins such as GDNF, guide migrating neurons and growing axons during development, drive synaptogenesis, and keep synaptic signalling efficient by clearing neurotransmitter (e.g. glutamate) and by releasing glial factors in response to neuronal activity.
