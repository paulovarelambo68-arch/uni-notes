

>Nociceptors are the free nerve endings that detect noxious, potentially tissue damaging stimuli and give rise to pain. They fall into two classes distinguished by their axons and stimulus selectivity: ==Aδ nociceptors, about 20%, small diameter myelinated fibres responding to mechanical stimuli and tissue injury==, and ==C nociceptors, about 80%, small diameter unmyelinated fibres that are polymodal==, responding to mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli. Their endings carry ==ion channel receptors tuned to each stimulus type==, and their sensitivity is raised by ==inflammatory mediators that reduce the pain threshold of the nerve==.

### 1. Aδ nociceptors

Aδ nociceptors make up about ==20% of nociceptors==. They respond to ==mechanical stimuli and tissue injury==, and their axons are ==small diameter myelinated fibres==, so they conduct relatively fast. The pain they produce is ==sharp, prickling, bright and well localised==, the immediate "Ooch!!" that comes with ==[[withdrawal]]== from the stimulus.

### 2. C nociceptors

C nociceptors make up the remaining ==80%==. They are ==polymodal==, meaning a single ending responds to ==mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli==. Their axons are ==small diameter unmyelinated fibres==, the slowest afferents in [[Sensory Receptors and Afferent Types]], and the pain they carry is ==slow, burning, aching and diffuse==, the lingering second pain that follows the sharp first impression. The classic demonstration of this polymodality is ==capsaicin, the irritant of hot peppers, which activates polymodal C nociceptors through TRPV1==, the same channel opened by acid and by tissue heating, which is why chilli is perceived as burning heat rather than a taste.

### 3. Ion channel receptors and firing

The free nerve endings of nociceptors express ==ion channel receptors== that transduce each class of noxious stimulus into a [[graded potential]]. The second figure maps each stimulus to its channels. When a noxious stimulus opens these channels, ==Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ flow into the ending and depolarise it==; if this depolarisation reaches threshold, the voltage gated sodium channels of the nociceptor, ==NaV1.8 and NaV1.9==, drive [[The Action Potential|action potentials]] that travel along the axon. The firing inset on the slide makes the coding point: ==firing frequency rises with stimulus intensity==, so the stronger the noxious stimulus, the higher the rate of action potentials sent to the spinal cord.

> **EXAM NOTE**
> The individual names of all of these pain ion channels do not need to be memorised. What matters is that ==each stimulus type has its own channels==, that ==opening them depolarises the ending==, and that ==NaV1.8 and NaV1.9 drive the action potentials==.

### 4. Inflammatory mediators

Tissue injury sets off a self reinforcing cascade around the nociceptor ending, and ==the mediators involved act to reduce the pain threshold of the nerve==. ==Damaged cells release their intracellular contents, including potassium, serotonin and bradykinin, which activate the nociceptor directly==. The activated ending in turn ==releases substance P==, which feeds back onto two targets: it ==stimulates [[mast cells]] to [[degranulate]] and release [[histamine]]==, and it ==dilates nearby blood vessels==, producing the redness and swelling of inflammation. Histamine adds further activation of the nociceptor, closing the loop.

In parallel, the membranes of the injured cells supply the ==sensitising arm== of the process. Phospholipase converts membrane phospholipids into arachidonic acid, which is then taken down two enzymatic paths: ==cyclooxygenase produces prostaglandins== and ==lipoxygenase produces leukotrienes==. Unlike the mediators above, ==prostaglandins and leukotrienes do not activate the nociceptor themselves; they sensitise it==, lowering its threshold so that stimuli that would normally be subthreshold now produce pain. This is why ==[[COX inhibitors]] work as analgesics==: by blocking cyclooxygenase they cut prostaglandin synthesis and remove the sensitisation.

### Summary

> Nociceptors are free nerve endings for noxious stimuli and come in two classes: Aδ nociceptors (20%, small myelinated fibres, mechanical stimuli and tissue injury, sharp prickling well localised pain with withdrawal) and C nociceptors (80%, small unmyelinated polymodal fibres responding to mechanical, thermal and chemical stimuli, slow burning aching diffuse pain, the same ending activated by capsaicin, acid and heat through TRPV1). Their endings carry ion channel receptors matched to each stimulus: TRPV1, TRPV2, TRPV3 and TREK1 for heat, TRPV1, ASIC and DRASIC for chemical stimuli such as acid, MDEG, DRASIC and TREK1 for mechanical stimuli, and TRPM8 for cold. Opening these channels admits Na⁺ and Ca²⁺, depolarising the ending until NaV1.8 and NaV1.9 fire action potentials whose frequency codes stimulus intensity. Inflammation then lowers the pain threshold through two arms: damaged cells release potassium, serotonin and bradykinin that activate the nociceptor, substance P from the ending drives mast cells to release histamine and dilates blood vessels, while prostaglandins and leukotrienes, synthesised from arachidonic acid by cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase, sensitise the ending without activating it, the step blocked by COX inhibitors as analgesics.
