
> Pain afferents synapse in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, whose [[grey matter]] is organised into layers that receive different fibre types. One of these layers, ==lamina II or the substantia gelatinosa==, modulates the nociceptive signal before it is relayed to the brain. Nociceptive input also drives the ==flexion (withdrawal) reflex==, a spinal circuit that pulls the limb away from a painful stimulus.

### 1. Where pain afferents terminate

Rexed's laminae are the ten layers into which the grey matter of the spinal cord is divided on the basis of its cell architecture; the dorsal horn comprises laminae I to VI. The nociceptive afferents do not synapse at random here: each fibre class terminates in its own characteristic laminae, where it contacts the second order neurons of the pain pathway.

### 2. Lamina II: the substantia gelatinosa

==Lamina II corresponds to the substantia gelatinosa==, a layer composed almost entirely of [[interneurons]] that is ==important in the modulation of pain==. The second order neurons do not sit here: they are the projection neurons of laminae I and V, whose axons cross the midline and ascend in the spinothalamic tracts. C fibres terminating in lamina II therefore synapse onto interneurons, which pass the signal, now modulated, on to the projection neurons, while Aδ fibres ending in laminae I and V can contact the projection neurons directly. At these synapses the afferent terminals release excitatory neurotransmitters, principally ==glutamate and substance P==, which generate [[Postsynaptic Potentials|excitatory postsynaptic potentials]] (EPSPs) in the postsynaptic membrane; when these summate to threshold they trigger [[The Action Potential|action potentials]] in the second order neuron, and the signal is carried onward. Lamina II is thus not a relay that simply amplifies the signal on its way up: its interneurons regulate how much of the incoming nociceptive signal is passed on to the projection neurons, and this modulation is not constant but conditional, because the output depends on what other inputs are active at the same time, so the same nociceptive stimulus can be transmitted with different strength under different circumstances.

### 3. The flexion (withdrawal) reflex

The flexion, or withdrawal, reflex produces ==withdrawal from a painful stimulus==. ==Aδ activation leads to activation of excitatory and inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord==: the excitatory interneurons drive the motor neurons supplying the agonist muscle, while the inhibitory interneurons silence the antagonist, giving ==contraction of the agonist muscle and withdrawal from the stimulus==. The reflex is organised at spinal level, which means that the whole circuit of afferent, interneurons and motor neurons, is completed within the cord, so the withdrawal does not depend on the brain or on consciously feeling the pain; the ascending signal still travels to the brain in parallel, and the pain is consciously perceived shortly afterwards.

### Summary

> In the dorsal horn, whose grey matter is divided into the Rexed laminae (I to VI dorsally), pain afferents terminate in a fibre specific pattern: Aδ fibres in laminae I and V and C fibres in laminae I and II. Lamina II is the substantia gelatinosa, where afferent terminals release glutamate and substance P to generate EPSPs and action potentials in the second order neurons, and whose interneurons modulate, rather than simply amplify, how much of the signal is relayed onward, depending on concurrent activity. The Aδ input drives the flexion (withdrawal) reflex: excitatory and inhibitory interneurons contract the agonist and silence the antagonist, a circuit completed entirely within the cord, independent of the brain and of conscious perception.
