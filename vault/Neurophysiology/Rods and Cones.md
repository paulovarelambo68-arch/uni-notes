
>==Rods and cones are the two types of photoreceptor== in the retina, and they divide the work of vision between them. Rods serve **night vision**, trading acuity for extreme sensitivity, while cones serve **day vision**, trading sensitivity for acuity, colour and temporal resolution.

### Rods

Rods are responsible for ==night vision==. They are ==very sensitive to light==, with a **low threshold** and a **high level of [[Photopigments and Colour Vision|photopigment]]**, so they respond even in dim conditions. The price of this sensitivity is ==low acuity==, and it comes from two features. First, there is **much amplification of the signal**, so even a tiny response is boosted. Second, there is ==convergence and summation of the signal at [[bipolar cells]]==: many rods synapse onto the **same bipolar cell** (this is the convergence), and that bipolar cell **adds together the graded potentials** arriving from all of them (this is the [[Postsynaptic Potentials|summation]]). Weak signals that would go unnoticed in a single rod can therefore combine into a signal strong enough to matter, but once merged they can no longer be traced back to any individual rod, so fine spatial detail is lost. Rods also ==adapt slowly==, which is why vision takes time to adjust when light levels change.

### Cones

Cones are responsible for ==day vision==. They are ==less sensitive== than rods, with a **high threshold**, so they only operate when light is abundant. In return they give ==high acuity==, because there is **less signal amplification** and **little convergence** in their circuitry, and they are ==concentrated in the fovea==, the region of sharpest vision. Cones come in ==three types, red, green and blue==, which together support colour vision, and they have ==high temporal resolution==, resolving rapid changes in the stimulus that rods would blur together.

### Distribution and wiring to the ganglion cells

Rods and cones sit at the **very back of the retina**, so light must pass through the other retinal layers before reaching them. **Rods outnumber cones** across the retina as a whole, while cones cluster mainly in the **fovea**. On the right, only two **cones** connect through their own bipolar cells to a separate ganglion cell: ==few cones feed each ganglion cell==, so each cone keeps a nearly private line and precision stays high. ### Graded response to light

Light ==hyperpolarises both rods and cones==, and it does so as a **graded response** rather than an action potential. This graded design works because the cell is small: the signal only has to spread the short distance from the outer segment to the receptor terminal, so no action potential is needed to carry it, and the size of the potential simply regulates how much transmitter the terminal releases. The bipolar cell that receives this signal also works with graded potentials; the ==first action potentials of the visual pathway are generated by the ganglion cells==, which convert the graded signal into a firing pattern able to travel the length of the optic nerve. The mechanism behind the hyperpolarisation itself is covered in [[The Dark Current and Phototransduction]].

The figure shows an intracellular recording from a single photoreceptor. Although only one flash symbol is drawn, it is just a time marker: the experiment was repeated many times with a flash of a different intensity each time, always delivered at 0 ms, and the traces from all those trials are overlaid on the same axes. In the dark the cell rests at about −40 mV, and every flash produces a downward dip, a hyperpolarisation. The **least intense flash** gives a shallow dip of a few millivolts that recovers quickly. As intensity rises, the dips grow deeper and slower to recover, until the **most intense flash** drives the potential down to about −65 mV, where the response ==saturates==: this is the most negative value the cell can reach, so beyond that point extra light produces no extra hyperpolarisation. The conclusion is that the response is ==proportional to stimulus intensity==: because both the depth and the duration of the dip grow with the flash, the graded potential encodes how bright the light was.

### Anatomy of a rod and a cone

Each rod and each cone is a **complete neuron** in its own right, so every receptor has its own cell body and its own synaptic terminal rather than being a part of a larger cell. Both share the same plan: an ==**outer segment**== at the back of the retina, packed with membranous folds that carry the [[Photopigments and Colour Vision|photopigment]] and receive the incoming light, an ==**inner segment**== that holds the nucleus and is crowded with **mitochondria** to meet the high energy demand, a narrow ==**ciliary bridge**== joining the two segments, and a ==**receptor terminal**== at the front that synapses onto a **bipolar cell**. The rod has a long, cylindrical outer segment, while the cone's outer segment is shorter and tapers, giving each cell its name.

### Summary

> Rods and cones are complete retinal neurons built on the same plan: an outer segment packed with photopigment, long and cylindrical in rods but short and tapering in cones, an inner segment crowded with mitochondria, a ciliary bridge and a receptor terminal synapsing onto bipolar cells. Rods are sensitive, slow and convergent, built for night vision at the cost of acuity, while cones are less sensitive but fast and precise, clustered in the fovea and divided into red, green and blue types for day and colour vision. Both respond to light with graded hyperpolarisation whose depth and duration encode intensity, and this signal travels as graded potentials until the ganglion cells convert it into the first action potentials of the visual pathway.
