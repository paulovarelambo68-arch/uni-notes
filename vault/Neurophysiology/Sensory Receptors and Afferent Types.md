
>Sensory receptors are the structures that convert stimuli from the outside world (or from the body itself) into electrical signals the nervous system can read. They range from ==simple free nerve endings to highly complex receptor cells of the special senses==, and they are distinguished from one another by ==four features: the form of the peripheral terminal, the sensitivity to particular stimuli (modality specificity, including threshold), the diameter of the axon and the presence of a myelin sheath==.

### 1. The four features that distinguish receptors

**Form of the peripheral terminal.** The terminal can be a bare free ending, an ending wrapped in a connective tissue capsule, or a separate receptor cell altogether. This structural plan determines how the stimulus reaches the neuronal membrane.

**Modality specificity and threshold.** Each receptor is sensitive to a particular kind of stimulus, meaning it responds preferentially (not always) to one modality (touch, pain, temperature, proprioception). Sensitivity is expressed as a ==threshold: the minimum stimulus intensity needed to trigger a response==. A receptor with a low threshold for its modality fires easily; other stimuli simply fail to activate it.

**Diameter of the axon and presence of a myelin sheath.** These two decide conduction velocity, exactly as in [[Classification of Nerve Fibres]]: larger diameter and myelin mean faster signalling, so the receptor type already tells you how quickly its information reaches the central nervous system.

### 2. Forms of the peripheral terminal 

**Simple receptors: free nerve endings.** The receptor is the ending of the sensory neuron itself, with no accessory structure. The axon is ==unmyelinated and conducts at 0.5 to 2 m/s==. Pain, temperature and itch are carried this way.

**Complex neural receptors: encapsulated endings.** The nerve ending is enclosed in a connective tissue capsule that shapes how the stimulus deforms the membrane (the [[Pacinian corpuscle]] is the textbook example). The axon is ==myelinated and conducts at 12 to 30 m/s==, much faster than a bare ending.

**Special sense receptors: receptor cells.** Here the receptor is a ==separate cell, not a neuron ending==. The stimulus acts on the receptor cell, which forms a synapse onto a sensory afferent and ==releases neurotransmitter onto it, initiating an action potential== in the afferent.

The graded potential produced by the stimulus has a different name in each plan. A stimulus acting directly on the afferent ending (free or encapsulated) produces a ==generator potential== in that ending; a stimulus acting on a separate receptor cell (special senses) produces a ==receptor potential== in the cell, which then drives transmitter release onto the afferent. Same logic as before: direct in the first two plans, through a synapse in the third.

### 3. Types of sensory afferents

Each sensory function is carried by a characteristic pairing of receptor and axon type, and ==the axon type predicts diameter and conduction velocity==:

- **Proprioception** uses the [[muscle spindle]], carried by ==Ia and II afferents, 13 to 20 µm, conducting at 80 to 120 m/s==. The fastest afferents, because posture and movement need rapid feedback.

- **Touch** uses the encapsulated skin mechanoreceptors (Merkel, Meissner, Pacinian and Ruffini cells), carried by ==Aβ afferents, 6 to 12 µm, 35 to 75 m/s==.

- **Pain and temperature** use free nerve endings on ==Aδ afferents, thinly myelinated, 1 to 5 µm, 5 to 30 m/s==.

- **Pain, temperature and itch** also use free nerve endings, but on ==C fibres, unmyelinated, 0.2 to 1.5 µm, 0.5 to 2 m/s==, the slowest of all.

| Sensory function | Receptor type | Afferent axon | Diameter | Velocity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Proprioception | Muscle spindle | Ia, II | 13 to 20 µm | 80 to 120 m/s |
| Touch | Merkel, Meissner, Pacinian, Ruffini | Aβ | 6 to 12 µm | 35 to 75 m/s |
| Pain, temperature | Free nerve endings | Aδ | 1 to 5 µm | 5 to 30 m/s |
| Pain, temperature, itch | Free nerve endings | C | 0.2 to 1.5 µm | 0.5 to 2 m/s |

### Summary

>Sensory receptors range from simple free nerve endings to specialised receptor cells and are distinguished by four features: form of the peripheral terminal, modality specificity with threshold, axon diameter and myelination. Structurally there are three plans: bare endings on unmyelinated axons (0.5 to 2 m/s), encapsulated endings on myelinated axons (12 to 30 m/s), and separate receptor cells that release neurotransmitter onto a sensory afferent. Stimulation produces a [[graded potential]], called a generator potential when it arises in the afferent ending itself and a receptor potential when it arises in a separate receptor cell. Functionally, proprioception runs on fast Ia and II afferents (80 to 120 m/s), touch on Aβ (35 to 75 m/s), pain and temperature on Aδ (5 to 30 m/s), and pain, temperature and itch on slow unmyelinated C fibres (0.5 to 2 m/s): the larger and more myelinated the axon, the faster the sense reaches the brain.
