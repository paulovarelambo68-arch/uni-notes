
>It's the process by which an action potential in the presynaptic neuron causes the release of neurotransmitter, which crosses the synaptic cleft and acts on receptors of the postsynaptic cell. It converts an electrical signal into a chemical signal and back into an electrical (or metabolic) response. The whole process from arrival of the action potential to transmitter release takes less than a millisecond.

### 2. The synapse's structures 

- **==Presynaptic neuron and axon terminal (synaptic bouton):==** the swollen ending of the axon that contains the machinery for transmitter release.

- **==Synaptic vesicles (secretory granules):==** membrane bound organelles filled with neurotransmitter, clustered in the terminal.

- **==Presynaptic membrane (active zone):==** the specialised region of the terminal membrane where vesicles dock and fuse.

- **==Synaptic cleft:==** the narrow gap (about 20 to 40 nm) separating pre and postsynaptic membranes; transmitter diffuses across it.

- **==Postsynaptic membrane and receptors:==** the membrane of the target cell, enriched with receptors that bind the transmitter and generate the postsynaptic response.

- **==Postsynaptic neuron:==** the cell receiving the signal.

1. **Action potential arrival.** The action potential propagates along the axon and invades the axon terminal, depolarising its membrane.

2. **Opening of voltage gated Ca²⁺ channels.** Depolarisation activates voltage gated calcium channels concentrated at the active zone. Because the electrochemical gradient strongly favours Ca²⁺ entry (very low intracellular Ca²⁺ at rest), Ca²⁺ rushes into the terminal. Local Ca²⁺ concentration near the channel mouths rises sharply.

3. **Activation of Ca²⁺/calmodulin dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII).** Entering Ca²⁺ binds the protein calmodulin. The Ca²⁺ calmodulin complex activates CaMKII, a kinase abundant in the terminal.

4. **Mobilisation of vesicles from the reserve pool.** In the reserve pool, vesicles are tethered to the cytoskeleton by the protein **synapsin**. CaMKII [[phosphorylates]] synapsin, which releases its grip on the vesicles, freeing them to move toward the membrane. This is the mobilisation step.

5. **Docking.** Mobilised vesicles are brought to the release sites on the presynaptic membrane and attached there by interaction between vesicle proteins (synaptobrevin, also called VAMP) and membrane proteins (syntaxin and SNAP 25). Together these form the **SNARE complex**.

6. **Priming.** The SNARE complex partially assembles and tightens, bringing the vesicle membrane into very close apposition with the plasma membrane. The vesicle is now release ready (part of the readily releasable pool).

7. **Fusion triggered by Ca²⁺.** Ca²⁺ binds **synaptotagmin**, the vesicular calcium sensor. This triggers complete SNARE zippering, the membranes merge, and a fusion pore opens.

![[Screenshot 2026-09-16 at 19.44.08.png]]

8. **Exocytosis.** The vesicle contents are discharged into the synaptic cleft by exocytosis (visible on electron microscopy as the omega shaped profile of a fusing vesicle). Transmitter then diffuses across the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors.

**One line summary to memorise:** AP → terminal depolarisation → voltage gated Ca²⁺ channels open → Ca²⁺ influx → Ca²⁺ calmodulin activates CaMKII → synapsin phosphorylation → vesicle mobilisation → docking → priming → Ca²⁺ synaptotagmin triggers fusion → exocytosis.

### 4.1 Vesicle recycling: clathrin mediated Endocytosis

![[Screenshot 2026-09-14 at 17.44.54.png]]

Vesicle membrane must be recovered and reused; this is the vesicle cycle:

1. **Coating.** After fusion, patches of membrane are coated on their cytoplasmic side by the protein **clathrin**, forming a clathrin coated pit.
 
2. **Budding.** The coated membrane [[invaginates]] and pinches off from the surface as a clathrin coated vesicle (the protein dynamin helps sever the neck).

3. **Uncoating.** The clathrin coat is removed, returning a naked vesicle.

4. **Endosome step.** Uncoated vesicles may pass through an [[endosome]] intermediate, which sorts and regenerates vesicles by [[budding]].

5. **Transmitter loading.** The regenerated vesicle is refilled with neurotransmitter by specific vesicular transporter proteins in its membrane.

6. **Return to the reserve pool.** Refilled vesicles rejoin the reserve pool, tethered again by synapsin to the cytoskeleton, ready for the next cycle of mobilisation, docking, priming and fusion.

**Key point:** recycling is continuous and local within the terminal; it allows sustained transmission without new vesicles being made in the cell body.

### 4.2 Vesicle recycling: why two budding steps?

The vesicle passes through two stations, and each budding has a different job.

1. **First budding (retrieval):** after exocytosis, the used vesicle membrane is a flat patch in the presynaptic membrane. Clathrin coats it, curves it into a pit, and it pinches off back into the terminal. This step simply recovers the used membrane. The clathrin coat is then removed (uncoating).

2. **Second budding (regeneration):** the recovered vesicle is the wrong shape, may carry leftover membrane proteins, and is empty. It fuses with the endosome, which acts as a sorting and repackaging station. A new vesicle of the correct size and protein composition buds off the endosome.

3. Only then is the vesicle refilled with neurotransmitter (transmitter loading) and returned to the reserve pool.

_(Caveat: some vesicles skip the endosome and are refilled directly after uncoating. The slides show the endosome route)_

### 5. Synaptic arrangement: axons to dendrites

![[Screenshot 2026-09-17 at 14.17.28 4.png]]

- **Most synaptic transmission in the nervous system is axodendritic:** the axon terminal of one neuron synapses onto the dendrite (often onto small protrusions called dendritic spines) of the next neuron.
- A single dendrite receives many synaptic contacts from many different axons (convergence), which is the anatomical basis for integration of inputs by the postsynaptic cell.
- Less common arrangements exist for comparison: **axosomatic** (axon onto cell body) and **axoaxonic** (axon onto another axon terminal, the basis of presynaptic inhibition).
