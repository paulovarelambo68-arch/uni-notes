
>A rapid, transient reversal of the membrane potential in an excitable cell (neuron or muscle fibre): the interior flips from about −70 mV (resting membrane potential, RMP) to about +30 mV and back, in roughly 1 ms. It is the unit of long distance electrical signalling along axons. It requires voltage gated ion channels, which are proteins built from subunits; channels can also be ligand gated or mechanosensitive.

### **Threshold**

The critical depolarisation needed to fire an AP, about +15 mV above RMP (from −70 mV to ≈ −55 mV). Depolarisations that stay below threshold produce only small graded potentials that fade out; crossing threshold opens voltage gated Na⁺ channels in a regenerative, self reinforcing way and the full AP follows.

Only depolarising stimuli can fire an AP; hyperpolarising currents never do, however strong. Subthreshold responses are graded: the closer the stimulus gets to threshold (e.g. 11% → 89% of threshold), the larger the local potential, but all fade out. At 100% of threshold the response jumps abruptly to a full AP, which is the experimental proof of the all or nothing law.

### **All or nothing**

If threshold is reached, the AP fires always at the same full amplitude (peak ≈ +30 mV); if threshold is not reached, nothing happens. Stimulus strength does not change AP size; stronger stimuli are coded as higher firing frequency, not bigger APs.

### **Self propagating**

The AP is regenerated at full size at every point along the axon: local current from the active region depolarises the neighbouring membrane to threshold, opening its Na⁺ channels. The signal therefore travels [[without decaying]], and it travels in one direction only because the region just behind is [[refractory]].

### **The three phases (the curve)**

1. **Depolarisation (rising phase):** threshold reached → activation gates of Na⁺ channels open → Na⁺ rushes in ([[conductance]] rises up to ×5000) → positive feedback drives the membrane toward +30 mV, close to the Na⁺ equilibrium potential.

2. **Repolarisation (falling phase):** Na⁺ inactivation gates close and voltage gated K⁺ channels (slower to open) finally open → K⁺ leaves the cell → the interior becomes negative again.

3. **Hyperpolarisation (undershoot):** K⁺ channels are slow to close, so K⁺ keeps leaving and the membrane dips below RMP, toward the K⁺ equilibrium potential (≈ −90 mV), before returning to rest. This phase matches the relative refractory period.

### **Voltage gated Na⁺ channel gates**

Each channel has two gates. The **activation gate** opens fast when the membrane depolarises to threshold. The **inactivation gate** closes shortly afterwards, automatically, even while depolarisation persists. States: closed but available at rest (activation shut, inactivation open) → open at threshold (both open, Na⁺ influx, conductance ×5000) → inactivated at the peak (inactivation shut, no Na⁺ flow) → back to closed and available once repolarisation resets both gates. The inactivated state is the molecular basis of the absolute refractory period.

### **Voltage gated K⁺ channels and repolarisation**

One slow activation gate, opened by the same depolarisation but with a delay. Their late opening, combined with Na⁺ channel inactivation, ends the upstroke: outward K⁺ current repolarises the membrane, and because they close slowly the membrane transiently hyperpolarises. On the current trace: the Na⁺ current is brief and inward (fast activation, fast inactivation), while the K⁺ current is delayed, outward and longer lasting, matching repolarisation and the undershoot.

Channel state sequences to memorise: Na⁺ channel goes closed → open → inactivating → inactivated → closed; the K⁺ channel goes closed → closed → open → open → closed. The key point is timing: the K⁺ channel opens with a clear delay relative to the Na⁺ channel, and that delay is what allows depolarisation to develop before repolarisation begins.

### **Refractory periods**

- **Absolute:** from the upstroke through most of repolarisation. No stimulus, however strong, can trigger a second AP, because Na⁺ inactivation gates are shut and cannot reopen until the membrane repolarises. Function: enforces one way propagation and caps maximum firing rate.

- **Relative:** during hyperpolarisation. A second AP is possible but only with a stronger than normal stimulus, because the membrane sits further from threshold and K⁺ is still leaving; some Na⁺ channels have recovered. Duration is more variable than the absolute period.

Graphically, threshold is not fixed: during the spike it rises off scale, so no stimulus can reach it (absolute refractory period), and during hyperpolarisation it gradually settles back toward normal, so only a stronger than normal stimulus can reach it (relative refractory period). The refractory periods can therefore be read directly from the threshold curve, not just from the membrane potential curve.

### **Key numbers to memorise**

RMP −70 mV, threshold −55 mV (+15 mV above RMP),  peak +30 mV, Na⁺ conductance up to ×5000, duration ≈ 1 ms.
