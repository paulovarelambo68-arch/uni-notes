
>The **eye** is the organ of vision: it gathers light and focuses it onto the ==retina==, the neural layer lining the back of the globe where the image is formed and processing begins. The image projected onto the retina is **inverted** and covers both a ==binocular zone== and a ==monocular zone==.

### Anatomy of the Eye

The figure shows the eye in sagittal section and in external view.

Light enters through the ==cornea==, the transparent front surface, then crosses the **anterior chamber** and **posterior chamber**, which are filled with ==aqueous humour==, a clear watery fluid that nourishes the cornea and lens and maintains the pressure of the globe, before passing the **pupil** and the ==lens==, which is held in place by the **suspensory ligament** attached to the ==ciliary body==. The main cavity is filled by the **vitreous body**, a transparent gel that lets light pass through to the back of the eye.

The wall of the globe has three layers: the tough outer ==sclera==, the middle ==choroid==, and the inner ==retina==, with the **fovea** sitting at the back pole. The **optic nerve** exits at the ==blind spot==, together with the retinal artery, wrapped in a nerve sheath.

The external view figure shows the visible front of the eye: the pupil and **iris** seen through the cornea, the white sclera, the eyelids and eyelashes framing the **palpebral fissure**, with the medial and lateral commissures at each corner.

The **iris** contracts and dilates to control how much light enters, and ==dilation lets in more light==; iris colour has no functional purpose. The **choroid** is lightly pigmented with ==melanin== and absorbs [[stray light]], which matters in albino people who lack this pigment. 

A rise in pressure of the aqueous humour at the front of the eye is ==glaucoma==: when the fluid is produced faster than it drains, the pressure inside the globe rises, and if sustained it can damage the **optic nerve** and gradually erode vision.

### Accommodation

**Accommodation** is the process by which the ==lens changes shape== to focus light sharply on the retina. The diagram contrasts the two states side by side: in the **unaccommodated** state the ==ciliary muscle== is relaxed, the ==zonule fibres== are [[pulled taut]] and the lens is held thin and flattened, which suits distant vision. In the **accommodated** state the ciliary muscle contracts, the zonule fibres [[slacken]], and the elastic lens rounds up into a thicker, more curved shape, which increases its ==refractive power== for near vision. 

**Accommodation** is the process by which the ==lens changes shape== to focus light sharply on the retina, and the diagram contrasts the two states side by side. In the **unaccommodated** state the ==ciliary muscle== is relaxed, the ==zonule fibres== are pulled taut and the lens is held thin and flattened, which suits distant vision. In the **accommodated** state the ciliary muscle contracts, the zonule fibres slacken, and the elastic lens rounds up into a thicker, more curved shape, which increases its ==refractive power== for near vision.

The key to understand this is that the ==ciliary muscle is a ring==: relaxed, the ring is wide and pulls the zonule fibres tight, stretching the lens flat; contracted, the ring narrows and the fibres go slack, so the elastic lens springs back to its natural rounded shape.

### Focusing Light and Refractive Errors

Light is focused by the **cornea** and the **lens** onto the retina (back of the eyeball), and it is directed specifically to arrive at the ==fovea==. The cornea and lens bend incoming rays to a focal point, but whether that point lands exactly on the retina depends on how far back the retina sits, that is, on the length of the eyeball. When the two are matched, we call it ==emmetropia==, normal sight or ==20/20== vision because a healthy eye reads on the standard chart at 20 feet what should be readable at 20 feet. The figure compares this ideal with the two refractive errors using ray diagrams.

In ==myopia==, or short sightedness, the eyeball is longer than normal, so the rays come together before they reach the retina and then spread out again, blurring distant objects; the corrective lens therefore ==spreads the light== before it enters, pushing the focal point back onto the retina. In ==hypermetropia==, or long sightedness, the eyeball is shorter than normal, so the rays have not finished converging by the time they reach the retina; a convex lens ==converges the light== earlier to bring the focal point forward; **Presbyopia** is the age related form of this loss of focusing ability.

### The Retinal Image

The cornea and lens each bend incoming light rays inwards, towards a focal point, which is why they are described as ==converging lenses==, the same principle as a magnifying glass. A consequence of bending light this way is that the ==retinal image== comes out ==inverted==, flipped 180 degrees both vertically and horizontally, so objects above you project onto the lower retina and objects to your left project onto the right. Light therefore arrives from the opposite side to what you perceive, and the brain has to learn to reinterpret the projection, something babies take about **6 weeks** to master, helped by watching where their own hands are.

The visual field is also divided into zones. The central ==binocular zone== is covered by both eyes at once, while the **monocular zones** at the far edges are seen by one eye only, which is why the full extent of your peripheral vision to either side can only be appreciated with the other eye closed.

### The Fundus

The fundus photograph shows the back of the eye as seen in a normal ==[[fundoscopic examination]]==. The pale **optic disc** is where the optic nerve leaves the eye, and the small paler depression at its centre is the ==optic cup==, the point through which the nerve fibres and vessels actually pass. From the disc, retinal **arteries** and **veins** radiate outwards across the surface. Lateral to the disc lies the darker ==macula==, the region responsible for detailed central vision, and at its centre sits the **fovea**, a tiny pit where photoreceptors are densest and acuity is sharpest. **Macular degeneration** is the clinical correlate of damage to this region. Because the optic disc carries no photoreceptors it forms the ==blind spot==, where the brain guesses and fills in the missing input, much like a car sitting in your blind spot.

### Summary

>Vision begins as an optical problem: the cornea and lens must project a ==focused image== onto the fovea, and accommodation adjusts the lens to keep it sharp at different distances. What the retina receives is an **inverted projection** with a built in blind spot, and perception is the brain's reconstruction of that image.
