
> ==Frequency and loudness, the two perceptual dimensions of sound, are coded by separate strategies in the cochlea==. ==Frequency is coded by the **place principle**==: each frequency moves one particular stretch of the **basilar membrane** most strongly, so the position of the responding hair cells identifies the pitch. ==Loudness is coded by how strongly and how widely the membrane responds==, and by how hard the cochlea's own amplifier is driven.

### The hearing range and its loss with age

The ==young ear hears from 20 to 20,000 Hz==, a range that narrows with age to ==50 to 5,000 Hz==. The frequencies that actually matter for daily life are only ==a few hundred to a few thousand Hz==, because this middle band carries speech, so the older range still covers conversation and the narrowing is far from incapacitating. The loss itself reflects the progressive death of hair cells, whose mechanism is covered in [[Deafness and Hearing Tests]].

### The basis of the place principle

The place principle rests on one mechanical fact: the basilar membrane ==is not of equal thickness along its length==. ==Thickness and rigidity decrease from the base to the **helicotrema**==, and ==as thickness decreases, stiffness decreases too==. The paired diagram draws this gradient, with the membrane ==narrow and tight at the base== and ==wide and loose at the apex==. A structure of this kind is frequency selective by its very construction, because stiff tissue can follow rapid vibration while loose tissue can follow only slow vibration, so ==high frequency sounds produce maximal motion at the base== and ==low frequency sounds at the [[helicotrema]]==.

### The travelling wave

When the stapes sets the cochlear fluid moving, the basilar membrane does not vibrate all at once: the disturbance runs along it ==from base to apex as a **travelling wave**==. How far the wave gets before it dies is decided by the stiffness gradient. The wave grows as it leaves the base, ==reaches its maximum where the membrane's stiffness best matches the frequency==, and collapses just beyond, where the membrane can no longer follow that frequency at all. A high frequency wave meets membrane stiff enough to keep up with it right at the base, so it peaks there almost at once; a low frequency wave passes the stiff base without moving it much and travels on, peaking only where the membrane has grown loose enough to follow it, close to the helicotrema. Between the extremes, every intermediate frequency finds its best matched region at its own point, and once reached, ==the peak stays fixed at that place for as long as the tone sounds==. The conclusion is the place principle itself: ==each frequency of stimulation excites maximal motion at a specific position, its place, along the membrane==, the hair cells sitting at that place are driven hardest, and the brain reads pitch from which place is signalling most strongly.

### Loudness coding

Loudness is sensed through three mechanisms that all grow with the strength of the vibration. First, the ==**amplitude of response**==: a louder sound displaces the basilar membrane further, so each responding hair cell has its stereocilia bent harder, ==more K⁺ channels open and more K⁺ enters the cell==, giving a larger depolarisation; because the receptor potential is graded, its size tracks stimulus strength, and the cell releases more transmitter onto its afferent fibre, which fires faster (the transduction steps themselves are in [[Hair Cells and Cochlear Transduction]]). Second, ==**spatial summation**==: a stronger vibration also drives a ==wider stretch of membrane== past threshold, so ==more hair cells respond==, and since each hair cell passes its signal to its own afferent fibres, ==recruiting more hair cells directly recruits more afferent fibres==, whose summed activity the brain reads as a louder sound. Third, ==activation of the **outer hair cells**==: these cells amplify the membrane's motion continuously, and the harder a sound drives them the larger the motion they feed back, so their activation compounds the first two mechanisms at once, enlarging each hair cell's response and widening the band of cells recruited. Their boost is proportionally greatest for quiet sounds, whose vibration it raises up to a hundred fold, which is what brings faint sounds above threshold at all.

### Summary

> The place principle rests on the membrane's gradient: thickness, rigidity and stiffness fall from base to helicotrema, so each frequency's travelling wave peaks at its own place, high frequencies at the base and low frequencies at the helicotrema, and the hair cells at the peak report the pitch. Onto this map the young ear lays 20 to 20,000 Hz, narrowing with age to 50 to 5,000 Hz. Loudness is coded separately, growing with the amplitude of each hair cell's response, with the number of hair cells and afferent fibres recruited, and with the activation of the amplifying outer hair cells.
