
> The visual pathway is the route that ganglion cell axons follow from the retina into the brain, and its targets are the structures where those axons terminate. Its organisation follows a single rule, ==each side of the brain is connected to the opposite half of the visual field==, and its output splits in two: a large cortical route that supports ==the conscious perception of vision== and a small set of subcortical stations, structures below the cortex, that drive automatic functions such as reflexes and daily rhythms.

### The optic nerve and the optic chiasm

Ganglion cell axons leave the eye as the **optic nerve** and meet at the **optic chiasm**, where a partial crossing takes place: ==fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross to the other side of the brain==, while fibres from the temporal half stay on their own side. Behind the chiasm the fibres continue as the **optic tract**, and the sorting has a precise consequence: ==axons from the left half of each retina project in the left optic tract, carrying the representation of the right visual hemifield==, and the right optic tract symmetrically carries the left hemifield. Each tract therefore carries input from both eyes about the same hemifield, so the two retinal views of the same part of the world are brought together in one hemisphere, where they can be compared, and ==this arrangement allows depth perception==.

### Cortical and subcortical targets

The great majority of the output serves perception: ==90% of ganglion fibres== synapse in the **lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus**, which relays the signal to the **visual cortex**, where ==perception of vision== takes place.

The remaining ==10% of fibres terminate on [[subcortical targets]]==, each with a distinct function:

- **Pretectum**: ==blinking response==
- **Superior colliculus**: ==head and eye movements==
- **Suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus**: ==circadian rhythms==

### The pathway in the brain

The ventral view of the brain seen in the figure assembles the whole route. The optic nerves run back from the eyes to the chiasm, where the nasal fibres cross, and the optic tracts sweep around the midbrain to reach the lateral geniculate nucleus on each side. From there the **optic radiation** fans out broadly through the white matter and ends in the **striate cortex** at the occipital pole, the first cortical station of vision. Drawn in red, the subcortical branches leave this main route: small projections reach the hypothalamus for the regulation of circadian rhythms, the pretectum (captioned in the figure as the reflex control of pupil and lens, the same reflex circuitry behind the blinking response listed above) and the superior colliculus, which orients the movements of head and eyes.

### Visual field defects after lesions

The defects figure shows the same ventral view with three lesion sites marked, and beside each one a pair of circles representing the visual fields of the left and right eye, with the lost region shaded. The logic always follows from the crossing rule: know which fibres run at the lesion site, remember that the nasal retina sees the temporal field and the temporal retina the nasal field, and the defect follows.

1. A lesion of one **optic nerve** destroys every fibre coming from that retina before any crossing has occurred, so ==the loss of vision is limited to the eye of origin==: the whole field of the left eye is shaded while the right eye sees normally. It is a purely monocular blindness.

2. A lesion of the **midline optic chiasm** destroys only the fibres that cross, those coming from the nasal half of each retina, while the uncrossed temporal fibres are spared. Because the nasal retina of each eye receives the temporal half of the visual field, ==both temporal hemifields are lost==, a defect called **bitemporal hemianopia**.

3. A lesion of one **optic tract** removes fibres that have already been sorted by the chiasm: the left tract contains the uncrossed temporal fibres of the left eye together with the crossed nasal fibres of the right eye, and both sets carry the right visual hemifield. Cutting it therefore ==blinds the right half of the visual field in both eyes==, a **right homonymous hemianopia**. The figure shows the pathway continuing beyond the tract, through the lateral geniculate body and the optic radiation, all the way to the visual cortex (areas 17, 18 and 19, that is V1 to V4, plus V5). The same reasoning applies at every one of these stations, because the sorting made at the chiasm, one hemifield per side, is kept unchanged up to the cortex: ==any lesion behind the chiasm produces a contralateral homonymous defect==, meaning the opposite hemifield is lost in both eyes.

### Summary

> The chiasm sorts fibres so that each hemisphere receives the opposite hemifield, 90% of fibres reaching the lateral geniculate nucleus and striate cortex for perception and 10% feeding the pretectum, superior colliculus and suprachiasmatic nucleus. Lesions read like the anatomy: nerve cuts blind one eye, chiasm cuts remove both temporal fields, and retrochiasmatic cuts erase the contralateral hemifield in both eyes.
