
#### Why the peak goes toward E_Na?

**1. It is simply what permeability does:** the membrane potential always moves toward the equilibrium potential of the most permeable ion. When voltage gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ permeability jumps far above K⁺ permeability, so the membrane potential shoots toward E_Na (positive 50 mV). The cell does not choose this value; physics does.

**2. A huge driving force means a fast signal.** At rest (negative 70 mV) the driving force on Na⁺ is about 120 mV, the difference between the membrane potential and E_Na. This makes Na⁺ rush in explosively, so depolarization is extremely fast. A fast upstroke means fast, reliable propagation along the axon.

**3. A large peak guarantees the signal does not die out.** The strong positive voltage (the overshoot) passively spreads to the next patch of membrane and pushes it well above threshold, ensuring the action potential regenerates itself along the whole axon with a large safety margin. A small peak would risk conduction failure.

**4. In practice the peak never fully reaches E_Na** (usually around positive 30 to 40 mV), because Na⁺ channels begin to inactivate and K⁺ channels are already opening. Saying "the peak approaches E_Na" is the exam level explanation.

#### Why the cell does not need to "clean" Na⁺ quickly

This is the elegant part: repolarization does not depend on removing the Na⁺ that entered.

**Channel inactivation stops the signal, not ion removal.** Voltage gated Na⁺ channels inactivate within about a millisecond, slamming shut on their own. The signal ends because the door closes, not because the Na⁺ is swept away.

**Repolarization is done by K⁺, not by pumps.** Voltage gated K⁺ channels open and K⁺ leaves the cell, dragging the voltage back down toward E_K. This is fast and costs no ATP.

**The amount of Na⁺ that enters is trivial.** A single action potential changes intracellular Na⁺ concentration by a tiny fraction. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump, although slow, has more than enough time to restore the gradients between signals. Pumps are for long term maintenance; channels are for speed.

**Moving charge is what matters, not moving mass.** The membrane potential depends only on the thin layer of charge right next to the membrane. Shifting a minuscule number of K⁺ ions out is enough to restore negative 70 mV; the bulk concentrations barely change.

#### Exam ready summary

>The action potential peak approaches E_Na because Na⁺ permeability transiently dominates, and this is advantageous because the large driving force on Na⁺ produces a rapid upstroke and a strong overshoot that guarantees propagation. The signal is terminated not by removing Na⁺ but by fast Na⁺ channel inactivation plus K⁺ efflux, while the slow Na⁺/K⁺ pump only needs to top up the gradients afterwards, since the actual quantity of Na⁺ entering per action potential is negligible.
